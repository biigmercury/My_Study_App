## Matrix Multiplication: The Problem

Given two $n \times n$ matrices $A$ and $B$, compute their product $C = A \times B$.

The entry at row $i$, column $j$ of $C$ is:

$$C[i][j] = \sum_{k=1}^{n} A[i][k] \cdot B[k][j]$$

That is, the dot product of the $i$-th row of $A$ with the $j$-th column of $B$.

**Example ($2 \times 2$):**

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{pmatrix}$$

$$C[1][1] = 1 \times 5 + 2 \times 7 = 19$$
$$C[1][2] = 1 \times 6 + 2 \times 8 = 22$$
$$C[2][1] = 3 \times 5 + 4 \times 7 = 43$$
$$C[2][2] = 3 \times 6 + 4 \times 8 = 50$$

$$C = \begin{pmatrix} 19 & 22 \\ 43 & 50 \end{pmatrix}$$

---

## The Naive Algorithm

```
ALGORITHM NaiveMatMul(A, B, n):
  FOR i FROM 1 TO n:
    FOR j FROM 1 TO n:
      C[i][j] = 0
      FOR k FROM 1 TO n:
        C[i][j] = C[i][j] + A[i][k] * B[k][j]
```

**Complexity:** Three nested loops, each running $n$ times.

$$T(n) = n \times n \times n = O(n^3)$$

For $n = 1000$: about $10^9$ multiplications. For $n = 10{,}000$: about $10^{12}$ — completely impractical.

Can we do better? For decades, people assumed $O(n^3)$ was a fundamental limit. Then Strassen showed it was not.

---

## Step 1: Divide into Sub-matrices

Assume $n$ is a power of 2 (if not, pad with zeros). Partition each matrix into four $(n/2) \times (n/2)$ sub-matrices:

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} \\ A_{21} & A_{22} \end{pmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{pmatrix} B_{11} & B_{12} \\ B_{21} & B_{22} \end{pmatrix}$$

Then:

$$C = A \times B = \begin{pmatrix} C_{11} & C_{12} \\ C_{21} & C_{22} \end{pmatrix}$$

where:

$$C_{11} = A_{11}B_{11} + A_{12}B_{21}$$
$$C_{12} = A_{11}B_{12} + A_{12}B_{22}$$
$$C_{21} = A_{21}B_{11} + A_{22}B_{21}$$
$$C_{22} = A_{21}B_{12} + A_{22}B_{22}$$

---

## The Naive Recursive Approach (8 Multiplications)

Computing $C_{11}, C_{12}, C_{21}, C_{22}$ as above requires **8 recursive matrix multiplications** of size $n/2$, plus 4 additions.

**Recurrence:**

$$T(n) = 8T(n/2) + O(n^2)$$

By Master Theorem: $a=8$, $b=2$, $\log_2 8 = 3$. Since $f(n) = O(n^2) = O(n^{\log_2 8 - 1})$ (Case 1):

$$T(n) = O(n^3)$$

No improvement over the naive algorithm. The recursion did not help unless we reduce the number of sub-multiplications.

---

## Strassen's Algorithm (7 Multiplications)

In 1969, Volker Strassen showed that only **7 recursive multiplications** are needed (instead of 8). The savings come from algebraic re-arrangement.

### The 7 Products

Define 7 matrices (each a $(n/2) \times (n/2)$ product):

$$M_1 = (A_{11} + A_{22})(B_{11} + B_{22})$$
$$M_2 = (A_{21} + A_{22})B_{11}$$
$$M_3 = A_{11}(B_{12} - B_{22})$$
$$M_4 = A_{22}(B_{21} - B_{11})$$
$$M_5 = (A_{11} + A_{12})B_{22}$$
$$M_6 = (A_{21} - A_{11})(B_{11} + B_{12})$$
$$M_7 = (A_{12} - A_{22})(B_{21} + B_{22})$$

### Recovering the Result

$$C_{11} = M_1 + M_4 - M_5 + M_7$$
$$C_{12} = M_3 + M_5$$
$$C_{21} = M_2 + M_4$$
$$C_{22} = M_1 - M_2 + M_3 + M_6$$

**Verification ($2 \times 2$ example):**

Using $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, $B = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{pmatrix}$ (scalars now):

- $M_1 = (1+4)(5+8) = 5 \times 13 = 65$
- $M_2 = (3+4) \times 5 = 35$
- $M_3 = 1 \times (6-8) = -2$
- $M_4 = 4 \times (7-5) = 8$
- $M_5 = (1+2) \times 8 = 24$
- $M_6 = (3-1)(5+6) = 2 \times 11 = 22$
- $M_7 = (2-4)(7+8) = -2 \times 15 = -30$

Then:
- $C_{11} = 65 + 8 - 24 + (-30) = 19$ ✓
- $C_{12} = -2 + 24 = 22$ ✓
- $C_{21} = 35 + 8 = 43$ ✓
- $C_{22} = 65 - 35 + (-2) + 22 = 50$ ✓

---

## Strassen's Recurrence and Complexity

**Recurrence:**

$$T(n) = 7T(n/2) + O(n^2)$$

- 7 recursive multiplications (each size $n/2$)
- 18 matrix additions/subtractions for computing $M_1 \ldots M_7$ and $C_{11} \ldots C_{22}$ — each $O(n^2)$

**Master Theorem:** $a=7$, $b=2$, $f(n) = O(n^2)$.

$$n^{\log_b a} = n^{\log_2 7} \approx n^{2.807}$$

Since $f(n) = O(n^2) = O(n^{2.807 - 0.807})$ — so $f$ grows slower than $n^{\log_b a}$, this is Case 1:

$$T(n) = \Theta(n^{\log_2 7}) \approx O(n^{2.807})$$

Often stated as $O(n^{2.81})$.

---

## The Comparison

| Algorithm | Recurrence | Complexity |
|---|---|---|
| Naive | — | $O(n^3)$ |
| Naive recursive (8 mults) | $T(n) = 8T(n/2) + O(n^2)$ | $O(n^3)$ |
| Strassen (7 mults) | $T(n) = 7T(n/2) + O(n^2)$ | $O(n^{2.81})$ |

**How much does this help?**

For $n = 1000$:
- $n^3 = 10^9$
- $n^{2.81} \approx 10^{8.43} \approx 2.7 \times 10^8$

About 4× faster. For $n = 10{,}000$:
- $n^3 = 10^{12}$
- $n^{2.81} \approx 10^{11.24} \approx 1.7 \times 10^{11}$

About 6× faster. The advantage grows with $n$.

---

## Why Saving One Multiplication Matters

The exponent in Strassen's complexity is $\log_2 7$. This comes from 7 sub-problems of half the size. Compare:

| Sub-problems | Exponent | Complexity |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | $\log_2 8 = 3$ | $O(n^3)$ |
| 7 | $\log_2 7 \approx 2.807$ | $O(n^{2.807})$ |
| 6 | $\log_2 6 \approx 2.585$ | $O(n^{2.585})$ |
| 4 | $\log_2 4 = 2$ | $O(n^2)$ |

Each saved multiplication shaves off a fraction of the exponent. The divide-and-conquer framework turns arithmetic savings at the base level into asymptotic improvements.

---

## Current State of Research

Strassen's $O(n^{2.81})$ was not the final word. Subsequent algorithms improved the exponent:

| Year | Algorithm | Complexity |
|---|---|---|
| 1969 | Strassen | $O(n^{2.807})$ |
| 1990 | Coppersmith-Winograd | $O(n^{2.376})$ |
| 2012–2024 | Various improvements | Down to $O(n^{2.371})$ |

The theoretical lower bound is not known. Some believe $O(n^2)$ might be achievable. This is an open problem.

---

## Key Takeaway

> Strassen's algorithm shows that the naive $O(n^3)$ matrix multiplication is not optimal. By algebraically reorganising to need only 7 (not 8) recursive sub-multiplications, the complexity drops to $O(n^{2.81})$ — a meaningful improvement for large matrices. The general lesson: in divide-and-conquer, saving even one recursive call can change the exponent, because the exponent is $\log_b a$ where $a$ = number of sub-problems.

## Exam Tips

**What lecturers test:**
- Write the Strassen recurrence $T(n) = 7T(n/2) + O(n^2)$ and apply Master Theorem
- Explain why the naive recursive approach (8 multiplications) gives $O(n^3)$, not an improvement
- State and verify the 7 Strassen products for $2 \times 2$ matrices
- Compare Strassen to naive: when is the improvement noticeable?

**Common mistakes:**
- Forgetting that matrix additions are $O(n^2)$, which is why the combine step is $O(n^2)$
- Computing $\log_2 7$ incorrectly — it is approximately 2.807, not 2.5 or 3
- Thinking Strassen is always faster — for small $n$, constants matter and naive is faster

**Likely question:**
> "Describe Strassen's algorithm for $n \times n$ matrix multiplication. Write the recurrence relation and derive the time complexity using the Master Theorem. Compare with the naive algorithm." (12 marks)
