# Sequences and Summations

In programming, we constantly deal with arrays and lists of data. In mathematics, an ordered list of elements is called a **Sequence**. 

Formally, a sequence is a function from a subset of integers (usually the natural numbers $\mathbb{N}$) to a set $S$. We use the notation $a_n$ to denote the image of the integer $n$. 

> **Real World Analogy:** Think of a sequence as a line of houses on a street. The index $n$ is the address of the house (House #1, House #2), and $a_n$ is the family that lives inside it. 

Example: The sequence of even positive integers.
$a_1 = 2, a_2 = 4, a_3 = 6, a_4 = 8 ...$

We can define this sequence elegantly using a formula: $a_n = 2n$.

---

## The Formula Game

Being able to look at a sequence of numbers and deduce the underlying formula is a critical pattern-recognition skill. 

### Worked Example 1: Deducing Sequences

Let's find the formulas $a_n$ for the following sequences (assuming we start counting at $n=1$).

1. **Sequence:** $1, 3, 5, 7, 9, ...$
   - **Pattern:** These are odd numbers. We know evens are $2n$. To shift an even number down to an odd number, we subtract 1.
   - **Formula:** $a_n = 2n - 1$

2. **Sequence:** $-1, 1, -1, 1, -1, ...$
   - **Pattern:** The sign is alternating back and forth. Raising $-1$ to a power creates this exact oscillation.
   - **Formula:** $a_n = (-1)^n$

3. **Sequence:** $3, 9, 27, 81, 243, ...$
   - **Pattern:** Each number is being multiplied by 3. These are powers of 3.
   - **Formula:** $a_n = 3^n$

*(Note: Finite sequences—sequences that eventually stop—are often called **strings** in computer science. The length of the string is simply the number of terms it contains).*

## Summations ($\Sigma$)

Often, we don't just want to list the terms in a sequence; we want to add them all up. To express long addition concisely, we use **Summation Notation**, denoted by the Greek letter Sigma ($\Sigma$).

$$ \sum_{j=m}^{n} a_j $$

This notation instructs us to:
1. Start with the index $j$ equal to the lower limit $m$.
2. Substitute $j$ into the formula $a_j$.
3. Increment $j$ by 1, and repeat the process until $j$ reaches the upper limit $n$.
4. Add all the resulting terms together.

It represents the exact same thing as a `for` loop in programming:
```python
total = 0
for j in range(m, n + 1):
    total += a[j]
```

## Calculating Summations (The Famous Formulas)

If I ask you to compute $\sum_{j=1}^{100} j$, doing it manually ($1 + 2 + 3 + ... + 100$) would take an immense amount of time. 

Mathematical geniuses throughout history have provided us with closed-form formulas to calculate these massive sums instantly. You **must** memorize these for your exams.

### 1. The Arithmetic Series (Gauss's Formula)
When you are simply summing up consecutive integers:

$$ \sum_{j=1}^{n} j = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} $$

**Application:** $\sum_{j=1}^{100} j = \frac{100(101)}{2} = \frac{10100}{2} = 5050$

### 2. The Geometric Series
When the terms of the series are growing by a constant multiplier (like powers of $a$):

$$ \sum_{j=0}^{n} a^j = \frac{a^{n+1} - 1}{a - 1} $$

**Application:** $1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + ... + 1024$
This is $\sum_{j=0}^{10} 2^j$. Using the formula with $a=2, n=10$:
$ \frac{2^{11} - 1}{2 - 1} = \frac{2048 - 1}{1} = 2047 $

### Other Useful Series to Memorize
- Sum of Squares: $\sum_{j=1}^{n} j^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}$
- Sum of Cubes: $\sum_{j=1}^{n} j^3 = \frac{n^2(n+1)^2}{4}$

## Double Summations

In computer science, we frequently deal with nested `for` loops. In mathematics, this is represented by **Double Summations**.

### Worked Example 2: Evaluating a Double Sum

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{5} \sum_{j=1}^{2} ij $$

To solve this, always evaluate the **inner summation first**, treating the outer variable $i$ as a constant.

1. **Evaluate inner sum (from $j=1$ to $2$):**
   $\sum_{j=1}^{2} ij = i(1) + i(2) = i + 2i = 3i$

2. **Substitute back into outer sum:**
   We are left with: $\sum_{i=1}^{5} 3i$

3. **Evaluate final sum:**
   $3(1) + 3(2) + 3(3) + 3(4) + 3(5) = 3 + 6 + 9 + 12 + 15 = 45$

### Key Takeaways
- A **Sequence** is an ordered mathematical list generated by a formula $a_n$.
- **Summation Notation ($\Sigma$)** is the mathematical equivalent of a `for` loop, used to add up the terms of a sequence.
- The **Arithmetic Series** ($n(n+1)/2$) and **Geometric Series** ($(a^{n+1}-1)/(a-1)$) formulas allow you to compute massive sums instantly without manual addition.

### Exam Tips
- **Index Starting Points:** Always double-check the bottom of the Sigma ($\Sigma$) symbol. The geometric series formula starts at $j=0$. If an exam question starts the sum at $j=1$, you must calculate the sum from $j=0$ and then manually subtract the $j=0$ term!
- **Oscillating Signs:** If a sequence goes $+ - + -$, remember the trick is to use $(-1)^n$. If it goes $- + - +$, use $(-1)^{n+1}$.
