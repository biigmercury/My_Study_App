# Recurrence Relations

In the Mathematical Induction topic, we explored recursively defined sequences (like the Fibonacci sequence), where a term is defined by calculating the terms that came immediately before it. 

A **Recurrence Relation** is the formal mathematical equation that captures this behavior. 

A recurrence relation for a sequence $\{a_n\}$ expresses $a_n$ in terms of one or more of the previous terms ($a_{n-1}, a_{n-2}$, etc.). A sequence is called a **solution** if its terms satisfy the recurrence relation.

If you don't provide **initial conditions** (like $a_0 = 1$), a recurrence relation can have infinite possible solutions. But if both the relation and the initial conditions are provided, the sequence is uniquely locked in.

---

## Modeling with Recurrence Relations

Recurrence relations are incredibly useful for modeling real-world compounding scenarios and dynamic systems.

### Worked Example 1: Financial Modeling
You deposit $\$10,000$ into a bank yielding $5\%$ interest compounded annually. How much money is in the account after 30 years?

1. **Let $P_n$ be the amount after $n$ years.**
2. **Build the Relation:** To find this year's money, we take last year's money, and add $5\%$ of last year's money to it.
   $P_n = P_{n-1} + 0.05P_{n-1}$ 
   $P_n = 1.05P_{n-1}$
3. **The Base Case:** $P_0 = 10,000$

We can expand this to find a closed-form formula!
$P_1 = 1.05 P_0$
$P_2 = 1.05 P_1 = 1.05(1.05 P_0) = (1.05)^2 P_0$
$P_n = (1.05)^n P_0$

Now we can bypass the recursive loop entirely. To find the money after 30 years, we just plug in $n=30$:
$P_{30} = (1.05)^{30} \cdot 10,000 = \$43,219.42$.

## Solving Linear Homogeneous Recurrence Relations

Finding the closed-form formula in the bank example was easy. But what about a complex relation like $a_n = a_{n-1} + 2a_{n-2}$? We need a formal system to solve these.

A **linear homogeneous recurrence relation of degree $k$ with constant coefficients** looks like this:
$$ a_n = c_1a_{n-1} + c_2a_{n-2} + ... + c_ka_{n-k} $$
*(It is "linear" because there are no $a_{n-1}^2$ terms. It is "homogeneous" because there are no standalone constants hanging off the end).*

### The Characteristic Equation

To solve these, we assume the solution has the form $a_n = r^n$, where $r$ is a constant. We substitute $r^n$ into the relation, and then do some heavy algebraic division to arrive at the **Characteristic Equation**. 

For a degree two relation ($a_n = c_1a_{n-1} + c_2a_{n-2}$), the characteristic equation is always a simple quadratic:
$$ r^2 - c_1r - c_2 = 0 $$

### The Two Root Theorem
If the quadratic characteristic equation yields **two distinct roots** ($r_1$ and $r_2$), the formula for the sequence is guaranteed to be:
$$ a_n = \alpha_1(r_1)^n + \alpha_2(r_2)^n $$
Where $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ are constants that we can solve for using the initial conditions!

---

### Worked Example 2: The Two Root Solution

Find the explicit formula for $a_n = a_{n-1} + 2a_{n-2}$ with $a_0 = 2$ and $a_1 = 7$.

**Step 1: Extract the Characteristic Equation**
Move everything to one side conceptually to match $r^2 - c_1r - c_2 = 0$.
Here, $c_1 = 1$, and $c_2 = 2$.
Equation: $r^2 - r - 2 = 0$.

**Step 2: Find the Roots**
Factor the quadratic: $(r - 2)(r + 1) = 0$.
The roots are $r_1 = 2$ and $r_2 = -1$.

**Step 3: Setup the Formula Template**
$a_n = \alpha_1(2)^n + \alpha_2(-1)^n$

**Step 4: Solve for Constants using Base Cases**
We know $a_0 = 2$. Plug $n=0$ into the template:
$2 = \alpha_1(2)^0 + \alpha_2(-1)^0$ 
$2 = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2$   *(Equation A)*

We know $a_1 = 7$. Plug $n=1$ into the template:
$7 = \alpha_1(2)^1 + \alpha_2(-1)^1$
$7 = 2\alpha_1 - \alpha_2$   *(Equation B)*

**Step 5: Solve the System of Equations**
Add Equation A and B together:
$9 = 3\alpha_1 \implies \alpha_1 = 3$.
If $\alpha_1 = 3$, then $\alpha_2 = -1$.

**Final Answer:**
$a_n = 3(2)^n - 1(-1)^n$

## What if there is only one root?

If your quadratic equation factors into a perfect square, like $(r - 3)^2 = 0$, you only get one root: $r_0 = 3$. 

The Two Root theorem fails here. Instead, we use the **Single Root Theorem**. 
If $r^2 - c_1r - c_2 = 0$ has exactly one root $r_0$, the solution formula template is:
$$ a_n = \alpha_1(r_0)^n + \alpha_2 n(r_0)^n $$

Notice the extra $n$ injected into the second term! You then use your base cases to solve for $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ exactly as you did in the previous example.

---

### Key Takeaways
- A **Recurrence Relation** defines a sequence based on previous terms. Without initial conditions, it has infinite solutions.
- To convert a recursive loop into a closed-form formula, we convert the relation into a quadratic **Characteristic Equation**.
- If the quadratic has two roots, use the template: $a_n = \alpha_1(r_1)^n + \alpha_2(r_2)^n$.
- If the quadratic has one root, use the template with the injected $n$: $a_n = \alpha_1(r_0)^n + \alpha_2 n(r_0)^n$.

### Exam Tips
- **Don't Forget the Algebra:** You must be perfectly comfortable factoring quadratics and solving systems of two linear equations. If you make an algebra mistake solving for $\alpha_1$, the entire final formula will be wrong.
- **The $c_2$ Sign Trap:** The characteristic equation is $r^2 - c_1r - c_2 = 0$. Notice the minus signs! If your relation is $a_n = 5a_{n-1} - 6a_{n-2}$, then $c_2$ is $-6$. Therefore, the equation is $r^2 - 5r - (-6) = 0$, which becomes $r^2 - 5r + 6 = 0$. Messing up this sign is the #1 mistake students make.
