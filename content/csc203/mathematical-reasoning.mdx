# Mathematical Reasoning

In the sciences, we form hypotheses and conduct experiments to gather evidence. But in mathematics, evidence is not enough. To establish a mathematical truth, we must construct an airtight, undeniable **Proof**.

We need mathematical reasoning for two main reasons:
1. To determine whether an existing mathematical argument is correct or incorrect.
2. To construct our own mathematical arguments from scratch.

This formalized logic is the exact foundation upon which Artificial Intelligence systems (like expert systems and inference engines) are built.

## The Terminology of Proofs

Before we begin proving things, we must understand the vocabulary of the mathematical courtroom.

- **Axiom:** A basic, fundamental assumption that requires no proof (e.g., "$1 = 1$").
- **Theorem:** A major mathematical statement that can be shown to be true.
- **Lemma:** A "mini-theorem". It's a stepping stone result used only to help prove a larger theorem.
- **Corollary:** A proposition that follows instantly and obviously from a theorem that was just proven.
- **Conjecture:** A statement whose truth value is currently unknown. (Once someone proves it, it graduates to become a Theorem).
- **Fallacy:** A case of incorrect, flawed reasoning.

## Rules of Inference

A proof consists of a sequence of statements. But how do we link one statement to the next? We use **Rules of Inference**. These are logically sound templates that guarantee that if our premises are true, our conclusion *must* be true.

We write them in a column, with a line representing "therefore" ($\therefore$).

### 1. Modus Ponens (Law of Detachment)
If $p$ is true, and $p \rightarrow q$ is true, then $q$ must be true.
$$
\begin{array}{l}
p \\
p \rightarrow q \\
\hline
\therefore q
\end{array}
$$

### 2. Modus Tollens
If $p \rightarrow q$ is true, but $q$ is false, then $p$ must be false.
$$
\begin{array}{l}
\neg q \\
p \rightarrow q \\
\hline
\therefore \neg p
\end{array}
$$

### 3. Hypothetical Syllogism
The chain reaction rule.
$$
\begin{array}{l}
p \rightarrow q \\
q \rightarrow r \\
\hline
\therefore p \rightarrow r
\end{array}
$$

### 4. Disjunctive Syllogism
Process of elimination. If one of two options must be true, and option A is false, option B must be the true one.
$$
\begin{array}{l}
p \lor q \\
\neg p \\
\hline
\therefore q
\end{array}
$$

## Arguments and Validity

An **Argument** consists of one or more hypotheses (premises) and a conclusion.
An argument is **Valid** if, whenever all its hypotheses are true, its conclusion is guaranteed to be true.

> **CRITICAL DISTINCTION:** An argument can be *Valid* but still have an *Incorrect* conclusion! How? If the logic structure is valid, but the starting hypothesis is a lie.

**Example of a Valid but Incorrect Argument:**
- Hypothesis 1: "If 101 is divisible by 3, then $101^2$ is divisible by 9." ($p \rightarrow q$)
- Hypothesis 2: "101 is divisible by 3." ($p$)
- Conclusion: "Consequently, $101^2$ is divisible by 9." ($\therefore q$)

The structure here is perfect **Modus Ponens**. The logic is **Valid**. However, the conclusion is completely **Incorrect** because Hypothesis 2 is mathematically false!

## Rules of Inference for Quantified Statements

When dealing with $\forall$ (For All) and $\exists$ (There Exists), we have special rules to move between universal claims and specific instances.

1. **Universal Instantiation:** If a rule applies to everything ($\forall x P(x)$), it must apply to a specific item $c$ ($P(c)$).
   - *Example: All UMB students are geniuses. George is a UMB student. Therefore, George is a genius.*
2. **Universal Generalization:** If you can prove a property holds for an arbitrary, generic element $c$, you can claim it holds for everything ($\forall x P(x)$).
3. **Existential Instantiation:** If you know that $\exists x P(x)$, you can assume there is some specific element $c$ that satisfies the property, even if you don't know its exact identity.
4. **Existential Generalization:** If you know $P(c)$ is true for a specific $c$, you can zoom out and claim $\exists x P(x)$.

## Methods of Proving Theorems

### 1. Direct Proof
To prove an implication $p \rightarrow q$, you **assume $p$ is true**, and then use algebra and inference rules to logically arrive at $q$.

**Theorem:** "If $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd."
**Proof:**
1. Assume $n$ is odd. 
2. By mathematical definition, an odd number can be written as $n = 2k + 1$ (where $k$ is an integer).
3. We square it: $n^2 = (2k + 1)^2$.
4. Expand: $n^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1$.
5. Factor out a 2 from the first terms: $n^2 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1$.
6. Since $(2k^2 + 2k)$ is just another integer, the result is in the form $2(\text{integer}) + 1$.
7. Thus, $n^2$ is odd. **Proof complete.**

### 2. Indirect Proof (Proof by Contrapositive)
Sometimes assuming $p$ doesn't give you enough mathematical material to work with. Recall from logic that $p \rightarrow q$ is logically equivalent to its **Contrapositive**: $\neg q \rightarrow \neg p$.

To prove $p \rightarrow q$ indirectly, you **assume $q$ is false**, and prove that $p$ must also be false.

**Theorem:** "If $3n + 2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd."
*(Trying to isolate $n$ from $3n+2$ using direct proof involves messy fractions. Let's use indirect proof).*
**Proof:**
1. We prove the contrapositive: "If $n$ is EVEN, then $3n+2$ is EVEN."
2. Assume $n$ is even. 
3. By definition, $n = 2k$.
4. Substitute into the equation: $3(2k) + 2$.
5. Simplify: $6k + 2$.
6. Factor out a 2: $2(3k + 1)$.
7. Since it is in the form $2(\text{integer})$, the result is even. 
8. Since the contrapositive is true, the original theorem is true. **Proof complete.**

### Key Takeaways
- **Rules of Inference** are the grammatical rules of logic. Modus Ponens and Modus Tollens are the most critical tools for chaining thoughts together.
- A **Valid** argument structure does not guarantee a correct conclusion if your starting premises are false.
- A **Direct Proof** assumes $P$ and calculates $Q$.
- An **Indirect Proof** proves the contrapositive: assuming $\neg Q$ and calculating $\neg P$.

### Exam Tips
- **Recognizing Fallacies:** The two most common exam fallacies are the *Fallacy of Affirming the Conclusion* (assuming $q$ and trying to prove $p$) and the *Fallacy of Denying the Hypothesis* (assuming $\neg p$ and trying to prove $\neg q$). Neither of these are valid inference rules!
- **Odd/Even Definitions:** In any proof involving odd or even numbers, your absolute first step must be converting the English word into algebra: $Even = 2k$, $Odd = 2k + 1$. You cannot do math on the word "odd".
