# Counting and Combinatorics

How many different 8-character passwords can exist? What are the exact odds of winning the lottery? To answer these questions, we don't guess—we count systematically using **Combinatorics**.

Counting forms the absolute basis of probability theory. If you cannot correctly count the total number of possible events, you cannot calculate the probability of a specific event occurring.

## Basic Counting Principles

All complex counting can be broken down into two fundamental rules.

### 1. The Sum Rule
If a task can be done in $n_1$ ways, and a second task can be done in $n_2$ ways, and these tasks **cannot be done at the same time**, then there are $n_1 + n_2$ ways to do *either* task.

> **Example:** A department will award a free laptop to either a CS student OR a CS professor. There are 530 students and 15 professors. Because you cannot be both a student and a professor, the total choices are $530 + 15 = 545$.

**Set Theory Translation:** If sets $A$ and $B$ are completely disjoint (no overlap), then the cardinality of their union is simply $|A \cup B| = |A| + |B|$.

### 2. The Product Rule
If a procedure can be broken down into two successive, independent tasks, and there are $n_1$ ways to do the first task, and $n_2$ ways to do the second task, then there are $n_1 \cdot n_2$ ways to do the procedure.

> **Example:** How many 3-letter license plates exist? There are 26 choices for the first letter, 26 for the second, and 26 for the third. $26 \cdot 26 \cdot 26 = 17,576$. 

**Set Theory Translation:** The cardinality of a Cartesian Product is $|A \times B| = |A| \cdot |B|$.

## The Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion

What happens if the tasks in the Sum Rule *can* be done at the same time? We can't just add them blindly, or we will double-count the overlap!

**The Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion** dictates that we must subtract the overlap to get an accurate count:
$$ |A \cup B| = |A| + |B| - |A \cap B| $$

### Worked Example 1: Inclusion-Exclusion
How many bit strings of length 8 either start with a $1$ or end with $00$?
1. **Task 1 (Start with 1):** The first bit is fixed (1 option). The remaining 7 bits can be anything ($2^7 = 128$ options).
2. **Task 2 (End with 00):** The last 2 bits are fixed (1 option). The first 6 bits can be anything ($2^6 = 64$ options).
3. **The Overlap:** Strings that start with 1 AND end with 00. The first bit is fixed, the last two are fixed. The middle 5 bits can be anything ($2^5 = 32$ options).

**Total Calculation:** $128 + 64 - 32 = 160$ strings.

## The Pigeonhole Principle

This is an incredibly simple concept that yields profound mathematical proofs. 
**The Pigeonhole Principle:** If you have $(k+1)$ pigeons, and you put them into $k$ boxes, at least one box MUST contain two or more pigeons.

> **Example:** If 11 soccer players score a total of 12 goals in a game, we know with 100% mathematical certainty that at least one player scored 2 or more goals. 

### The Generalized Pigeonhole Principle
If $N$ objects are placed into $k$ boxes, then there is at least one box containing at least $\lceil N/k \rceil$ objects. (Remember, $\lceil \rceil$ is the ceiling function!).

> **Example:** In a class of 60 students, grades are A, B, C, D, or F (5 boxes). At least $\lceil 60 / 5 \rceil = 12$ students will get the exact same letter grade.

## Permutations vs. Combinations

This is the most common stumbling block for students. Both deal with selecting items from a larger group. The difference is entirely about **Order**.

### 1. Permutations (Order Matters)
An $r$-permutation is an ordered arrangement of $r$ elements from a set of $n$ elements.
- **Formula:** $P(n, r) = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!}$
- **Analogy:** Entering a 4-digit PIN code. 1-2-3-4 is completely different from 4-3-2-1. Order is critical. 

### 2. Combinations (Order is Meaningless)
An $r$-combination is an unordered selection of $r$ elements from a set.
- **Formula:** $C(n, r) = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}$
- **Analogy:** Picking 3 people to form a committee. Picking Alice, Bob, and Charlie is the exact same committee as picking Charlie, Alice, and Bob. The internal order doesn't matter.

> **CRITICAL INSIGHT:** Notice the formula for Combinations divides the Permutation formula by an extra $r!$. Why? Because if we select 3 people, there are $3!$ ($6$) ways those 3 people can be arranged. Since order doesn't matter, we divide by $3!$ to collapse those 6 identical permutations into 1 single combination.

## Binomial Coefficients & Pascal's Triangle

Expressions of the form $C(n, r)$ are also called **Binomial Coefficients**, because they appear when you expand binomial expressions like $(a + b)^n$.

To expand $(a + b)^4$, you don't have to manually multiply $(a+b)(a+b)(a+b)(a+b)$. The coefficients map perfectly to the rows of **Pascal's Triangle**.

```text
Row 0:        1
Row 1:       1 1
Row 2:      1 2 1
Row 3:     1 3 3 1
Row 4:    1 4 6 4 1
```

Using Row 4, we can instantly expand $(a+b)^4$:
$$ 1a^4 + 4a^3b + 6a^2b^2 + 4ab^3 + 1b^4 $$

**Pascal's Identity** proves the mathematical structure of the triangle: each number is the sum of the two numbers directly above it:
$$ C(n+1, k) = C(n, k-1) + C(n, k) $$

### Key Takeaways
- Use the **Sum Rule** for "OR" scenarios (disjoint tasks). Use the **Product Rule** for "AND" scenarios (sequential tasks).
- The **Pigeonhole Principle** is a powerful tool for proving existence without having to find the specific item.
- **Permutations** = Order Matters (PIN codes, races). **Combinations** = Order is Meaningless (Committees, pizza toppings).

### Exam Tips
- **Double Counting:** If an exam asks "How many passwords have 3 letters OR 3 numbers", immediately write down the Inclusion-Exclusion formula before calculating anything. The overlap is the number one cause of failed counting questions.
- **Combination Symmetry:** Remember that $C(n, r) = C(n, n-r)$. Picking 2 elements to keep out of 6 is mathematically identical to picking 4 elements to throw away. Using this trick can drastically simplify the factorials you have to compute by hand.
