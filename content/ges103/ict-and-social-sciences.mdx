## Why ICT Belongs in the Social Sciences

**Information and Communications Technology (ICT)** is part of everyday life and plays an important role in **teaching and learning**. Teaching and learning are **relationship issues** — and the social sciences study **human interaction and behaviour**. As the chapter's author (**A. S. Jegede**) puts it, **"all disciplines rest on the oars of the social sciences"**, because no discipline is independent of human beings. So ICT in the social sciences indirectly improves the performance of every other discipline.

**Learning outcomes.** By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. distinguish the **natural** from the **social sciences**;
2. explain what **ICT** is;
3. explain the **relevance of ICT** to the social sciences;
4. explain **ICT's role in teaching and learning** the social sciences.

## Natural and Social Sciences

**Science** is **the application of systematic methods to obtain knowledge, and the knowledge obtained by those methods**.

- People have always tried to understand their world. In ancient times their explanations were mixed with **magic and superstition**.
- **Science** grew as **systematic methods of studying the social and natural world**.

| Natural sciences | Social sciences |
|---|---|
| Explain and **predict events in the natural environment** | **Examine human relationships** — attempting to understand the **social world objectively** |
| The ordered, lawful relationships of nature are **not obvious** and must be found by **controlled observation** | The ordered relationships of the social world are also **not obvious**, and must be revealed by **repeated observation** |
| **Biology** (botany, zoology), **geology** (mineralogy, geomorphology), **chemistry** (organic, inorganic), **physics** (biophysics, quantum mechanics) — each examines a **"slice" of nature** | **Anthropology, economics, political science, psychology, sociology** |

```sort
Organic chemistry => Natural science :: A branch of chemistry.
Psychology — the study of behaviour and mental processes => Social science :: The textbook lists psychology among the social sciences.
```

## What Is ICT?

The term **ICT** was introduced in the **early 1990s** to **replace "information technology" (IT)**. The **"C"** recognises the **communicating abilities** of the computer (Adesoki and Fatoki 2013).

- ICT covers a **whole range of applications, techniques and systems** — **computers, communications equipment and their associated services** (Clarke 2006).
- ICT is **not only applications and systems but a skill for life**, like **literacy**. Everyone needs it to live **confidently, effectively and independently** in modern society (Clarke 2006).

### Three positions of ICT in the curriculum

| Position | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Learning about ICT** | ICT as a **subject of learning** in the curriculum | A course in computer literacy or programming |
| **Learning with ICT** | ICT as a **medium to facilitate instruction** | A lecturer using slides, videos or an interactive simulation |
| **Learning through ICT** | ICT **integrated as an essential tool**, so the course **cannot be taught or learnt without it** | A statistics course taught entirely in SPSS, or an online degree |

<SortingGame title="About, with or through ICT?">

```sort
@buckets Learning about ICT | Learning with ICT | Learning through ICT
Students take a course on how computer networks work => Learning about ICT :: ICT is the subject being learnt.
A sociology lecturer shows a video of a market to illustrate social interaction => Learning with ICT :: ICT is a medium supporting instruction.
A data-analysis course can only be done in SPSS in the computer lab => Learning through ICT :: The course is impossible without the technology.
Pupils learn the parts of a computer and what a mouse does => Learning about ICT :: Computer knowledge as curriculum content.
A lecturer posts lecture slides and quizzes on the university's learning platform => Learning with ICT :: Technology facilitates the teaching.
A fully online distance-learning degree delivered through video lectures and forums => Learning through ICT :: The whole course depends on ICT.
```

</SortingGame>

## The Relevance of ICT to the Social Sciences

ICT helps us **understand the social sciences better** today. It has become:

- a **learning tool**;
- a **platform of interaction between groups**;
- a **form of communication** fast becoming a **universal language**.

When ICT supports traditional study, **computer-based learning becomes a tool**.

| Area | How ICT helps |
|---|---|
| **Economics and management** | Computer knowledge helps manpower in the **production and distribution of goods and services**. With computers, an organisation can track **what it produces, what it costs and how it is distributed** |
| **Employability** | Jobs increasingly depend on **computer skills** — especially in the **financial and banking sector** |
| **Political science** | Analysing **voting behaviour**, and even **predicting it** from pre-election surveys. The **card reader** in Nigerian elections influences how people attain office. In some developed countries results appear **as votes are cast** *(beyond the textbook: INEC replaced the card reader with the **Bimodal Voter Accreditation System (BVAS)** and uploaded results to its **IReV** portal in the **2023** elections)* |

### ICT literacy

Nigerian institutions now teach the social sciences within a framework of theory and research in which effective classroom communication **requires communication technologies**. **Alvin Toffler** warned:

> "The illiterate of the 21st century will not be those who cannot read and write, but those who cannot **learn, unlearn, and relearn**."

### Four basic issues in using ICT

**Haddad and Jurich (2002)** identify **four issues** in using ICT in education:

1. **effectiveness**;
2. **cost**;
3. **equity** (the textbook says "equality");
4. **sustainability**.

There is great interest in how ICTs — above all **computers and the internet** — can improve the **efficiency and effectiveness** of education at every level, formal and non-formal.

## ICT's Role in Teaching and Learning

ICT affects three things:

- the **teacher**;
- the **learner**;
- the **image of the social sciences** as a discipline.

### From content to competency

**Conventional teaching** emphasises **content**, delivered through **lectures and presentations** with tutorials to rehearse it (Kamal and Banu 2010). **Contemporary curricula** — especially in developed countries — emphasise **competency and performance**: **how information will be used**, not just what it is. Instructional technologies support this shift (Stephenson 2001). Such curricula require:

- access to a **variety of information sources**, forms and types;
- **student-centred** learning based on **information access and inquiry**;
- **problem-centred, inquiry-based** activities;
- **authentic** settings and examples;
- **teachers as coaches and mentors** rather than content experts.

| Conventional (teacher-centred) | Contemporary (student-centred, ICT-supported) |
|---|---|
| Content-centred curriculum | **Competency- and performance-based** curriculum |
| Knowledge **transmitted** by the teacher | Knowledge **constructed** by the learner |
| Teacher as content expert | Teacher as **coach and mentor** |
| Students wait to be told | Students take **responsibility for their own learning** |
| Library and lecture notes | The **web**, databases and **problem-based learning** |

### Information literacy skills

Graduates must show **information literacy**: **"the capacity to identify an issue and then to identify, locate and evaluate relevant information in order to engage with it or to solve a problem arising from it"** (McCausland, Wache and Berk 1999). Institutions want graduates with **generic skills** as well as subject knowledge:

- reasoning and problem solving;
- communication and negotiation;
- time and project management;
- teamwork;
- and now **information literacy** (Kamal and Banu 2010).

### Independent learning

- Students used to be comfortable learning by **transmission** — letting others present the curriculum to them.
- ICTs are **by nature tools for independent learning**. They move education from a **teacher-directed enterprise** to **student-centred** models.
- Students increasingly use computers as **information sources and cognitive tools** (Jonassen and Reeves 1996).
- ICT removes the limits of **distance**: students can learn **anywhere** — a herder in a remote village can learn by mobile phone.

### ICT and constructivism

ICT's rise has coincided with **constructivist** learning theories (Duffy and Cunningham 1996):

- **Learning is the active construction of knowledge**, supported by multiple perspectives in **meaningful contexts**.
- **Social interaction** plays a **critical role** in learning.
- Instruction **supports knowledge construction** rather than **transmitting knowledge**.
- Learning is the **construction of meaning**, not the **memorisation of facts** (Lebow 1993; Jonassen and Reeves 1996).

<Analogy>
Old-style teaching is like being handed a cooked meal: you eat what the lecturer prepared. Constructivist, ICT-supported learning is like being given a kitchen, ingredients and a recipe book. You search, try, fail, ask friends online and cook your own understanding — and you remember it far better.
</Analogy>

## Factors Supporting or Hindering ICT Use

| Supporting factors | Hindering factors |
|---|---|
| **Availability of funds** for integrating ICT into university education (Nwankwoala 2015) | **Inadequate funding** of Nigerian universities; high costs of ICT facilities and **internet maintenance** |
| **Availability of infrastructure** — computers, multimedia, information providers, **adequate power supply** (Kessy, Kaemba and Gachoka 2006) | **Inadequate infrastructure** — **shared computers** that must be **booked in advance**; poor-quality or **obsolete** equipment; **no broadband**; **epileptic power supply** |
| **Positive attitude to technological change** — teachers' attitudes and **constructivist beliefs** strongly predict ICT use (Almusalam 2001; Sang et al. 2010) | **Negative attitude to change** — lecturers comfortable with **face-to-face, teacher-centred** methods, which give them a sense of power. Some see ICT as a **waste of time** and skip ICT workshops |
| **Competence in operating ICT** — **Bemer (2003)** found that faculty's **belief in their computer competence** was the **greatest predictor** of classroom computer use (among seven variables) | **Inadequate professional training** — too few **ICT technical staff** to train lecturers (Mathipa and Mukhari 2014) |
| **Personal characteristics** — **younger, less experienced** teachers use computers more (US Department of Education 2000). In Nigeria, some **older lecturers feel ICT is for the younger generation** | **Lack of technological know-how** → low **confidence**, **anxiety and fear of failure** (Pelgrum 2001). Students may have other priorities, or institutions may be slow to introduce ICT |

<SortingGame title="Does it support or hinder ICT use?">

```sort
@buckets Supports ICT use | Hinders ICT use
The faculty budget funds new computers and broadband => Supports ICT use :: Availability of funds.
Students must book the only computer lab days in advance => Hinders ICT use :: Inadequate, shared infrastructure.
A lecturer believes she is competent with computers => Supports ICT use :: Bemer (2003): belief in one's competence is the strongest predictor.
Power goes off in the middle of an online test => Hinders ICT use :: Irregular power supply.
A senior lecturer says "ICT is for the young people" and avoids it => Hinders ICT use :: A negative attitude to technological change.
A young lecturer who used computers throughout her training readily adopts online quizzes => Supports ICT use :: Personal characteristics — fewer years of experience predict more use.
There are no technical staff to train lecturers on the new platform => Hinders ICT use :: Inadequate professional training.
```

</SortingGame>

## Advantages and Disadvantages of ICT in the Social Sciences

ICT has made the world a **global village** (Bhatti 2013). **Fung (2013)** says ICT affects all **three phases of social science research**:

- **pre-data analysis**;
- **data analysis**;
- **post-data analysis**.

To succeed in each, the social scientist must be **skilled in ICT**.

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Advantages: research">
**Pre-data analysis (literature):**

- **Articles are available online** — no more hunting through libraries, borrowing or buying books, or scanning hard copies line by line for key words.
- **Online libraries** hold millions of digitised journals, books and encyclopaedias, and **complete theses**, available **any time of day**.
- Digital storage **cannot be destroyed, stolen or misshelved** like printed copies.
- ICT enables the **exchange of ideas** among social scientists anywhere — a research hub for a **global village** of different disciplines.
- **Mendeley** manages the large numbers of articles and books read during research.

**Data analysis:**

- **Qualitative:** **Dragon NaturallySpeaking** transcribes recorded interviews accurately; **NVivo** and **ATLAS.ti** reduce the stress of manual analysis.
- **Quantitative:** **SPSS** is available and affordable — no manual working of formulas, though you must know **which analysis you need**.

**Post-data analysis:**

- **Plagiarism-detection** software.
- **Online submission** of manuscripts, with easy communication with **reviewers, editors and publishers**, and no manuscripts lost in transit.
</Tab>
<Tab label="Advantages: teaching">
- Learning is **no longer limited to time and place** (Talebian, Mohammadi and Rezvanfar 2014); remoteness is no barrier.
- It opens **collaboration** between students and academics; students can be referred to **online resources** for specific courses.
- **Flexibility** allows new forms of learning and **student-centredness** (OECD Centre for Educational Research and Innovation 2008). Students learn by **thinking and connecting with the real world**, which builds **confidence**.
- It improves the **quality of education**, with a **wider reach** through **chat rooms and video conferencing**.
- It gives lecturers **more time for personal development** alongside teaching, research and administration.
</Tab>
<Tab label="Disadvantages">
**Research:** online resources, especially online libraries, must be **continually increased and updated** as demand grows.

**Teaching:**

- Online resources may be **inaccurate** or out of step with the course content.
- It is hard to hold **practical sessions** and assess **practical knowledge** online.
- It **limits face-to-face interaction** between students and lecturers.
- It is hard to **measure students' progress**.
- It is hard to **verify that the registered student** is the one participating.
- Students may lack **internet access**, which hinders full participation.
</Tab>
</Tabs>

<SortingGame title="Which phase of research does the tool help? (Fung 2013)">

```sort
@buckets Pre-data analysis | Data analysis | Post-data analysis
Searching an online library for journal articles for your literature review => Pre-data analysis :: Literature is sourced before data are analysed.
Managing hundreds of references in Mendeley => Pre-data analysis :: Tracking the literature read during the research.
Running a t-test in SPSS => Data analysis :: Quantitative data analysis.
Coding interview transcripts in NVivo => Data analysis :: Qualitative data analysis.
Transcribing recorded interviews with Dragon NaturallySpeaking => Data analysis :: The textbook lists transcription software under managing qualitative data.
Checking a thesis with plagiarism-detection software => Post-data analysis :: After analysis, when the work is written up.
Submitting a manuscript online and corresponding with peer reviewers => Post-data analysis :: Publication happens after analysis.
```

</SortingGame>

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. Distinguish between the natural sciences and the social sciences.">
Science is the systematic method of obtaining knowledge and the knowledge obtained. The natural sciences explain and predict events in the natural environment through controlled observation; they include biology (botany, zoology), geology (mineralogy, geomorphology), chemistry (organic, inorganic) and physics (biophysics, quantum mechanics). The social sciences examine human relationships, trying to understand the social world objectively through repeated observation, because its ordered relationships are not obvious; they include anthropology, economics, political science, psychology and sociology.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. What is ICT, and what are its three positions in the educational curriculum?">
ICT, introduced in the early 1990s to replace "IT", covers a whole range of applications, techniques and systems — computers, communications equipment and their services — and is also a life skill comparable to literacy (Clarke 2006). Learning about ICT: ICT as a subject. Learning with ICT: ICT as a medium to facilitate instruction. Learning through ICT: ICT integrated as an essential tool, so the course cannot be taught without it.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Explain five ways in which ICT is relevant to the social sciences.">
Any five: a learning tool; a platform for interaction between groups; a form of communication fast becoming a universal language; helping manpower in the production and distribution of goods and services (tracking output, costs and distribution); employability, especially in banking; political analysis of voting behaviour and electoral technology (card readers, now BVAS); ICT literacy (Toffler's "learn, unlearn, relearn"); information literacy; independent, student-centred learning; and support for constructivist learning.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Identify the factors favouring and hindering the use of ICT in the social sciences.">
Favouring: available funds; available infrastructure (computers, multimedia, power); positive attitudes and constructivist beliefs; competence in operating ICT (Bemer: belief in one's competence is the greatest predictor); and personal characteristics (younger, less experienced teachers use it more). Hindering: inadequate funding and costly internet; inadequate infrastructure (shared computers, obsolete equipment, no broadband, epileptic power); inadequate professional training and too few technical staff; negative attitudes to change (preferring teacher-centred methods, seeing ICT as a waste of time); and lack of know-how, which causes anxiety.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="5. Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of ICT in the social sciences.">
Advantages in research (Fung's three phases): online articles, libraries and theses, and reference managers like Mendeley (pre-data); transcription (Dragon), NVivo and ATLAS.ti for qualitative data and SPSS for quantitative data (analysis); plagiarism detection and online submission and peer review (post-data); plus idea exchange in a global village. In teaching: learning unbounded by time and place, collaboration, flexibility and student-centredness, quality and reach (video conferencing), and lecturer time. Disadvantages: online resources need constant updating and may be inaccurate; practicals are hard to run; less face-to-face interaction; progress and identity are hard to verify; and many students lack internet access.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Science** = systematic methods plus the knowledge they produce. **Natural sciences** (biology, geology, chemistry, physics) vs **social sciences** (anthropology, economics, political science, psychology, sociology).
- **ICT** replaced "IT" in the **early 1990s**; it covers computers, communications equipment and services, and is a **life skill** (Clarke 2006).
- Three positions: learning **about**, **with** and **through** ICT.
- Relevance: learning tool, interaction platform, universal language, production and distribution, **employability** (banking), **voting analysis** and election technology.
- **Toffler**: the illiterate cannot "learn, unlearn and relearn"; **Haddad and Jurich**: effectiveness, cost, equity, sustainability.
- Teaching and learning: **competency-based**, student-centred curricula; **information literacy**; **independent learning**; **constructivism**.
- **Support vs hindrance**: funds, infrastructure, attitude, competence (**Bemer**), personal traits vs poor funding, power, training, attitude, know-how.
- **Fung's three research phases**: pre-data (online libraries, Mendeley), data (SPSS, NVivo, ATLAS.ti, Dragon), post-data (plagiarism checks, online submission).
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- The **three positions of ICT** are a near-certain MCQ — watch for the fake option "learning development of ICT".
- Know the **four issues of Haddad and Jurich** and **Toffler's quotation**.
- For the "factors" essay, present **supporting and hindering factors as pairs** (funding vs inadequate funding, and so on) — it is easy to remember and to mark.
- Name **specific software** for each research phase: Mendeley, SPSS, NVivo, ATLAS.ti, Dragon NaturallySpeaking.
