## An Interdisciplinary Field

**Peace and conflict studies** is an **interdisciplinary social science and humanistic** field. It draws on:

- sociology, political science and philosophy;
- history and psychology;
- social anthropology, geography and economics;
- religious studies.

It gives students the **knowledge, language and skills** to understand conflict situations and to see the **opportunities in peace**.

---

## Origins: Two Streams That Merged

The field now called **peace and conflict resolution studies** was born **simultaneously but separately** as two streams. Their aims are **fundamentally and logically interwoven** (Robben and Ferrandiz 2007; Gleditsch et al. 2014; Ramsbotham et al. 2011):

| | Peace research | Conflict studies |
|---|---|---|
| **Focus** | **Prevention of conflicts and maintenance of peace** | **Settling disputes and sustaining peace** |
| **Roots** | Moral reflection by statesmen — **Woodrow Wilson, Georges Clemenceau, David Lloyd George** — on the cost of the **First World War**: about **8 million soldiers killed** and **2 million missing**. This led to the **League of Nations (1919)** | The **Cold War** of the 1950s–60s, when **nuclear weapons** and superpower rivalry seemed to threaten human survival |
| **Where and when** | **Scandinavian universities, 1950s**. Calls for the **systematic study of the conditions of peace** as a discipline — by social and political scientists, in contrast to **war studies**, which military scholars dominated | **North America, 1950s–60s**: the **Journal of Conflict Resolution (1957)** and the **Centre for Conflict Resolution, University of Michigan (1959)** |
| **Grew into** | Human rights, **human security**, **transitional justice**, violence prevention, peace building, **reconciliation**, identity formation, cultural trauma | The study of conflict as a **general phenomenon** — whether international, domestic, industrial, communal, family or between individuals. It applied methods from **industrial relations** and **community mediation** |

**Conflict resolution in action by the 1980s:**

- **South Africa's Centre for Intergroup Studies** applied the methods to the struggle against **apartheid**.
- **Israeli and Arab negotiators** gained experience of each other through **problem-solving workshops** before the Middle East peace process.
- **Development and humanitarian agencies** in Africa and South-East Asia made conflict resolution part of their work.

### The merger after the Cold War

The **dissolution of the Soviet Union** ended the Cold War. Attention shifted to **internal conflicts**:

- **ethnic conflicts**;
- **secessionist movements**;
- **power struggles within states**.

At their extremes these brought **mercenary armies**, underpaid **militias preying on civilians**, and aid diverted by **criminal networks**. Peace researchers, conflict scholars, practitioners and statesmen **cross-fertilised** their ideas (Ramsbotham et al. 2011):

- Statesmen adopted the language of **"peace building"** and **"conflict resolution"**.
- The **OSCE** and the **OAU** set up **conflict prevention mechanisms and centres**.
- **NGOs** emerged — e.g. the **Carter Center** and the **Nyerere Foundation**.
- Development ministries created **conflict units**. The **IMF and World Bank** took on peace-building roles.
- **Preventing violent conflict** became a **central goal of the international community** in the new millennium.

## The Concept of Conflict

Understanding conflict is the **starting point for appreciating peace**. **Conflict is a universal feature of human society**. It arises from:

- **economic differentiation**;
- **social change**;
- **cultural formation**;
- **psychological development**;
- **political organisation**.

It becomes visible when **parties with mutually opposing goals** form.

**Lewis Coser (1956):** conflict is **"the struggle over values or claims to status, power and scarce resources, in which the aims of the individuals or groups involved are to neutralise, injure or eliminate rivals."** It requires:

- **two or more parties**;
- **incompatible interests** or goals (real or perceived);
- **hostile attitudes**, or actions that **damage the other**.

**Parties** may be **individuals, small or large groups, or countries**. The usual **issues** are (Payson Conflict Group 2001):

- **access to and distribution of common resources** — territory, money, energy, food;
- **political power** and participation in decision-making;
- **identity** — cultural, social, political communities;
- **values** — systems of government, religion, ideology.

**Conflicts are dynamic.** They **escalate and de-escalate**, and they take on a life of their own through attitudes and behaviour. **Third parties** often get drawn in and become parties to an **extended conflict** (Ramsbotham et al. 2011).

### Types of conflict

General types include:

- **pseudo-conflict**;
- conflict of **interests**, **values**, **goals** and **perception**;
- **role** conflict;
- **resource** conflict.

In international relations, **Peter Wallensteen (2002)** identifies **three general types**:

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Inter-state conflict** | Disputes **between nation-states**, or violations of the state system of alliances | Iraq's invasion of Kuwait (1990); the Ethiopia–Eritrea war (1998–2000) |
| **Internal conflict** | **Civil crises and ethnic wars** within a state | Rwanda (1994); communal clashes |
| **State-formation conflict** (a variant of internal conflict) | **Anti-colonial struggles, secessionist and autonomy movements, territorial disputes, battles over control of government** | The Biafran secession (1967–70); Kenya's Mau Mau anti-colonial struggle |

Other scholars split internal conflict into **intra-group, inter-group and national** conflict. There are also **global conflicts**, in which **non-state groups** fight **international and regional organisations**. The international community is increasingly worried by the rise of **internal conflicts**. They are driving more — and more sophisticated — **interventionist policies**.

## Theories of the Causes of Conflict

**McCandless (2007)** groups theories into two broad categories:

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Agency: social & psychological">
These theories locate causes in **individual or collective agency** — **human behaviour**, **perceptions and misperceptions**:

1. **Aggression is innate** — **biologically programmed** in humans.
2. **Psychoanalytic:** the distinction between **"self" and "other"** creates a **psychological need for enemies**.
3. **Psycho-social:** **group formation and differentiation** — images, perceptions, **misperceptions, stereotyping and dehumanisation** in decision-making — lead to violence.

Drawing on these, **"ethnic conflict"** and **"identity conflict"** became common labels for many African conflicts in the **1990s**.
</Tab>
<Tab label="Structure: political economy">
These theories hold that **the organisation of society itself** creates the causes and conditions of conflict. They look at society's general forces and dynamics:

- **Johan Galtung's "structural violence"** — **unequal social structures** give different groups **unequal access to resources**.
- A **1990s revival** in **international political economy** and **war-economy** studies argues that **global political and economic processes** produce **systemic conflicts**. These studies ask **who benefits from war** and how societies are **re-ordered** by it (Duffield; Keen 1998; Goodhand and Hulme 1999).
</Tab>
</Tabs>

### Concepts that balance both perspectives

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Protracted social conflict (Azar)">
**Edward Azar's protracted social conflict (PSC)** centres on **religious, cultural or ethnic communal identity**. It arises from **long-term unmet basic needs** — **security, communal recognition and distributive justice**. These produce patterns that **ordinary diplomacy or force cannot easily remedy**. Azar names **four preconditions**:

1. **Communal content** — politicised groups sharing ethnic, religious, linguistic or cultural identity.
2. **Deprivation of human needs** — physical security, access to institutions, acceptance of identity.
3. **The state and governance** — whether the state **satisfies or denies** basic needs.
4. **International linkages** — economic dependency and **patron–client** relations with strong states, where protection is given in return for loyalty.

PSCs occur mostly in **developing countries**, worsened by **extreme poverty**. Even **rapid growth-oriented strategies** can worsen them by increasing **inequality**, **duality** between modern and traditional sectors, and **marginalisation** (Azar 1990).
</Tab>
<Tab label="Complex political emergencies">
**Jonathan Goodhand and David Hulme (1999)** use **complex political emergencies (CPEs)** as shorthand for conflicts that:

1. occur **within and across state boundaries** — a **hybrid**, neither purely inter-state nor contained by domestic institutions;
2. have **political origins** — **competition for power and scarce resources** is the central dynamic;
3. are **protracted**, with enduring features that block a quick return to normal;
4. involve **social cleavages** — political, economic, social and cultural;
5. feature **predatory social formations**, often **ethno-nationalist**, mobilised and manipulated by **"conflict entrepreneurs"** and political opportunists.
</Tab>
</Tabs>

## The Concept of Peace

| Negative peace | Positive peace |
|---|---|
| **The absence of conflict or war** — "neither war nor peace", a **tense, yet-to-be-violent** situation (Miller 2005) | A **political condition that ensures justice and social stability** through formal and informal **institutions, practices and norms** |
| Mere absence of violence **despite mutually antagonistic goals, interests and values** (Galtung 1990) | More than the absence of war. **Absence of conflict is impossible** in human society — peace is **notoriously elusive** |

**Four ways of defining peace** (Payson Conflict Group 2001):

- **peace as harmony** — the absence of conflict;
- **peace as order** — stability, **"peace through strength"**;
- **peace as justice** — the absence of **domination and poverty**;
- **peace as conflict management** — a **process** for obtaining interests and needs, not an end in itself.

**Conditions for positive peace** (Miller 2005):

1. A **balance of political power** among groups.
2. **Legitimacy** of decision-makers, backed by **transparency and accountability**.
3. Recognised, valued **interdependent relationships** that sustain long-term co-operation.
4. **Reliable and trusted institutions** for resolving conflicts.
5. A **sense of equality and respect**, in sentiment and practice.
6. **Mutual understanding** of rights, interests and intents, with flexibility.

Peace should also be distinguished from **techniques that merely avoid conflict**, and from **violent or coercive** ways of managing it.

<SortingGame title="Negative or positive peace?">

```sort
@buckets Negative peace | Positive peace
Two communities have stopped fighting, but still distrust each other and wait for revenge => Negative peace :: The absence of violence despite antagonistic goals.
A country builds trusted courts and fair institutions where groups settle disputes => Positive peace :: Reliable institutions for resolving conflict.
A ceasefire holds only because soldiers patrol every junction => Negative peace :: Peace through strength without justice — "neither war nor peace".
Minority groups share power and feel equally respected => Positive peace :: A balance of power, and equality and respect.
Poverty and domination are reduced so that grievances disappear => Positive peace :: Peace as justice.
```

</SortingGame>

## Key Terms in Peace and Conflict Studies

### Peace terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Peace building** | Measures to **consolidate peaceful relations** and prevent tensions escalating into conflict; promoting **human security** so societies can **manage conflict without violence** |
| **Peace enforcement** | The **use or threat of military force**, usually with **international authorisation** (**Chapter VII of the UN Charter**), to compel compliance with resolutions or sanctions and **restore peace** — e.g. **Korea, 1950–1953**. It **can occur without the consent** of the warring parties. It includes **sanctions**, arms embargoes, **air or missile strikes** on the side that refuses to stop, and rapid deployment to disarm armed groups |
| **Peacekeeping operations** | Activities to halt or prevent fighting, uphold **law and order**, deliver **humanitarian aid** and restore institutions and daily life — **conducted under a mandate from the UN or a regional organisation** |
| **Peace operations** | The **umbrella term**: **diplomatic** activities (preventive diplomacy, peacemaking, peace building) plus two **military** ones (**peacekeeping and peace enforcement**) |

### Conflict resolution terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Conflict resolution** | A process that **transforms conflicts in an enduring way**, rather than settling or suppressing them. It addresses **basic human needs** and builds **sustainable relationships** — e.g. through **equality among identity groups, multiculturalism and federalism** — and fosters co-operation and trust through **reconciliation** |
| **Peace and Conflict Impact Assessment System (PCIAS)** | Estimates the **negative effects of conflict escalation**; early-warning systems should also show the **cost of a "wait-and-see" policy** |
| **Conflict mitigation / management** | Containing and **reducing violence** and engaging the parties in a settlement process |
| **Conflict prevention** | Actions, policies and institutions that keep groups from **using armed force** — including after a conflict, to **avoid recurrence** |
| **Post-conflict peace building** | Multi-dimensional programmes that address **root causes** to prevent a **relapse** |
| **Preventive diplomacy** | **Skilled assistance** — good offices, mediation — to resolve disputes **before they erupt into armed conflict**, and to **contain their spread** |
| **Early warning** | **Systematic collection and analysis of information** from crisis areas to **anticipate escalation** and present options to decision-makers |
| **Interactive conflict resolution** | **Small-group problem-solving** between **unofficial representatives** of groups in conflict, facilitated by an **impartial third party** |
| **Preventive (pre-conflict) peace building** | Using **relief and development resources** to strengthen conflict-prone societies' capacity for **non-violent** dispute resolution, without creating new disputes |
| **Preventive strategies** | Rest on three principles: **early reaction** to warning signs; a **comprehensive, balanced approach**; and **effort to resolve root causes**. Two types: **operational prevention** (in an immediate crisis) and **structural prevention** (so crises do not arise or recur) |

### Other common terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Mediation** | A **neutral, impartial third party** helps disputants **negotiate** an agreement. **Unlike judges or arbitrators, mediators cannot decide the dispute** — the parties empower them to help |
| **Power mediation** | Mediation **plus leverage** — **promised rewards or threatened punishment** |
| **Pure mediation** | Facilitation through **reasoning, persuasion, control of information and suggestion** only |
| **Reconciliation** | Addressing the conditions that caused conflict and **transforming adversarial relationships into harmonious ones**; builds nationhood and **social capital** |
| **Reconstruction** | Rebuilding **physical structures** and services, and the **economic, political and social** rebuilding of a post-conflict society — **disarmament, demobilisation and reintegration** of combatants, return of refugees and **internally displaced persons**, **new elections** |
| **Capacity building** | Developing individual and institutional abilities to **transform conflict into a constructive force** |
| **Compromise** | A settlement in which **each party makes concessions** |
| **Conciliation** | Two sides **voluntarily** agree to a compromise; unlike **arbitration**, it **does not compel** acceptance. A **conciliator** is a trusted third party who provides a **communication link**, lowers tension and moves the parties towards **negotiation** |

<FlowDiagram title="The four stages of mediation">
<FlowStep title="1. Create the forum">
Set up the **bargaining framework** — who meets, where, under what ground rules, with the mediator accepted by both sides.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="2. Gather and share information">
Each side's **interests, grievances and facts** are brought out and exchanged, often through the mediator.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="3. Problem-solving bargaining">
The parties explore options and trade concessions, with the mediator suggesting and persuading (**pure mediation**) or adding rewards and threats (**power mediation**).
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="4. Decision making">
The **parties themselves** reach and commit to an agreement — the mediator has **no power to impose** one.
</FlowStep>
</FlowDiagram>

<SortingGame title="Name the peace or conflict-resolution activity">

```sort
@buckets Peacekeeping | Peace enforcement | Preventive diplomacy | Mediation | Early warning | Reconciliation
ECOMOG troops deployed under a regional mandate to hold a ceasefire and protect aid convoys => Peacekeeping :: Operations under a UN or regional mandate to halt fighting and restore order.
UN-authorised air strikes on a militia that refuses to stop fighting => Peace enforcement :: Chapter VII force, which may be used without the parties' consent.
An elder statesman's good offices defuse a boundary dispute before any shots are fired => Preventive diplomacy :: Skilled assistance before disputes erupt into armed conflict.
A neutral third party helps two unions and management reach their own agreement => Mediation :: The mediator facilitates but cannot impose a decision.
An NGO monitors hate speech and arms movements in a region and alerts the government => Early warning :: Systematic collection and analysis to anticipate escalation.
Former enemies take part in truth-telling and joint community projects => Reconciliation :: Transforming adversarial relationships into harmonious ones.
```

</SortingGame>

## Case Study: Niger Delta Militancy and the Federal Response

*(The textbook asks students to note the peace and conflict terms used in this case.)*

The **Niger Delta** provides most of Nigeria's revenue — about **80% of the national wealth** from oil. The region is usually taken to include **nine states**:

- **Abia, Akwa Ibom, Bayelsa**;
- **Cross River, Delta, Edo**;
- **Imo, Ondo, Rivers**.

Despite this wealth, the region shows **extreme poverty, unemployment, water pollution, environmental degradation and government neglect**. Its **ethnic minorities** agitated for a larger share of oil wealth and **resource control**. The agitation degenerated into **youth militancy and criminality** that shook the Nigerian state.

**Classification:** the Niger Delta militancy is a **protracted social conflict (PSC)**.

**Explanation — the frustration–aggression theory:** proposed by **John Dollard and his associates** (the textbook dates it 1938; *Frustration and Aggression* was published in **1939**):

- **aggression always presupposes frustration**, and frustration leads to some form of aggression;
- when people are **blocked from reaching their goals**, they become **frustrated**, and a **trigger** turns frustration into aggression;
- aggression is aimed at the **cause of the frustration** — or, if that is impossible, **displaced** onto another person or object (Berkowitz).

<FlowDiagram title="Frustration–aggression in the Niger Delta">
<FlowStep title="1. Goals">
A decent life from the region's oil wealth — jobs, clean water, development, a fair share of revenue.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="2. Blocked → frustration">
**Extreme poverty, unemployment, polluted water, environmental degradation and neglect**, despite the huge wealth taken from the area.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="3. Trigger">
Repression, failed promises, arrests of leaders (e.g. Asari Dokubo in 2005).
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="4. Aggression at the source">
Armed attacks on the **state and oil installations** — **pipeline sabotage**, **oil theft**, guerrilla warfare.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="5. Displaced aggression">
**Kidnappings** and criminality affecting people who did not cause the frustration.
</FlowStep>
</FlowDiagram>

### Four categories of federal response

| Category | What was done | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Repression (military response)** | Deployment of the **police and military** — **pro-active or pre-emptive deterrence**, or massive engagement to restore order | Criticised for **militarising** the region and **escalating** youth militancy and crime |
| **2. Preventive peace building — constitutional and institutional approaches** | **Niger Delta Development Board (NDDB)** — provided for in **June 1959** by Order-in-Council, **section 14 of the 1960 (Constitution) Order-in-Council**, and the **NDDB Act 1961**. It **failed**. **Niger Delta Basin Development Authority (NDBDA)** — **Decree 37 of 1976** — to tackle floods, pollution and river-bank erosion and speed up development. It **failed**, because government admitted that the **oil industry** caused the problems but would not interfere with it. **OMPADEC** (Oil Mineral Producing Areas Development Commission) — **Decree 23 of 1992** (Babangida) — **3% of the Federation Account**; an improvement that directly addressed the oil-producing areas. **NDDC** (Niger Delta Development Commission) replaced OMPADEC after 1999 *(its Act is dated 2000; the textbook says 1999)*. Section 7(1) covers the main issues **except 50% derivation and resource control** | The NDDC was weakened by **leadership struggles**, disputed performance, and the perception that its funding was **tokenism** (Briggs 2007) |
| **3. Post-conflict peace building — fiscal redistribution** | **Derivation** fell from **50% (1951)** to **1.5%** under the Buhari regime. It was raised to **3% (June 1992)**; ecological funds went from **1% to 2%** of the Federation Account; from **1991** NNPC and its joint-venture partners had to devote **3% of capital investment** to **host-community development**. Derivation rose to **13%** under the Abacha regime — now **section 162(2) of the 1999 Constitution** | A marked change from oil companies giving "any paltry amount they deemed fit" |
| **4. Preventive diplomacy — amnesty** | **1967:** Gowon pardoned **Isaac Adaka Boro** and the **Niger Delta Volunteer Force** (sentenced to death for their February 1966 insurgency). **October 2004:** Obasanjo's peace accord with **Asari Dokubo (NDPVF)** and **Ateke Tom (NDV)** — a pardon, disarmament and demobilisation, and resettlement with **4,000 jobs** (capacity building). Over **5,000 weapons** were surrendered, but **no resettlement or jobs followed**. Asari was **arrested in September 2005**, and militants regrouped as **MEND** — the most violent phase. **2009:** President **Umar Musa Yar'Adua** (with Vice-President **Goodluck Jonathan**, a Niger Delta indigene) set up a **45-member Technical Committee** to harmonise all reports from the **1957 Willink Commission** onwards. He declared **amnesty on 25 June 2009**, with a **4 October 2009** deadline. About **15,000 militants** surrendered their arms | **Relative peace** followed |

**Is the Niger Delta conflict resolved?** The textbook's answer is **no**:

- Granting the militants' **resource-control** demand would tilt **economic power** towards the region and could **weaken or even disintegrate the Nigerian state**.
- Concern for **Nigeria's survival** has made the conflict **intractable**.
- At best, what is possible is **efficient management of the conflict**, not permanent resolution.

<SortingGame title="Which category of federal response?">

```sort
@buckets Repression | Preventive peace building | Post-conflict peace building | Preventive diplomacy
Troops deployed to the creeks to restore public order by force => Repression :: Military deterrence or engagement — criticised for escalating militancy.
The Oil Mineral Producing Areas Development Commission (OMPADEC), 1992 => Preventive peace building :: A constitutional or institutional approach.
Raising the derivation formula to 13% => Post-conflict peace building :: A fiscal redistributive mechanism.
The 2009 amnesty with a disarmament deadline of 4 October => Preventive diplomacy :: A political soft landing instead of full-scale military assault.
The Niger Delta Development Board created under the 1960 constitutional order => Preventive peace building :: The first institutional approach, dating from 1959.
Requiring NNPC and its partners to spend 3% of capital investment on host communities => Post-conflict peace building :: Redistribution to oil-producing communities.
Gowon's 1967 pardon of Isaac Adaka Boro => Preventive diplomacy :: The first use of amnesty as a tool for peace.
The Niger Delta Development Commission (NDDC) => Preventive peace building :: The institutional successor to OMPADEC.
```

</SortingGame>

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. Trace the origins of peace research and conflict studies and explain how they merged.">
Peace research grew from statesmen's reflection on the cost of the First World War (Wilson, Clemenceau, Lloyd George; about 8 million soldiers dead), which led to the League of Nations (1919), and from Scandinavian universities in the 1950s. Its focus was preventing conflict and maintaining peace. Conflict studies began in North America in the 1950s–60s amid Cold War nuclear fears (the Journal of Conflict Resolution, 1957; the Michigan centre, 1959), focused on settling disputes. By the 1980s its ideas were used in South Africa and the Middle East. After the Cold War, attention turned to internal and ethnic conflicts, and the fields merged. The OSCE and OAU, NGOs (the Carter Center, the Nyerere Foundation), development ministries, the IMF and the World Bank all adopted peace building and conflict resolution.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Define conflict and explain Wallensteen's three types of conflict.">
Coser: conflict is a struggle over values or claims to status, power and scarce resources, aimed at neutralising, injuring or eliminating rivals. It requires two or more parties with incompatible interests and hostile attitudes or actions. The usual issues are resources, power, identity and values. Wallensteen: inter-state conflicts (between nation-states); internal conflicts (civil crises, ethnic wars); and state-formation conflicts (anti-colonial struggles, secession and autonomy movements, territorial disputes, battles for control of government). There are also global conflicts involving non-state groups.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Compare agency and structural theories of the causes of conflict.">
Agency theories (social and psychological) locate causes in human behaviour: innate biological aggression, the psychoanalytic need for enemies (self versus other), and psycho-social group processes — misperception, stereotyping, dehumanisation — which gave rise to the labels ethnic and identity conflict. Structural (political-economy) theories locate causes in how society is organised: Galtung's structural violence (unequal structures, unequal access to resources), and international political economy and war-economy analyses of who benefits from war. Azar's protracted social conflict and Goodhand and Hulme's complex political emergencies balance both.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Distinguish negative from positive peace and state the conditions for positive peace.">
Negative peace is the absence of war or violence, a tense "neither war nor peace" despite antagonistic goals. Positive peace is a political condition of justice and social stability secured by institutions and norms; peace can be defined as harmony, order, justice or conflict management. Conditions (Miller 2005): a balance of political power; legitimate, transparent and accountable decision-makers; valued interdependent relationships; reliable institutions for resolving conflict; equality and respect; and mutual understanding with flexibility.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="5. Explain peace building, peacekeeping, peace enforcement and preventive diplomacy.">
Peace building consolidates peaceful relations and human security so that societies manage conflict without violence (pre-conflict or post-conflict). Peacekeeping operations, under a UN or regional mandate, halt fighting, uphold order, deliver aid and restore institutions. Peace enforcement uses or threatens military force under Chapter VII (e.g. Korea, 1950–53), possibly without the parties' consent, including sanctions, embargoes and strikes. Preventive diplomacy gives skilled assistance — good offices, mediation — to resolve disputes before they turn violent and to contain their spread.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="6. Using the frustration–aggression theory, analyse the Niger Delta conflict and the federal responses to it.">
The Niger Delta, the source of about 80% of national wealth, suffered poverty, unemployment, pollution and neglect — frustration which, once triggered, became aggression (militancy, sabotage, kidnapping) directed at the state and oil installations and displaced onto others. It is a protracted social conflict. Federal responses: (1) repression by military deployment, which escalated the conflict; (2) institutions — the NDDB (1959/61), the NDBDA (1976), OMPADEC (1992, 3%) and the NDDC; (3) fiscal redistribution — derivation from 1.5% to 3% to 13%, ecological funds, and 3% of JV capital for communities; (4) amnesty — Adaka Boro (1967), Asari and Ateke (2004, which collapsed into MEND), and Yar'Adua's 2009 amnesty (about 15,000 disarmed). The conflict is managed rather than resolved, because resource control threatens national cohesion.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Peace research** (prevention and maintaining peace; WWI, the League of Nations 1919, Scandinavia in the 1950s) and **conflict studies** (settling disputes; the Cold War; the *Journal of Conflict Resolution* 1957) **merged after the Cold War**.
- **Conflict** (Coser 1956): a struggle over values, status, power and scarce resources to neutralise, injure or eliminate rivals; it is universal and dynamic.
- **Wallensteen**: **inter-state, internal, state-formation**; plus global conflicts.
- **Causes**: **agency** (innate aggression, need for enemies, misperception) vs **structure** (Galtung's structural violence, political economy); **Azar's PSC** (communal content, deprivation of needs, the state, international linkages); **CPEs** (Goodhand and Hulme).
- **Negative peace** (absence of war) vs **positive peace** (justice, institutions, equality); peace as harmony, order, justice or conflict management.
- **Terms**: peace building, **peacekeeping** (mandate), **peace enforcement** (Chapter VII), preventive diplomacy, early warning, mediation (pure and power; **four stages**), conciliation, reconciliation, reconstruction.
- **Niger Delta**: PSC explained by **frustration–aggression** (Dollard); responses: **repression**, institutions (**NDDB → NDBDA → OMPADEC → NDDC**), **derivation up to 13%**, **amnesty (25 June 2009)** — managed, not resolved.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Link each **concept to its author**: Coser — conflict; Wallensteen — three types; Azar — PSC; Goodhand and Hulme — CPE; Galtung — structural violence and negative/positive peace; Dollard — frustration–aggression.
- Distinguish the **"peace" terms carefully**: *keeping* (mandate), *enforcement* (Chapter VII force), *building* (consolidating peace).
- For the Niger Delta, memorise the **institution sequence with dates** (1959/61, 1976, 1992, NDDC) and the **derivation figures** (50% → 1.5% → 3% → 13%).
- In essays, always end the case study with the textbook's conclusion: **management, not permanent resolution**.
