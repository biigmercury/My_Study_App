## Part 1: The Psychobiological Basis of Behaviour

To understand the **mind**, we must understand the **physical structure responsible for it**. Our experiences, behaviour and even our **sense of personal identity** are products of the **brain**. The **psychobiological basis of behaviour** studies how **the brain and the rest of the nervous system** produce them. In short, **human behaviour comes from the brain**.

Psychologists who study the biology of behaviour ask two kinds of question:

- **Philosophical:** what is the relationship between **mind and brain**? Could a mind exist **without** a brain? If not, what about the brain's structure produces the mind?
- **Practical:** can **biological measurements** predict who will develop **alcoholism, depression or schizophrenia**? How can disorders such as **antisocial personality, insomnia, anxiety attacks and hyperkinetic disorders** be prevented?

**The aim of biological psychology** is to examine the **bodily mechanisms that make behaviour possible**. The goal is to use its findings to **improve the health and well-being** of humans and other animals.

### Four types of psychobiological explanation

| Explanation | Asks… | Example: why do we sweat in the heat? |
|---|---|---|
| **Physiological** | How the **brain and organs** produce the behaviour, down to the **cellular and chemical** level | The hypothalamus detects rising temperature and signals sweat glands |
| **Ontogenetic** | How the structure or behaviour **develops** — starting from the **genes** and tracing how they combine with **environmental** influence | How sweating capacity develops from infancy (partly set by the climate one grows up in) |
| **Evolutionary** | The **evolutionary history** of the species | Humans inherited sweating from ancestors who hunted in hot savannas |
| **Functional** | **Why** the behaviour evolved — what **advantage** it gives *(the textbook names this type without describing it)* | Evaporating sweat **cools the body**, allowing long activity in the heat |

### Forced and enabled behaviour

Psychobiology distinguishes two kinds of factor:

- factors that **force** a behaviour to occur;
- factors that **enable** a behaviour to occur.

Behaviour the body **forces** — controlled by **instinct and reflex** — includes:

- **sweating** when too hot;
- the **pupil constricting** in bright light;
- **palms becoming wet** when anxious;
- the **knee-jerk** when the knee is tapped;
- **more saliva** after tasting unsweetened lemon juice.

All of these are controlled by the **brain and nervous system**.

The body's parts must communicate. **Two systems** do this: the **endocrine system** and the **nervous system**.

## The Endocrine System

The endocrine system consists of **glands that release chemical substances called hormones** into the bloodstream, regulating body functions. **Each hormone affects only cells that have receptor sites for it.**

| Gland | Hormone(s) | Main effects and disorders |
|---|---|---|
| **Pituitary** — the **"master gland"**, because its secretions control many other glands | **Anterior pituitary** (makes six hormones, released under control of **hypothalamic releasing hormones**): **growth hormone (somatotropic)**, **thyrotropic**, **adrenocorticotropic (ACTH)**, **FSH**, **LH**, **prolactin** | Growth hormone promotes **growth throughout the body** — **excess → pituitary giant**; **deficiency → pituitary dwarfism**. The others control the **thyroid**, the **adrenal cortex**, the **gonads** and the **mammary glands** |
| | **Posterior pituitary** (an **extension of the hypothalamus**; releases two hormones): **oxytocin**, **vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone)** | Oxytocin: **uterine contractions in childbirth** and **milk release**. Vasopressin: **constricts blood vessels, raises blood pressure** (and conserves water) |
| **Thyroid** | **Thyroxine / triiodothyronine** | Increase **metabolic rate**, growth and maturation. **Over-secretion → hyperactivity**; **under-secretion → low metabolism** |
| **Parathyroids** | **Parathormone** | **Raises blood calcium** (and lowers phosphate). Under-secretion → low calcium and muscle spasms; over-secretion draws calcium from bone, causing weak bones and **lethargy** *(the textbook's "decrease potassium" should read phosphate)* |
| **Adrenal medulla** | **Epinephrine (adrenaline)** and **norepinephrine** | Prepare the body for **stress and emergencies** — **faster heartbeat, higher blood pressure**, blood diverted from the stomach and intestines |
| **Adrenal cortex** | **Aldosterone, cortisol, corticosterone** | **Sodium** metabolism, **food metabolism**, **sexual maturity** and activity |
| **Pancreas** | **Insulin** (lowers blood sugar) and **glucagon** (raises it) | **Insulin under-secretion → diabetes**; over-secretion → low blood sugar and **lethargy** |
| **Ovaries** (female) | **Estrogen, progesterone** | Female reproductive functions and **secondary sexual characteristics** |
| **Testes** (male) | **Androgens (testosterone)** | **Secondary sexual characteristics** (e.g. facial hair) and **sexual behaviour**; under-secretion → **decreased sexual vigour** |
| **Pineal** | **Melatonin** (the textbook misprints it as "melanin") | Regulates the **sleep–wake cycle**; can **suppress gonadal hormone output** |
| **Thymus** | **Thymosin** and others | Support **immune responses** |

## The Nervous System

The **nervous system** is an organ system containing a network of specialised cells called **neurons** that **coordinate actions and transmit signals** between parts of the body. It has two divisions:

| Central Nervous System (CNS) | Peripheral Nervous System (PNS) |
|---|---|
| The **brain** and the **spinal cord** | **All the nerves outside** the brain and spinal cord |

The **brain is the control centre for all forms of behaviour**. It has the same general structure as other mammals' brains, but is **larger than expected for body size** among primates (Herculano-Houzel 2009).

### Forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain

| Division | Structures | Functions |
|---|---|---|
| **Forebrain** (the largest part in mammals) | The outer **cerebral cortex**, plus **thalamus, basal ganglia and limbic system** beneath it | **(i)** Receives **sensation**; **(ii)** initiates **movement**; **(iii)** **cognitive activity** — thinking, talking, creating, imagining |
| **Midbrain** (surrounded by the forebrain) | **Tectum** (superior and inferior colliculi), **tegmentum**, and the **substantia nigra** — important because of its role in **Parkinson's disease** | **(i)** **Connects** the other two divisions; **(ii)** **alerts the forebrain** to incoming sensations |
| **Hindbrain** (the most posterior part) | **Medulla, pons, cerebellum** | **(i)** **Sleeping and waking**; **(ii)** **coordination of body movement**; **(iii)** **vital reflexes** — heart rate, blood pressure, respiration |

### The cortex and its lobes

The **cortex** is a **thin layer of cells** covering the forebrain's surface. It contains most of the forebrain's neurons.

![Side view of the left hemisphere of the human brain with the frontal lobe in blue at the front, the parietal lobe in yellow on top behind it, the temporal lobe in green along the side, the occipital lobe in pink at the back, and the cerebellum and brain stem below](/images/ges103/brain-lobes.svg)

*Lobes of the cerebral cortex (left side view, front of the head on the left). Base drawing by Mysid after Gray's Anatomy, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons; labels added.*

| Lobe | Location | Functions |
|---|---|---|
| **Frontal** | Front of the brain | **Social-emotional behaviour and personality** (frontal injury may change **personality** and cause **emotional problems**). **Makes and carries out plans**; initiates **all voluntary movement** (injury may cause **paralysis**) |
| **Parietal** | **Directly behind** the frontal lobe | **Body sensations** — touch, pain, temperature from the skin and joints |
| **Temporal** | **Directly below** the parietal lobe | **Hearing and speech** |
| **Occipital** | The **very back** of the brain | **Vision** |

| Left hemisphere | Right hemisphere |
|---|---|
| **Verbal, mathematical and analytic** ability | **Non-verbal** (simple reading, writing and understanding, but **cannot speak**), **spatial** and **holistic** |

### Brain cells: neurons and glia

Nervous tissue has **two main cell types**:

- **Neurons** — the textbook gives **about 100 billion**.
- **Glial cells** — the textbook says **10 to 50 times** as many as neurons.

*(Beyond the textbook: more recent counts give about **86 billion neurons** and roughly the **same number of glial cells**; the old "10 to 50 times" figure is now considered an overestimate.)*

Facts about **neurons**:

- **Dendrites** bring electrical signals **to** the cell body; **axons** carry them **away**.
- They transmit signals over distances from a fraction of an inch to **over 3 feet**, at up to about **200 miles per hour**.
- It was long believed that humans grow **virtually no new neurons after birth**. Princeton researchers found new neurons continually added to **adult monkeys'** brains. *(Beyond the textbook: new neurons also appear to form in the adult human **hippocampus**, though how many is debated.)*

![Diagram of a neuron: branching dendrites around a cell body with its nucleus, an axon covered by myelin sheath with nodes of Ranvier, axon terminals, and an enlarged synapse showing synaptic vesicles releasing neurotransmitter across the synaptic cleft to receptors](/images/ges103/neuron.svg)

*A typical neuron and synapse. Diagram by LadyofHats (Mariana Ruiz Villarreal), public domain, via Wikimedia Commons. Tap to zoom.*

| Part | Structure and function |
|---|---|
| **Dendrites** | **Branch-like extensions** of the cell body; **receive signals** from other neurons, muscles or sense organs |
| **Cell body** | Egg-shaped; provides **fuel**, **manufactures chemicals** and keeps the neuron working. Its **nucleus** holds the **DNA** instructions |
| **Axon** | A single **thread-like** extension that **carries signals away** from the cell body to another neuron, an organ or a muscle fibre |
| **Terminal buttons** | Bulb-like swellings at the **ends of the axon's branches**, with **vesicles** that store **neurotransmitters**. They come very close to — but **do not touch** — the next cell |
| **Synapse** | The **infinitely small gap** between a terminal button and the next neuron, muscle or organ |
| **Neurotransmitters** | **Chemical messengers** released into the synapse. They act on **receptors** of the next cell, either to **excite** or to **inhibit** it |

<FlowDiagram title="How a message travels through a neuron">
<FlowStep title="1. Dendrites">
**Receive** the signal from another neuron, a muscle or a sense organ.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="2. Cell body">
Integrates the incoming signals; the **nucleus** directs the neuron's work.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="3. Axon">
Carries the **electrical impulse away** from the cell body.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="4. Terminal buttons">
The impulse reaches the ends of the axon and causes **vesicles to release neurotransmitters**.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="5. Synapse → next cell">
Neurotransmitters cross the gap and bind to **receptors** on the next **neuron, muscle fibre or organ**, **exciting or inhibiting** it.
</FlowStep>
</FlowDiagram>

**Glial cells** ("supporting cells") produce no action potentials, but they:

- provide **scaffolding** that guides the growth of developing neurons;
- **wrap around mature neurons** as **insulation** against interfering signals;
- release chemicals that **influence neuron growth and function**.

**Integration:** behaviour, from simple to complex, depends on many processes working together. The **nervous system** provides this integration, **with the help of the endocrine system**.

## Part 2: Measurement in Psychology

**Psychological measurement** is **the use of specified testing procedures to evaluate the abilities, behaviours and personal qualities of people**. It describes how individuals **differ from or resemble** others — e.g. how many more questions they answer than others of their age.

**Thorndike and Hagen (1986):** measurement is *"the process of **quantifying observations** (or descriptions) of a quality or attribute of a thing or person."* It involves **three steps**:

1. **Identify and define** the quality or attribute to be measured.
2. Determine **operations** that make the attribute **manifest and perceivable**.
3. Establish **procedures for translating observations into quantitative statements** of degree or amount.

### Variables

A **variable** is **anything that varies and can be measured**. For example:

- the **sex** of GES 103 students (male or female);
- **socio-economic status** (high, medium, low).

So measurement can simply mean **categorisation**.

| Independent variable (IV) | Dependent variable (DV) |
|---|---|
| The variable the experimenter **purposely manipulates** — the **reason** for the research. It "stands alone" | Changes **as a function of** the IV — its value **depends on** the IV. It is the **effect** in the cause–effect relation |
| Examples: **price**, deprivation, **temperature**, **noise level**, removal of part of the brain, psychological context | In "the effect of price on consumer buying behaviour", **quantity purchased** |

**Participants** are assigned to one of two groups (Shenge and Icheke 2005):

- the **experimental group** — exposed to the **IV**;
- the **control group** — exposed to **all conditions except the IV**.

### Two major types of measurement

| Score (numerical) measurement | Nominal (category) measurement |
|---|---|
| A **numerical value** is assigned — most physical and psychological measures; the values are called **scores** | Deciding **which category** a case belongs to; categories are **named** in words |
| **Table 10.1 — GES 103 scores:** Ayo **60**, Olabisi **55**, Mojisola **65**, Banke **45**, Dare **56** | **Table 10.2 — occupations of 50 employees:** lorry drivers **15**, security officers **30**, cashiers **5** |
| Each number is **one measurement** of one person | The numbers are **frequencies** (counts) — an **aggregate** of many measurements, **not scores** |

### The four scales of measurement

There are **four "theoretical" scales of measurement** in psychology:

| Scale | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Nominal** | Placing cases into **named categories**. It is the **lowest** form of assigning numbers (Balogun 2005) — the numbers are only **labels** | Sex, religion, occupation. *(The textbook's example, socio-economic status coded 1 = high, 2 = medium, 3 = low, is really ordered — an ordinal example.)* |
| **2. Ordinal** | Scores can be put **in order** from smallest to largest — **rank** measurement (1st, 2nd, 3rd…), with no fixed distance between ranks | Class position, Likert ratings |
| **3. Interval** | Has the qualities of nominal and ordinal scales **plus equal distances**: numerically equal intervals represent equal differences in the property. It is the **most widely used** in scientific research | With 4, 6, 8, 10, the gap from 4 to 6 equals the gap from 8 to 10; temperature in °C; IQ scores |
| **4. Ratio** | Like interval, **plus an absolute zero** | **Distance, weight** — zero on a tape measure means **no distance at all** |

## Research Methods in Psychology

### The observational method

**Systematic observation is the starting point of any science.** We all watch how people eat, dress and associate. Psychologists do the same, but **more objectively and systematically**, in three ways:

| Technique | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Case study** | An **individual studied in great depth and detail**; causes of behaviour are **inferred** by rules of logic. Often used in **clinical settings** to form hypotheses | **Paul Broca** (1824–1880) studied a patient nicknamed **"Tan"**, who could say little but "tan". Broca hypothesised that the speech-production centre lay in the **left frontal lobe** and was damaged — the **autopsy proved him right** (now called **Broca's area**) |
| **Survey** | People are asked specific questions about their **behaviour, opinions, attitudes, feelings, expectations or knowledge**, by **interview** (face-to-face) or **questionnaire** (structured or unstructured, open or closed) | Establishing how strong a reaction is in a population, how widespread a problem is, or what the public's key issues are |
| **Naturalistic observation** | Observing **naturally occurring behaviour** without changing or interfering with it | Watching primary school children play from behind a **one-way glass**; useful early in an investigation to find the **important variables** |

### The correlational method

**Survey research** is a form of the broader **correlational method**.

- It does **not manipulate** independent variables.
- It assesses **relationships among naturally occurring variables** to find **predictive relationships**.
- These predictions are used, for example, in **diagnosing disorders** and **selecting job applicants**.

A **correlation** exists when two measures of the same people or things **vary together** (**covary**). The **correlation coefficient** shows its **direction** and **strength**:

| Positive correlation | Negative correlation |
|---|---|
| As one measure **increases**, the other **increases** | As one measure **increases**, the other **decreases** |
| The longer a person has **smoked**, the greater the likelihood of **lung disease** | High school seniors who spent **more time watching TV** answered **fewer** achievement-test questions correctly (**Keith, Reimers et al. 1986**) |

*(Beyond the textbook, but vital: **correlation does not prove causation** — that is why psychologists turn to experiments.)*

### The experimental method

Because naturalistic observation cannot establish **cause**, psychologists run **controlled experiments**:

- observations are made under **systematically varied conditions**;
- the investigator **manipulates** the stimulus variable (the **IV**) and observes the effect on behaviour (the **DV**);
- participants are assigned to **experimental** and **control** groups.

Experiments are used to **test hypotheses** and establish **cause–effect relationships**. Whatever the method, the collected **data** must then be **processed and analysed statistically**.

## Why Measure — and What Makes a Good Measure?

**Measurement helps the psychologist to:**

1. **Predict** individual behaviour with **minimum error and maximum accuracy**.
2. **Make sense of individual differences** — how traits combine into a unique **personality**. By testing and classifying people with similar traits, behaviour can be linked to **personality types**.
3. **Learn how people develop** — at what age children acquire which skills and attitudes, and how important **sex, race and intelligence** are in predicting behaviour.

**Instruments** include:

- **interviews**;
- **psychological tests, scales and inventories** measuring intelligence, personality and creativity;
- **self-reports** (self-inventories);
- **judges' ratings**.

**Every instrument must be:**

| Requirement | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Reliable** | **Consistently** gives the **same results** over and over again |
| **Valid** | Measures **what it is intended to measure** — a valid intelligence test tells us how intelligent people are and **predicts performance** where intelligence matters |
| **Standardised** | **Administered to everyone in the same way under the same conditions**, with **norms** (statistical standards) so that an individual's score can be **compared** with a defined group |

Otherwise the **trustworthiness of a person's score** is in doubt.

<Analogy>
A **reliable** bathroom scale shows 60 kg every time you step on it. A **valid** one shows your *true* weight — a scale that always reads 5 kg too low is reliable but not valid. A **standardised** one is used the same way for everyone (shoes off, morning), so your reading can be compared with the norms for your age and height.
</Analogy>

**Well-known standardised instruments:**

| Test | Measures | Origin |
|---|---|---|
| **Stanford–Binet Intelligence Scale** | **Intelligence** | Lewis Terman's 1916 revision of Binet's test; the textbook cites the **1960** revision |
| **Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI)** | **Personality** | **Starke Hathaway and J. C. McKinley**, University of Minnesota. The textbook says 1930; it was **published in 1943** |
| **Rorschach inkblot test** | **Personality** | Swiss psychiatrist **Hermann Rorschach** — published **1921** (the textbook says 1924) |
| **Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)** | **Personality** | American psychologist **Henry Murray** (with Christiana Morgan). The textbook says 1938; it was **introduced in 1935** |

## Using the Data: Statistics

Observations must be **organised and interpreted** to answer research questions and test hypotheses. There are two families of technique:

| Descriptive statistics | Inferential statistics |
|---|---|
| Process raw data so it can **describe** the phenomenon of interest | Use raw data and **rules of logic** to infer how subjects would behave — assessing the **probability** of sample results under given population conditions (e.g. for **policy decisions**) |

The **descriptive** techniques are:

- **Frequency distribution** — how often each score occurs. The first step is **ranking** scores by magnitude.
- **Central tendency** — the **mode** (most frequent score), **median** (the central score) and **mean** (the "average"). Used to compare groups by their **typical** scores.
- **Variability** — how spread out the scores are, and how representative the average is:
  - the **range** (highest minus lowest);
  - the **standard deviation** (the average distance of scores from the mean).
- **Correlation** — the **correlation coefficient**, the degree of relationship between two variables.

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. Name the four types of psychobiological explanation and distinguish forced from enabled behaviour.">
Physiological (how the brain and organs produce behaviour, down to cells and chemicals), ontogenetic (how behaviour develops from genes interacting with the environment), evolutionary (the species' evolutionary history) and functional (why the behaviour evolved — its advantage). Forced behaviours are those the body makes happen — instinctive reflexes such as sweating in heat, pupil constriction in bright light, the knee-jerk, and salivating at lemon juice — all controlled by the brain and nervous system. Enabling factors make behaviour possible without compelling it.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Describe five endocrine glands, their hormones and effects.">
Pituitary (master gland): growth hormone (gigantism if in excess, dwarfism if deficient), hormones controlling the thyroid, adrenals and gonads, and prolactin; posterior oxytocin (childbirth, milk) and vasopressin (blood pressure). Thyroid: thyroxine (metabolic rate — hyperactivity or low metabolism). Parathyroid: parathormone (blood calcium). Adrenal: medulla epinephrine and norepinephrine (emergency responses), cortex aldosterone and cortisol (sodium and food metabolism). Pancreas: insulin and glucagon (blood sugar; diabetes). Gonads: estrogen, progesterone and testosterone (sexual characteristics). Pineal: melatonin. Thymus: thymosin (immunity).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Describe the divisions of the brain and the functions of the four cortical lobes.">
Forebrain (cortex, thalamus, basal ganglia, limbic system): sensation, movement and cognition. Midbrain (tectum, tegmentum, substantia nigra): connects the divisions and alerts the forebrain to sensations. Hindbrain (medulla, pons, cerebellum): sleep and waking, coordination, vital reflexes. Lobes: frontal (personality, social-emotional behaviour, planning, voluntary movement), parietal (touch, pain, temperature), temporal (hearing and speech), occipital (vision). The left hemisphere is verbal and analytic; the right is spatial and holistic.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Explain the structure of a neuron and how a message crosses a synapse.">
Dendrites receive signals; the cell body (with the DNA-containing nucleus) fuels and maintains the neuron; the axon carries the impulse away; terminal buttons at the axon's ends store neurotransmitters in vesicles. When the impulse arrives, the vesicles release neurotransmitters into the synapse (the tiny gap). They bind to receptors on the next neuron, muscle or organ, exciting or inhibiting it. Glial cells guide growth, insulate neurons and release growth-influencing chemicals.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="5. Define measurement and explain the four scales of measurement with examples.">
Measurement is the process of quantifying observations of a quality or attribute (Thorndike and Hagen 1986): define the attribute, make it observable, and translate observations into amounts. Nominal: named categories, numbers as labels (sex, religion). Ordinal: rank order without equal gaps (positions in a race). Interval: equal distances and no true zero (°C, IQ) — the most widely used. Ratio: equal distances plus an absolute zero (weight, distance).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="6. Compare the observational, correlational and experimental methods.">
Observational methods — case study (e.g. Broca's patient "Tan"), survey (interviews, questionnaires) and naturalistic observation (a one-way glass) — describe behaviour. The correlational method measures how naturally occurring variables covary (smoking and lung disease positively; TV and achievement negatively) to make predictions, but cannot show cause. The experimental method manipulates the independent variable, compares experimental and control groups, and establishes cause–effect relationships by testing hypotheses.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="7. What are reliability, validity and standardisation? Name four standardised tests.">
Reliability: consistent results on repeated use. Validity: measuring what the test is meant to measure and predicting relevant performance. Standardisation: uniform administration and scoring, with norms for comparison. Tests: the Stanford–Binet (intelligence), the MMPI (personality, Hathaway and McKinley), the Rorschach inkblot test (personality, Hermann Rorschach) and the Thematic Apperception Test (personality, Henry Murray).
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Behaviour comes from the brain**; four explanations: **physiological, ontogenetic, evolutionary, functional**; behaviour may be **forced** (reflexes) or **enabled**.
- **Endocrine system**: glands → **hormones** acting on cells with receptors. **Pituitary = master gland** (growth hormone: giant/dwarf; oxytocin; vasopressin); thyroid (metabolism); parathyroid (calcium); **adrenal** (epinephrine — emergencies); pancreas (**insulin — diabetes**); gonads; pineal (melatonin); thymus.
- **Nervous system**: **CNS** (brain + spinal cord) and **PNS**. **Forebrain** (sensation, movement, cognition), **midbrain** (connections; substantia nigra — Parkinson's), **hindbrain** (sleep, coordination, vital reflexes).
- **Lobes**: frontal (personality, planning, movement), parietal (touch), temporal (hearing, speech), occipital (vision). Left = verbal and analytic; right = spatial and holistic.
- **Neuron**: dendrites → cell body → axon → terminal buttons → **neurotransmitter** across the **synapse**; **glia** support and insulate.
- **Measurement** (Thorndike and Hagen): quantifying observations; **IV** (manipulated) vs **DV** (depends on it); experimental vs control group.
- Score vs category measurement; **four scales**: nominal, ordinal, interval, **ratio (true zero)**.
- Methods: **case study** (Broca's "Tan"), **survey**, **naturalistic observation**, **correlational** (positive/negative; not causal), **experimental** (cause–effect).
- Good tests are **reliable, valid and standardised**: Stanford–Binet, MMPI, Rorschach, TAT. Statistics: **descriptive** vs **inferential**.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- **Hormone → gland → disorder** chains are favourites: growth hormone → pituitary → giant/dwarf; insulin → pancreas → diabetes.
- Memorise **lobe → function** and **brain division → structures**.
- The **order of a neural message** (dendrite → cell body → axon → terminal button → synapse) is a common MCQ.
- Know the **four scales in order**, with one example each, and remember that **only ratio has a true zero**.
- In "IV or DV" questions, ask: *which one does the researcher change?* That is the IV.
