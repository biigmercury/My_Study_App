## Nigeria at Independence

Nigeria gained **independence from Britain in 1960** and became a **republic in 1963**. It is endowed with vast, largely untapped natural resources:

- **crude oil** and **natural gas**;
- **columbite**, **gold and silver**, **iron ore**;
- **marble**, **lead**, **zinc**, **tin**, **limestone**.

Yet at independence the economy showed classic **features of underdevelopment**:

- **low productivity growth** in most activities;
- **low per capita income** and a poor standard of living;
- a **poor state of social and economic infrastructure**;
- a dominant **primary-product (agriculture)** sector.

The drive for growth led to:

- **four national development plans** (1962–1985);
- the **Structural Adjustment Programme (SAP)** in 1986, after persistent underdevelopment and crisis *(the textbook says June 1986 here and July 1986 later; SAP was launched in **July 1986**)*;
- **three-year rolling plans** within a perspective plan in the 1990s.

These plans aimed at five goals (Anyanwu et al. 1997):

- a rise in the **real income** of the average citizen, with **more equitable distribution**;
- **less unemployment and underemployment**;
- more **Nigerian ownership and management** of enterprises;
- more **reliance on local resources**;
- **economic stability**.

### Five decades in brief

| Period | What characterised it |
|---|---|
| **1960s** | **Political crises**, military intrusion and the **civil war** (1967–1970), with adverse economic consequences |
| **1970s** | Military rule, the **oil boom**, more public involvement in the economy, **invasive economic controls** |
| **1980s** | The **oil glut**, a protracted **economic crisis**, the return of the military and the adoption of **SAP** |
| **1990s** | **Intensified economic liberalism** under SAP; **rolling plans** |
| **1999 onwards** | Return of democracy; a move away from conventional development plans to a **new paradigm** (NEEDS and after) |

## Trends in Economic Growth, 1960–2015

**Kuznets (1971)** defines economic growth as *"the **long-term rise in capacity to supply increasingly diverse economic goods** to its population, … based on **advancing technology** and the **institutional and ideological adjustment** that it demands."* A sustained higher standard of living must rest on a sustained rise in **productive capacity and real output per capita**.

- The size of an economy is measured by **GDP, GNP or GNI**.
- The **GDP growth rate** is the conventional measure of how fast an economy grows.
- The **main sectors** are **agriculture, oil and mining, manufacturing, and services**.

**What the numbers show:**

| Indicator | Story |
|---|---|
| **Real GDP** | Highest average growth **12.6% (2001–05)**; lowest **−1% (1981–85)**, caused by **austerity measures** after the **oil glut**. The good growth of the first two decades came from the **oil boom**, which caused an explosion in **public spending** — post-war reconstruction and big salary increases (the **Udoji awards**) |
| **Agriculture** | **No appreciable growth 1960–1980**; its all-time low of **−1.5% (1976–80)** was caused by the **Dutch disease**. Attention returned with the oil glut: **3.6% and 7.0%** in the two halves of the 1980s, a boom of **18.1% (2001–05)**, then **6.51%** and **4.11%** |
| **Inflation** | Rose to **18.1% (1976–80)**; the 1980s were a period of **recession**; inflation **peaked at 42% (1991–95)** and fell to single digits, **9.72% (2011–15)** |
| **External sector** | Grew strongly in the **1990s** (exports and imports growing by as much as **75–83%**), but fell by **3.96%** in 2011–15, its lowest since 1986. **Imports grew faster than exports** — a negative balance of trade — hence the drive to **diversify** away from oil towards agriculture and manufacturing |

<Analogy>
**Dutch disease** is named after the Netherlands, whose manufacturing declined after it discovered natural gas in the 1960s. It is like a family whose eldest child suddenly earns big money: everyone rushes to help him and the family farm is left to weeds. When the oil boom came, labour, capital and government attention flowed to oil, and **agriculture declined** — even though it employed most Nigerians.
</Analogy>

### Exchange rate, reserves and debt

- **Exchange rate:** the naira was **stronger than the dollar until 1985**. Under SAP it depreciated:
  - to about **₦4.5 per US\$1** in the late 1980s;
  - to **₦125.8** in 2005–08;
  - to about **₦192.44** officially at the end of 2015.
- **External reserves:**
  - rose from **US\$1.9 billion** in the late 1970s to **US\$43.7 billion** in 2005–08;
  - then fell by an average of **−1.17%** a year in 2011–15.
- **External debt** reached about **US\$32 billion** in 2000–04, then fell to about **US\$6.6 billion** in 2005–08 when more than half was written off.
  - The textbook credits the **HIPC** initiative. Strictly, Nigeria's 2005 relief came from the **Paris Club** of creditors, which wrote off about **US\$18 billion**.
  - The textbook's "US\$290b" for 2015 is a misprint: external debt was about **US\$10.7 billion** at the end of 2015.

## Sectoral Analysis

### Agriculture

Agriculture employs about **65% of the labour force**, and the **rural sector** is predominantly agricultural. Its policy objectives are:

- **self-sufficiency in food and fibre**;
- better **socio-economic welfare** of the people;
- lower **food inflation**;
- **diversified foreign-exchange earnings** through agricultural exports;
- **raw materials** for local agro-based industry.

**Government interventions:**

| Era | Interventions |
|---|---|
| **1950s–60s** | **Agricultural development corporations** and **farm settlement schemes** — to stabilise farmers' incomes, give access to land and modernise peasant farming |
| **"Preferred sector" policies** | **Concessionary interest rates** (at most **6%** in the 1970s and **9%** in the 1980s), specialised agricultural banks, **commodity boards**, input supply and **subsidies**, land use, research, **extension**, mechanisation, **irrigation** |
| **1976** | **Operation Feed the Nation (OFN)** |
| **1980** | **Green Revolution** (replaced OFN) |
| **Institutions** | **Nigerian Agricultural Co-operative Bank (NACB, 1973)**, **Agricultural Credit Guarantee Scheme (ACGS, 1977)**, commodity marketing boards, **River Basin Development Authorities**, **National Directorate of Employment (NDE)**, **DFRRI** (Directorate of Foods, Roads and Rural Infrastructure), **National Agricultural Land Development Authority (NALDA)**, **Family Economic Advancement Programme (FEAP)** |

Despite all this, agriculture **cannot feed the population** — Nigeria still spends **billions of naira importing rice**.

### Industry

The industrial sector comprises **manufacturing**, **oil, quarrying and mining**, and **electricity**.

| Subsector | Situation |
|---|---|
| **Manufacturing** | Consistently **under 10% of output**, with **capacity utilisation of about 50%**. Poor performance is due to **dependence on imported inputs**, **low product quality** and **undiversified exports** |
| **Oil, quarrying and mining** | Since the 1970s boom, oil provides the **largest share of exports, foreign earnings and federally collected revenue**. The textbook gives proven reserves of **32 billion barrels** (Nigeria is **6th largest producer in OPEC**) — about **35 years** at current rates. This leaves Nigeria at the mercy of the **international oil market**. **Nigeria LNG** harnesses associated gas for export (e.g. to Europe). **Solid minerals** (coal, tin, kaolin, gypsum, columbite, gold, barites, marble, tantalite, salt, sulphur) remain **undeveloped** |
| **Electricity** | One of the **major infrastructure problems**. The body in charge was renamed, but inefficiency persisted: **ECN → NEPA → PHCN**, then **unbundled** for private participation. The textbook says generation capacity was raised to "over 20,000 MW". *(Beyond the textbook: installed capacity is roughly 13,000 MW, power actually delivered has seldom exceeded about 5,000 MW, and the successor companies were **privatised in 2013**.)* |

**Federal core industrial projects:**

| Project | Details | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Refineries** | **Four**, government-owned: **Warri**, **Kaduna** and **two in Port Harcourt**. About **20 private licences** issued since 2000 | Nigeria still **imports more than two-thirds** of its petroleum products, hence the (debated) **fuel subsidy**. *(Beyond the textbook: the private **Dangote refinery** began production in **2024**.)* |
| **Fertiliser plants** | **Federal Super-Phosphate Fertiliser Company (FSFC)** and **National Fertiliser Company of Nigeria (NAFCON)** | Depend on **imported inputs**; scarce foreign exchange → **shortages every planting season** |
| **Steel** | Steel companies at **Itakpe, Ajaokuta and Aladja**; rolling mills at **Oshogbo, Jos and Katsina** | A **colossal failure** — currently **non-active**, despite deals with foreign firms |
| **Pulp and paper** | **Nigeria Paper Mill, Jebba**; **Nigerian Newsprint Manufacturing Company**; **Iwopin Pulp and Paper Company** | **Inadequate funding**, forex constraints, **high operating costs**, **obsolete equipment** |

**Summary:** **agriculture and services** are the largest contributors to output; **manufacturing is the least**.

## Planning for Development

Nigeria's planning framework evolved in three stages:

1. **medium-term development plans** from independence to the mid-1980s;
2. **rolling plans** in the 1990s;
3. **NEEDS, the Seven-Point Agenda, the Transformation Agenda and the ERGP** after 1999.

| Plan | Objectives and features | Why it fell short |
|---|---|---|
| **First National Development Plan (1962–1968)** | Accelerate **growth** and raise **living standards**; reduce dependence on **foreign capital and manpower**; priority to **agriculture and industry**. Projects: **Port Harcourt refinery**, **Kainji Dam**, **Nigerian Security Printing and Minting** | **Insufficient resources** (up to **50%** was to come from foreign allies and fell short); **inadequate manpower** (foreign advisers); **political problems** — federal–regional wrangling climaxing in the **civil war** *(the textbook misprints the war as "the last half of the 1970s"; it was **1967–1970**)* |
| **Second NDP (1970–1974)** | Drawn up after the war to **restructure** the economy. **Five national objectives**: a **united, strong and self-reliant nation**; a **great and dynamic economy**; a **just and egalitarian society**; a **land of bright and full opportunities**; a **free and democratic society**. First **oil windfalls**; the **naira replaced the British pound**; the **indigenisation decree**; new bodies such as the **River Basin Authorities, Industrial Training Fund, National Bank for Agriculture, Standards Organisation of Nigeria** and the **Nigerian National Oil Corporation** | **Inadequate capacity to execute** the plan |
| **Third NDP (1975–1980)** | Drafted amid the **oil boom**. Added **short-term goals**: higher per capita income, more even income distribution, less unemployment, more high-level manpower, **diversification**, **balanced development**, **indigenisation** | **Overstated government capacity** and a **negative oil shock**. Much was not executed, and **external borrowing** began the **rising debt profile**. Diversification and balance are **still not achieved** |
| **Fourth NDP (1981–1985)** | **Growth and development, price stability, social equity**; priorities: agriculture, education, manpower, infrastructure, housing, health | The **early-1980s oil glut**, the **high cost of running a democratic government**, large-scale **corruption**, **inflation**, **debt crises**, incoherent **fiscal and monetary policies** |

### The Structural Adjustment Programme (SAP), 1986–1993

By the mid-1980s Nigeria faced:

- deep **recession** and **structural imbalance**;
- **fiscal and exchange-rate crises**;
- **balance-of-payments** and **external debt** problems.

SAP was introduced in **July 1986**. It was **meant to last two years (1986–88)** but remained in force **until 1993**.

| Objectives | Policies |
|---|---|
| **Restructure and diversify** the productive base to cut dependence on **oil and imports** | **Realistic exchange rate** (devaluation) |
| Achieve **fiscal and balance-of-payments viability** | **Deregulation** and greater reliance on **market forces** |
| Lay a basis for **non-inflationary (or minimally inflationary) growth** | **Trade liberalisation** |
| Reduce **unproductive public investment**, improve efficiency, and boost **private-sector growth** | **Removal of subsidies**; **privatisation and commercialisation** of public enterprises; **smaller public sector**; strong **demand management** |

**Exchange-rate and trade reforms** were the lead policies. Deregulating the financial sector multiplied the number of **financial institutions**. SAP was **the most controversial policy package**: it brought **great hardship**, and **riots and protests** across the country.

### Rolling plans (1990s)

Medium-term plans were blamed for the failure to achieve sustainable development, so Nigeria shifted from **five-year plans to three-year rolling plans**.

- **1990–92 (the first rolling plan):**
  - objectives: **self-sufficiency in food and raw materials**; a foundation for **self-reliant industrial development**; **employment**; socio-political awareness; a stronger **market-oriented economy**; and relief for the groups hit hardest by adjustment;
  - weaknesses: it relied heavily on **deficit financing** and was a **package of uncompleted projects** without integrated goals.
- **1993–96 plan — the first under the National Planning Commission** (Decree 12 of 1992):
  - it addressed the **depreciating naira**, **inflation**, **sluggish manufacturing** and **debt**;
  - it **excluded local government estimates** and coincided with **political crises**, strikes and civil disturbances.

Rolling plans were guided by **deregulation** and the need for **rapid recovery**.

### Lessons of the development plans

- **Government was the main agent** of development — it held the **"commanding heights"** of the economy, producing and distributing through **state-owned enterprises**.
- The **private sector** was recognised on paper but given **little or no role**.
- In reality, more than **70% of the labour force** works in the **unorganised (informal) sector**. It drives the rural economy and supplies **cheap labour** to the cities.

## Development Initiatives since 1999

<Tabs>
<Tab label="NEEDS (2004)">
The **National Economic Empowerment and Development Strategy** focuses on **poverty reduction** through **economic growth, human development** and **changing the way business is done**. It was a **paradigm shift** in planning:

- it plays down **projects** and focuses on the **policy environment**;
- **measurable targets** and specific outputs, a **bottom-up** approach, **participatory**, with targets **keyed into the MDGs**;
- state and local counterparts: **SEEDS** (State) and **LEEDS** (Local Government) — no sustainable development without state and local governments.

**Basic needs targeted:** housing, clothing, health care, education, water, security, jobs, sanitation, nutrition, empowerment and good governance.

**Financing:** more **efficient use of resources**, **domestic savings**, **FDI**, **official development assistance** and **debt relief**.

**Outcomes:** **rising FDI inflows** and successful **debt relief and payoff**.
</Tab>
<Tab label="Seven-Point Agenda (2007)">
Released on **1 August 2007** (the Yar'Adua administration):

1. **Power and Energy** — adequate power so Nigeria can industrialise by **2015**
2. **Food Security and Agriculture** — modern technology and research for a **5–10-fold increase in yields**
3. **Wealth Creation and Employment** — **diversification** away from oil into agriculture and solid minerals
4. **Mass Transportation** — rehabilitate and **modernise the railways**
5. **Land Reform** — release land for **commercial farming** and large-scale private business
6. **Security** — a necessary infrastructure for investment
7. **Qualitative and Functional Education** — international minimum standards and excellence in **science and technology**

Its **short lifespan** meant little impact.
</Tab>
<Tab label="Transformation Agenda (2011–15)">
The Jonathan administration's plan. It aimed to fix the **absence of a long-term perspective** and the lack of the **"3Cs" — continuity, consistency and commitment** to agreed policies.

**Projected investment: ₦40.75 trillion**:

- **₦24.45 trillion (60%) from the public sector**;
- **₦16.30 trillion (40%) from the private sector**.

**Five priority policies:**

1. **Harmony between fiscal and monetary policy**
2. Sound macroeconomic policy — **single-digit inflation**
3. A clearer **budget process** between executive and legislature
4. A review of the **revenue allocation formula** for balanced fiscal federalism
5. **Institutionalising development planning**, with capital budgets tied to medium- and long-term plans

**The problem of discontinuity:** there is **no party ideology** in Nigeria's politics. The **same party produced three presidents** with **three unrelated programmes**, so programmes are frequently jettisoned.
</Tab>
<Tab label="ERGP (2017–20)">
The **Economic Recovery and Growth Plan** (Buhari/Osinbajo) was a **medium-term plan** to restore growth after the recession. Government moves from being an **"omnibus provider"** to **removing bottlenecks** for innovation and market solutions. It leverages **Science, Technology and Innovation (STI)** and is consistent with the **SDGs**.

**Three broad objectives:**

- **Restoring growth** — **macroeconomic stability** (fiscal stimulus, monetary stability, better external balance) and **diversification**: agriculture, energy, **MSME-led** industry and services.
- **Investing in our people**:
  - **social inclusion** — especially the **North East** and **Niger Delta**;
  - **job creation and youth empowerment** — **N-Power**, public works, **Made-in-Nigeria**, local content;
  - **human capital** — health (national rollout of the **NHIS**) and education.
- **Building a globally competitive economy**:
  - **infrastructure** (power, roads, rail, ports, broadband; **PPPs**);
  - the **business environment** (**PEBEC**; target: **top 100** in the World Bank's **Doing Business** index by 2020, up from **169**);
  - **digital-led growth** (Smart Nigeria Digital Economy Project, ICT clusters, broadband).

**Key executive priorities:**

- **macroeconomic stability**;
- **agriculture and food security**;
- **energy sufficiency** (power and petroleum products);
- **transport infrastructure**;
- **industrialisation through SMEs**.
</Tab>
</Tabs>

The programmes are good, but **poor implementation and planning attitudes** have denied the country their dividends.

## Poverty and Human Development

**Poverty** is the **lack of resources necessary to ensure access to a set of basic needs**. It covers not just **income** but also **access to health, education and other basic services**. There are **three approaches to measuring poverty**:

| Approach | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Relative poverty** | Defined by reference to the **living standards of the majority**. Households with per capita expenditure **above two-thirds of the mean** are **non-poor**; those **below** are **poor**. The poor are further split into the **core (extreme) poor** and the **moderate poor** |
| **Absolute poverty** (the **food energy intake** measure) | Poverty as the inability to afford **minimal standards of food, clothing, health care and shelter**, based on a minimum food basket of about **3,000 calories a day**. The World Bank's **purchasing power parity (PPP)** line counted as poor those living on **less than US\$1 a day** *(beyond the textbook: updated to US\$1.90 in 2015, US\$2.15 in 2022 and US\$3.00 in 2025)* |
| **Subjective poverty** | Based on **self-assessment** — whether respondents **consider themselves poor** |

The share of Nigerians in relative poverty rose from **less than 28% in 1980 to 69% in 2010** — an increase of about **150%**. By 2010 the **extreme poor (38.7%)** outnumbered the moderate poor. Poverty is closely linked with **growth and development**: Nigeria grew fast in 2001–10, yet poverty rose. This is a textbook case of **growth without development**.

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. Describe the state of the Nigerian economy at independence and the trends in growth from 1960 to 2015.">
At independence Nigeria had vast untapped resources (oil, gas, tin, columbite, iron ore and more) but underdevelopment: low productivity, low per capita income, poor infrastructure and dominant primary agriculture. The oil boom drove growth in the first two decades (public spending, Udoji awards) but caused the Dutch disease (agriculture fell by 1.5% in 1976–80). The 1980s oil glut brought austerity and negative growth (−1% in 1981–85), recession and inflation. Inflation peaked at 42% in 1991–95 and fell to 9.72% by 2011–15. Real GDP growth peaked at 12.6% in 2001–05. Imports outgrew exports, the naira depreciated from above parity to about ₦192/US\$ by 2015, and debt rose and was partly written off in 2005.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Discuss the changing structure of the Nigerian economy using sectoral shares of GDP.">
Agriculture contributed over a third of real GDP (37–41%) until 2001–05, then fell to about 23–25%, when services (32–36%) became the largest sector. Oil's share peaked at 28.6% in 2001–05 and fell to about 12%. Wholesale and retail trade held about 12–17%. Manufacturing fell from 9.17% to 3.91% before recovering to 8.87%, and building and construction stayed at 2–4%. The economy remains dominated by primary production and services, with weak manufacturing — a structural imbalance with implications for development.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Explain the objectives and failures of Nigeria's four national development plans.">
First (1962–68): accelerate growth and living standards, less foreign dependence; failed through insufficient (foreign) resources, foreign manpower and political crises and the civil war. Second (1970–74): post-war restructuring with five national objectives; oil windfalls, the naira and indigenisation; failed through inadequate capacity to execute. Third (1975–80): oil-boom plan adding per capita income, distribution, employment, diversification and balanced development; failed through overstated government capacity and an oil shock, leading to borrowing. Fourth (1981–85): growth, price stability and social equity; failed through the oil glut, the cost of democratic government, corruption, inflation, debt and incoherent policies.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. What were the objectives and policies of SAP, and what were its consequences?">
Objectives: restructure and diversify the economy away from oil and imports; fiscal and balance-of-payments viability; non-inflationary growth; reduce unproductive public investment and boost the private sector. Policies: a realistic exchange rate, deregulation and market forces, trade liberalisation, removal of subsidies, privatisation and commercialisation, a smaller public sector, and demand management. Consequences: more financial institutions and privatised enterprises, but a depreciated naira, great hardship, and riots and protests. Meant to last from 1986 to 1988, it lasted until 1993.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="5. Compare NEEDS, the Seven-Point Agenda, the Transformation Agenda and the ERGP.">
NEEDS (2004): poverty reduction through growth, human development and changing the way business is done; policy rather than projects; measurable targets keyed to the MDGs; SEEDS and LEEDS; it brought FDI and debt relief. Seven-Point Agenda (2007): power, food security, wealth creation, transport, land reform, security and education; short-lived. Transformation Agenda (2011–15): fixing the 3Cs, ₦40.75tn investment (60% public), five fiscal and planning priorities. ERGP (2017–20): restoring growth, investing in people (N-Power, NHIS), global competitiveness (infrastructure, Doing Business, digital economy). A common weakness: discontinuity and poor implementation.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="6. Define poverty and explain the three approaches to measuring it, with the trend in Nigeria.">
Poverty is the lack of resources to access basic needs — income, health, education and services. Relative poverty: households below two-thirds of mean per capita expenditure, split into core and moderate poor. Absolute poverty: inability to afford a minimum basket (food energy intake of about 3,000 calories; under US\$1 a day at PPP). Subjective poverty: self-assessment. In Nigeria relative poverty rose from 27.2% in 1980 to 69% in 2010, with extreme poverty reaching 38.7% — growth without development.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- At independence (1960; republic 1963) Nigeria was **resource-rich but underdeveloped** — dominant agriculture, low productivity and income, poor infrastructure.
- **Growth**: oil boom (1970s) → **Dutch disease** (agriculture −1.5% in 1976–80) → oil glut (−1% in 1981–85) → inflation peak **42% (1991–95)** → growth peak **12.6% (2001–05)**; naira from above parity to about ₦192/US\$.
- **Structure**: agriculture led (about 40%) until **services** took over (about 35%); **manufacturing is weakest** (under 10%, about 50% capacity utilisation).
- Interventions: **OFN (1976)**, **Green Revolution (1980)**, NACB, ACGS, RBDAs, DFRRI; core projects (refineries, fertiliser, **Ajaokuta** steel, paper mills) largely failed.
- **Plans**: 1st (1962–68), 2nd (1970–74, five objectives), 3rd (1975–80), 4th (1981–85) → **SAP (1986–93)** → **rolling plans** (NPC from 1992) → **NEEDS** (SEEDS/LEEDS) → **Seven-Point Agenda (2007)** → **Transformation Agenda (3Cs)** → **ERGP (2017–20)**.
- **Poverty**: relative, absolute (US\$1/day), subjective; **27.2% (1980) → 69% (2010)**.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Memorise the **plan dates** and one **distinguishing feature** of each (e.g. 2nd NDP = five national objectives; SAP = realistic exchange rate and deregulation).
- Know the **period extremes** in Table 8.1: GDP high 12.6% (2001–05), low −1% (1981–85); inflation high 42% (1991–95).
- Be able to **define and distinguish the three poverty measures**.
- For "why did the plans fail?" essays, group the reasons: **finance** (oil shocks, foreign funding), **capacity** (manpower, execution), **politics** (crises, discontinuity, corruption).
