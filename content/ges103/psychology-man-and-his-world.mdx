## What Psychology Is — and Is Not

In this chapter, "man" and "his" are used in a **generic sense**, meaning all human beings. **Psychologists** are people **trained to observe, analyse and evaluate behaviour**. Psychology has a **wide scope**. It asks questions such as:

- why some people **act or fail to act** in a particular way;
- why some people **conform** and others do not;
- why we **forget or remember**;
- why people **behave differently in the same situation**;
- why employees engage in **counterproductive work behaviour**.

Behaviour — of humans and animals — can be:

| Overt (manifest) | Covert (concealed) |
|---|---|
| **Observable** actions and responses | **Hidden, internal** events |
| Eating, talking, sneezing, gambling, basket weaving | **Thinking, remembering**, dreaming |

**Branches** include:

- industrial/organisational psychology;
- social and personality psychology;
- developmental psychology;
- clinical psychology;
- counselling psychology.

The psychologist most people know is the **clinical psychologist**, who helps depressed, worried or troubled people. But that is **only one aspect** of psychology.

**A common misconception:** taking an introductory psychology course will **not** enable you to "analyse people", discover the hidden parts of their personality, or control them. It will help you **understand certain aspects of behaviour**. It should also make you **sceptical** of anyone who claims to understand a person's personality from a **small sample of their behaviour**.

**Psychology is both a science and a profession** (Halpern 2003):

- some psychologists are **scientists** who do research to create new knowledge;
- some are **teachers** who pass knowledge on;
- others **apply** psychology to problems in mental health, education, business, sports, law and medicine.

## Defining Psychology

**Etymology:** from two Greek words:

- ***psyche*** — **mind or soul**;
- ***logos*** — **study** (or "reading").

So psychology was **originally the study of the mind or soul**. Its definition has changed as its **focus** changed:

| Era | Psychology defined as… |
|---|---|
| Earliest | The study of the **psyche** — the **mind** or **soul** |
| Also | The study of the **spirit** |
| 19th century | The study of **consciousness**. **William James** (1890s): *"the description and explanation of **states of consciousness** as such"* (a definition he borrowed from G. T. Ladd) *(beyond the textbook)* |
| Early 20th century | The **science of behaviour** — **John B. Watson** and the **behaviourists** (1913), who studied only what can be **observed** *(beyond the textbook)* |
| Today | **The scientific study of behaviour and mental processes** (of humans and animals) |

The modern definition includes **mental processes**, but psychologists study them **through behaviour**, because **the mind cannot be studied directly**.

**The goals of psychology**, like those of every science, are to **describe, explain, predict and control** behaviour.

### What contemporary psychologists do

Another way to define modern psychology is to look at **what psychologists actually do**:

- seek the **biological correlates** of mental processes such as sensation, perception and ideation;
- study the principles of **learning and memory**;
- understand humans by **studying non-human animals**;
- study **unconscious motivations**;
- improve **industrial productivity, education and child-rearing**;
- explain behaviour through **evolutionary theory**;
- account for **individual differences** in personality, intelligence and creativity;
- create **therapeutic tools** for people with **mental disturbances**;
- study **adjustment and problem-solving** strategies;
- study how **language** develops and relates to culture;
- use **computer programmes as models** of human thought;
- study how people **change across the lifespan** through **maturation and experience**.

## Origins: A Long Past but a Short History

Psychology has a **long past but a short history**:

- its **questions are ancient**;
- as a **science** it dates only from **1879**.

| When | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **5th–6th centuries BC** | **Greek philosophers** decided that people's lives were governed not so much by the gods as by **their own minds** — **people are rational**. They construed the world through perceptions (hot/cold, wet/dry, hard/soft). They did not study behaviour **systematically**, but their reliance on **observation** laid a scientific foundation. *(Beyond the textbook: **Socrates and Plato** stressed **reasoning** as the path to knowledge, while **Aristotle** stressed **observation**.)* |
| **Mid-1500s** | **Nicolaus Copernicus** (1473–1543): the **Earth revolves around the Sun**, not the centre of the universe |
| **1564–1642** | **Galileo Galilei** used a **telescope** to confirm Copernicus. During the Renaissance, **modern experimentation** was refined through observation |
| **17th century** | Philosophers popularised **dualism** — **mind and body are separate**. **René Descartes** (1596–1650) proposed a **link**: the mind controls the body's **movements, sensations and perceptions**, and mind and body **influence each other**. How they interact is still debated |
| **19th century** | **Hilgard (1987):** modern science combined philosophers' reflection, logic and mathematics with practical observation. **Cells** were recognised as life's building blocks, chemists built the **periodic table**, and physicists studied **atomic forces** — scientists **reduced complex phenomena to simpler parts**. Psychology was born in this environment |
| **1879** | **Wilhelm Wundt**, the **"father of psychology"**, set up the **first psychology laboratory** in **Leipzig, Germany**, to study **conscious experience** — how we form **sensations, images and feelings** |

**Wundt's method:** he measured responses to **stimuli** — lights, sounds, weights. A **stimulus** (plural **stimuli**) is **any physical energy that evokes a sensory response**.

- He also used **introspection** — **"looking inward"** to probe one's own reactions. *(If you stop reading now and carefully examine your thoughts, feelings and sensations, you are introspecting.)*
- He called his approach **experimental self-observation**, because it combined **trained introspection** with **objective measurement** (Lieberman 1979).
- The method had many limitations, but it gave psychology a good start by stressing **careful observation and measurement**.

## Three Philosophical Issues

Psychology grew out of **philosophy**. It now uses different methods of enquiry, but it still wrestles with three old questions:

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Free will vs determinism">
**Free will:** the most important causes of behaviour come **from the self** — behaviour is **freely chosen**, independent of physical or psychic causes.

**Determinism:** **everything** in the observable world **has a cause**. Determinism **shapes the work of scientists**.

- Some psychologists hold that free will is an **illusion** (**Wegner 2002**): what you call a conscious intention is **more a prediction than a cause** of your behaviour.
- Others argue that we **do** make decisions — something **within** us initiates action — yet **laws of cause and effect** still guide behaviour.
</Tab>
<Tab label="Mind–body (mind–brain)">
How can **physical, chemical and electrical** activity in the brain produce intricate **mental activity** such as feeling, thinking and learning? (Hergenhahn 2009)

| Dualism | Monism |
|---|---|
| The **mind is distinct from the brain** but somehow **controls** the brain and body | **Mind and brain are the same**, inseparable from each other |
</Tab>
<Tab label="Nature vs nurture">
How do:

- **nature** — **heredity**, genetic factors — and
- **nurture** — **environment**, experience and learning —

interact in the development of a person's **intellectual, emotional, physical and social** abilities?

Some psychologists stress **genetics**; others stress **experience and learning**. Separating them is very difficult. **Behaviour typically develops from the interaction of both.**
</Tab>
</Tabs>

The attempt to resolve these issues led the founders to develop **scientific psychology**, which grew into the **schools of thought** below.

<SortingGame title="Which position does this statement take?">

```sort
@buckets Free will | Determinism | Dualism | Monism | Nature | Nurture
"I chose to study tonight — nothing made me do it." => Free will :: Behaviour is freely chosen by the self.
"Every action has a prior cause, even if we cannot see it." => Determinism :: Everything in the observable world has a cause.
"The mind is separate from the brain but directs it." => Dualism :: Mind and brain are distinct entities.
"The mind is simply what the brain does." => Monism :: Mind and brain are one and the same.
"She is musical because both her parents are musical — it is in her genes." => Nature :: Heredity explains the ability.
"He speaks Yoruba fluently because he grew up in Ibadan." => Nurture :: Environment and learning explain the behaviour.
```

</SortingGame>

## Early Approaches (Schools of Thought)

As psychology grew, **competing schools** offered new approaches to the science of behaviour:

| School | Founder(s) | Key ideas | Criticism / legacy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Structuralism** | **Wilhelm Wundt** (1832–1920), in Leipzig, **1879**; named and taken to the USA by **Edward B. Titchener** (1867–1927) | Studied the **structure of mental life** — the **basic elements** (sensations and perceptions) of conscious experience. A perception is the **sum of its parts**, understood by breaking it into smaller elements. Method: **introspection** — subjects look inward and report sensations and perceptions | Introspectors gave **conflicting reports**, so it was criticised as **narrow and subjective**. This led to **functionalism**. Wundt is credited with establishing psychology as a **separate, formal field** |
| **Functionalism** | **William James** (1842–1910) — taught the **first psychology class at Harvard (1875)**; the **"father of psychology" in the USA**; wrote the first textbook, ***The Principles of Psychology* (1890)**, after **12 years** | Mental activities (thinking, feeling, learning, remembering) serve **one major function: to help us survive as a species**. It focused on the **functions and purposes** of consciousness and behaviour — how people and animals **adapt to their environments** | James was not interested in experiments, but his writings **remain influential** |
| **Gestalt** | **Max Wertheimer** (1880–1943), **Wolfgang Köhler** (1887–1967), **Kurt Koffka** (1886–1941), all German | *Gestalt* = **"form, pattern or whole"**. They studied thinking, learning and perception **as whole units**, rejecting analysis into parts (against structuralism and behaviourism) | Slogan: **"The whole is greater than the sum of its parts"** |

![The Kanizsa triangle: three notched discs and three corner marks, which the eye completes into a bright white triangle that is not actually drawn](/images/ges103/kanizsa-triangle.svg)

*The Kanizsa triangle (drawn for this lesson). There is no white triangle in the picture — only three notched discs and three angles. Your perception builds a **whole** that is more than its parts, just as the Gestalt psychologists argued. Structuralists would struggle to explain it by adding up the elements.*

## Contemporary Approaches

Many ideas from the early schools live on in today's six main approaches:

| Approach | Focus | Key figure / idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Psychoanalytic** | The **unconscious mind**. Conscious experience is only the **"tip of the iceberg"**; beneath lie **primitive biological urges** in conflict with society and morality. These unconscious motives and conflicts explain most behaviour — even **medically unexplained physical symptoms** | **Sigmund Freud** (1856–1939); method: **free association**. Still **powerful and controversial** |
| **Behavioural** | Behaviour as **relationships between observable stimuli and observable responses**. Studies only what can be **observed and measured**, ignoring unobservable mental processes — **actions, not thoughts** | *(Beyond the textbook: John B. Watson, B. F. Skinner)* |
| **Humanistic** | A **reaction to behaviourism**. Each person has great **freedom to direct his or her future**, a large capacity for **personal growth**, considerable **inner worth** and enormous potential for **self-fulfilment** | *(Beyond the textbook: Abraham Maslow, Carl Rogers)* |
| **Cognitive** | How we **process, store and use information**, and how that shapes what we **attend to, perceive, learn, remember, believe and feel** | Mental processes as information processing |
| **Biological** | The impact of **biology** on behaviour — the **brain, nervous system, hormones and genetics**. Its practitioners are **psychobiologists**; genetic factors influence a wide range of behaviours | See the next lesson |
| **Sociocultural** | **The newest approach**: how **cultural and ethnic similarities and differences** influence behaviour and social functioning | — |

<SortingGame title="Which school or approach?">

```sort
@buckets Structuralism | Functionalism | Gestalt | Psychoanalytic | Behavioural | Humanistic | Cognitive | Biological | Sociocultural
Trained observers introspect and report the basic elements of a sensation => Structuralism :: Wundt and Titchener broke experience into its elements.
Asks how remembering helps a species survive and adapt => Functionalism :: William James focused on the functions of the mind.
"The whole is greater than the sum of its parts" => Gestalt :: Wertheimer, Köhler and Koffka studied perception as wholes.
A patient's slips of the tongue are traced to unconscious conflicts through free association => Psychoanalytic :: Freud's unconscious mind.
Studies only how a rat's lever-pressing changes with rewards — no talk of thoughts => Behavioural :: Observable stimuli and responses only.
Stresses every person's inner worth and potential for self-fulfilment => Humanistic :: A reaction against behaviourism.
Studies how information is encoded, stored and retrieved in memory => Cognitive :: Processing, storing and using information.
Examines how hormones and genes influence aggression => Biological :: Psychobiologists study the brain, hormones and genetics.
Compares how Yoruba and Hausa children express respect to elders => Sociocultural :: Cultural and ethnic similarities and differences.
```

</SortingGame>

## Subfields of Psychology

Psychology divides broadly into:

- **pure (basic) psychology** — research that develops **theories and models**;
- **applied psychology** — applying psychological principles to **practical problems**.

| Subfield | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Clinical** | **Diagnosis and treatment of mental disorders** and personal problems; in private offices, mental hospitals, prisons and clinics |
| **Counselling** | Advises and assists people with the **problems of everyday life** — helping them **adjust**; often in schools or firms |
| **School** | Identifies children's **educational needs**, devises a plan to meet them, then implements it or advises teachers |
| **Educational** | Topics related to **teaching** children and young adults — **intelligence, memory, problem solving, motivation**. Evaluates teaching methods, **devises tests**, develops instructional devices |
| **Industrial/Organisational (I/O)** | **People at work**: matching the **right person to the right job**, work teams, salaries and bonuses, **performance feedback**, organisational structure, productivity and satisfaction |
| **Social** | How our **thoughts, feelings and actions are influenced by other people** — even by *imagining* how they would react |
| **Developmental** | **Physical, emotional, cognitive and social changes** throughout life — from children to the elderly, and even the **process of dying** |
| **Forensic** | **Crime and crime prevention**, rehabilitation, prisons, **courtroom dynamics**, selecting **police** candidates |
| **Cross-cultural** | **Compares behaviour across cultures** — like social psychology, but comparing one culture with another |
| **Environmental** | Effects of the **environment** on people — **natural disasters, overcrowding, pollution** |
| **Experimental** | **Sensation, perception, learning, human performance, motivation and emotion**, studied in **controlled laboratory** conditions with animal and human subjects |
| **Health** | How behaviour can **enhance health, prevent illness** and aid **recovery** |

<SortingGame title="Which psychologist would you consult?">

```sort
@buckets Clinical | Counselling | Educational | Industrial/Organisational | Forensic | Developmental | Environmental
A man hears voices and needs diagnosis and treatment => Clinical :: Diagnosis and treatment of mental disorders.
A 200-level student struggles to manage stress and choose a career path => Counselling :: Help with everyday problems of adjustment.
A school wants a fair test of pupils' problem-solving ability => Educational :: Educational psychologists devise tests and evaluate teaching.
A bank wants to select the right people for its customer-service jobs => Industrial/Organisational :: Matching the right person to the right job.
A court needs an expert on the reliability of eyewitness testimony => Forensic :: Courtroom dynamics and crime.
Researchers track how children's thinking changes from age 2 to 12 => Developmental :: Change across the lifespan.
A study of how overcrowding in a Lagos slum affects residents' stress => Environmental :: Effects of environment — crowding, pollution, disasters.
```

</SortingGame>

## Psychology's Contributions

**Shenge and Adu (2017)** point to psychology's demonstrated relevance:

- **Military:** the work of **Hovland, Lumsdaine and Sheffield (1949)** helped train pilots to **overcome fear** and return to combat.
- **Psychological testing and assessment** — pioneered by figures such as **Kasschau and Lewin** — is of tremendous benefit today.
- **Teaching and learning:** psychology tells educators **what to teach learners of different ages**.
- **The world of work** has become **more humanised** because of psychologists.
- **Health and well-being:** **health psychology** studies how behaviour can **enhance health, prevent illness** and aid **recovery**.

**Conclusion:** psychology is broad — **both a science and a profession**. It has come a long way since the days of **"studying bumps on skulls"** (phrenology). Its concerns go back to the **Greek philosophers**. Attempts to resolve its philosophical issues produced **scientific psychology**, then **competing schools**, then many **pure and applied subfields**.

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. Define psychology and trace how its definition has changed.">
From Greek psyche (mind or soul) and logos (study), psychology was first the study of the mind or soul; later of the spirit and of consciousness (William James: the description and explanation of states of consciousness); then the science of behaviour (the behaviourists); and today the scientific study of behaviour and mental processes, because the mind cannot be studied directly. Behaviour may be overt (observable, like talking) or covert (internal, like thinking). Its goals are to describe, explain, predict and control behaviour.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Explain why psychology is said to have 'a long past but a short history'.">
Its questions are ancient: the Greeks (5th–6th centuries BC) saw people as rational and relied on observation; Copernicus and Galileo advanced observation and experiment; Descartes proposed that mind and body interact; and 19th-century science reduced complex phenomena to simple parts. But as a science psychology began only in 1879, when Wilhelm Wundt set up the first laboratory in Leipzig to study conscious experience using measurement of stimuli and trained introspection (experimental self-observation).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Discuss the three philosophical issues in psychology.">
Free will versus determinism: is behaviour freely chosen by the self, or caused (Wegner: free will is an illusion)? Mind–body: is the mind separate from the brain but controlling it (dualism), or the same as the brain (monism)? Nature versus nurture: how do heredity and environment interact in developing intellectual, emotional, physical and social abilities? Behaviour usually results from both.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Compare structuralism, functionalism and the Gestalt school.">
Structuralism (Wundt, Titchener) analysed conscious experience into its basic elements through introspection — a perception is the sum of its parts; it was criticised as subjective and narrow. Functionalism (William James, Harvard, The Principles of Psychology, 1890) asked what the mind does — how mental processes help us adapt and survive. Gestalt (Wertheimer, Köhler, Koffka) studied perception and thinking as wholes: "the whole is greater than the sum of its parts".
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="5. Describe the six contemporary approaches to psychology.">
Psychoanalytic (Freud): unconscious urges and conflicts drive behaviour; free association. Behavioural: observable stimulus–response relations only. Humanistic: freedom, growth, inner worth and self-fulfilment — a reaction to behaviourism. Cognitive: how information is processed, stored and used. Biological: the brain, nervous system, hormones and genes (psychobiologists). Sociocultural (the newest): the influence of cultural and ethnic similarities and differences.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="6. Distinguish pure from applied psychology and describe five subfields.">
Pure (basic) psychology develops theories and models through research; applied psychology uses psychological principles to solve practical problems. Subfields: clinical (diagnosing and treating mental disorders), counselling (everyday adjustment problems), educational (teaching, intelligence, tests), industrial/organisational (people at work — selection, teams, pay, feedback), social (the influence of others), developmental (lifespan change), forensic (crime, courts, police selection), cross-cultural, environmental, experimental and health psychology.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Psychology** = *psyche* (mind/soul) + *logos* (study); today **the scientific study of behaviour and mental processes**. Behaviour is **overt** or **covert**; goals: **describe, explain, predict, control**.
- It is **both a science and a profession** — it will not let you "read" or control people.
- **Long past, short history**: Greeks → Copernicus, Galileo → **Descartes** (mind–body interact) → **Wundt, Leipzig, 1879** (introspection plus measurement = experimental self-observation).
- **Three issues**: free will vs **determinism**; **dualism** vs **monism**; **nature** vs **nurture**.
- **Early schools**: **structuralism** (Wundt, Titchener; elements; introspection), **functionalism** (William James; survival and adaptation; Harvard 1875; *Principles* 1890), **Gestalt** (Wertheimer, Köhler, Koffka; the whole is greater than its parts).
- **Contemporary**: psychoanalytic (**Freud**, unconscious, free association), behavioural, humanistic, cognitive, biological, **sociocultural (newest)**.
- **Subfields**: pure vs applied; clinical, counselling, school, educational, **I/O**, social, developmental, forensic, cross-cultural, environmental, experimental, health.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Know **who** did **what** and **where**: Wundt (Leipzig 1879), James (Harvard 1875, *Principles* 1890), Titchener (took structuralism to the USA), Freud (free association).
- **Structuralism vs Gestalt** is a classic contrast: sum of parts vs whole.
- Learn the **three philosophical issues** with their paired terms (**dualism/monism**, **nature/nurture**, **free will/determinism**).
- Be able to match a **problem to a subfield** — e.g. staff selection is **I/O**; eyewitness testimony is **forensic**.
