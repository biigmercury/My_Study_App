## What Is a Resource?

A **resource** is **any part of the environment** — land, water, air, minerals, forests, wildlife — **created through natural processes** that has the **potential to create wealth or satisfy human needs**. It is **the stock that can be drawn from nature to support life**.

- How a society **uses** its resources depends on its **culture, level of technology and economic system**.
- Rising demand for goods and services has **depleted resources** and caused **environmental problems**.
- **Today's use determines tomorrow's availability.**

Awareness of the links between **resource use, environmental problems, poverty and fairness between generations** led the world to adopt **sustainable development** as a main principle of environmental and development policy. The idea comes mostly from the **1987 Brundtland Report**.

## Classifying Resources

| Criterion | Classes |
|---|---|
| **Abundance and availability** | **Renewable (flow) resources** can **replenish themselves** within a reasonable time through recycling, reproduction and replacement — **water, forests, air, solar energy** — so they are effectively **inexhaustible**. **Non-renewable (stock) resources** exist in **finite quantities** and **cannot be renewed** as fast as they are used — **fossil fuels (coal, petroleum) and minerals** |
| **Origin** | **Biotic** — living, or derived from plants and animals, obtained from the **biosphere** (forests, animals). **Abiotic** — **non-living**, inorganic (air, land, water, minerals) |
| **Geographic concentration** | **Diffuse** resources cover **large areas** (forests). **Point** resources are **highly concentrated** with no significant areal extent on a map (**mineral deposits**) |
| **Stage of development** | **Potential** — known to exist and may be used in future (e.g. **petroleum that may lie under Lake Chad's sedimentary rocks**). **Actual** — surveyed, quantity and quality known, **in use now** (e.g. **Niger Delta** oil and gas). A **reserve** is the part of a resource that **can be extracted with available technology**. A **stock resource** is the part that **cannot be developed profitably for lack of technology** |

**de Zeeuw (2001)** subdivides resources by how long renewal or recycling takes:

| Class | Example |
|---|---|
| **(a) Non-renewable and non-recyclable** | **Fossil fuels** |
| **(b) Non-renewable but recyclable** | **Minerals** (e.g. metals) |
| **(c) Quickly renewable** | **Fish** |
| **(d) Slowly renewable** | **Forests** |
| **(e) Environmental renewable** | **Air, water, soil** |
| **(f) Flow or continuous** | **Sunlight, wind** — not reduced by continuous use |

**Flow and environmental resources are not depleted** — but environmental resources can be **degraded by pollution** and rendered useless.

### Resource and resistance

An element of the physical environment can be a **resource** or a **resistance**. A **resistance** is **a socio-cultural or physical obstacle that hinders the use of a resource** to satisfy human needs:

- **culture**;
- the **technology** available to exploit and process the resource;
- **physical barriers**.

**Culture and resources:** culture is **the total way of life of a people**. It consists of:

- **beliefs** — religious, political;
- **institutions** — legal, educational, governmental;
- **technology** — equipment and skills.

Traditions, religious beliefs and **taboos** shape how people **perceive** their environment. The level of **technology** governs how resources are **found, exploited and processed**. Societies with **low technology** have limited ability to use resources or modify their environment compared with advanced societies.

<Analogy>
Crude oil lay under the Niger Delta for millions of years. It became a **resource** only when the **technology** to find, drill and refine it arrived. Before that, it was just part of the rocks. In the same way, a **taboo** against eating a certain fish turns a food source into a "resistance" for that community.
</Analogy>

## Types of Natural Resources

| Resource | Key points |
|---|---|
| **Minerals** | Elements, compounds, minerals or rocks **concentrated enough to be extracted** for a useful product. **Finite and non-renewable**, and **unevenly distributed**. There are **92 naturally occurring elements**, but most of the crust is **eight**: **oxygen, silicon, aluminium, iron, calcium, sodium, magnesium, potassium**. The **deep ocean floor** holds **manganese nodules** (manganese, iron, copper, nickel, cobalt). **Metallic** minerals (copper, lead, tin, aluminium, iron) occur in **ores** with impurities. **Non-metallic** minerals include nitrate, sulphur, slate, building stone, graphite, quartz, **coal and petroleum**. Minerals are discovered, mined, transported, refined and manufactured into goods |
| **Energy** | **Energy consumption is an index of economic development.** **Renewable:** solar, wind, **hydro-electricity**, tides and waves, and **biomass** (crops, plants, animal waste burnt directly or converted). **Biogas** — mainly **methane** — comes from **anaerobic micro-organisms** digesting domestic and farm waste. **Non-renewable:** **fossil fuels** (crude oil, natural gas, coal, oil shales, tar sands) and **nuclear** energy (uranium, thorium). **Geothermal** energy (heat from the Earth's interior in volcanic areas) generates electricity in Russia (the textbook says USSR), New Zealand, Iceland, Mexico and California *(and, in Africa, **Kenya** — beyond the textbook)* |
| **Forests** | Cover about **32% of the world's land** — about **2.5 billion ha of closed forest** and **1.2 billion ha of open woodland and savanna**. They supply **wood** (pulp, building materials, fuelwood) and **non-wood products** (dyes, fibres, gums, latex, oils, fruits, nuts). **Ecosystem services:** controlling **runoff**, regulating **climate**, **protecting soil**, **filtering water** and **storing carbon (carbon sequestration)**. Demand for land for housing, urbanisation and cattle ranching destroys them |
| **Water** | **Essential for life** — it dissolves and distributes nutrients, regulates body temperature, supports structures and removes wastes. It covers over **70% of the surface**. The textbook gives a total of more than **1,404 million km³** here (the previous lesson quoted 1,386 million — estimates differ): **97% oceans, 2% ice, 1%** mostly **groundwater**. The **global supply is fixed and finite**. Shortages arise from **natural** causes (low rainfall, hot winds, rivers changing course) and **human** causes (rising demand, urbanisation, **overgrazing**, poor farming practices, **inadequate sewage** contaminating supplies). **Over-pumping** dries wells, springs and wetlands |
| **Biodiversity** | The **variety of life** — micro-organisms, plants, animals and ecosystems. Levels: **genetic** (variety of genes within a species), **species** (number of kinds in an ecosystem), **ecological/ecosystem** (richness and complexity of communities — niches, trophic levels, processes), and **molecular**. Diversity **increases from the poles to the tropics** — **tropical moist forests hold about 50% of the world's species**, while North America and Europe hold only 10–15% |
| **Soil** | A **medium for plant growth**. To the geographer, soil is **"a living body formed by the interaction of certain forces and processes"** (Faniran and Ojo 1980), continually formed from below as the top is eroded. It is made of rock and mineral particles, water, air, organisms and their remains. **Formation is extremely slow** — thousands of years for a few centimetres. It is technically renewable but **effectively irreplaceable**. Of **over 13 billion ha** of land, **less than half** can be farmed or grazed, and only about **1.4 billion ha** suits crops — the rest is too wet, dry, shallow, rocky, toxic, frozen or infertile |

**Functions of biodiversity** (Rana 2007):

1. **Food** for humans and animals.
2. **Livelihoods** — wood, fuelwood, ornamental plants and industrial materials (fibre, oil, dyes, rubber, timber, paper).
3. **Traditional medicine** — plants and extracts used directly.
4. **Modern medicine** — plant chemicals; antiviral and heart-active substances from marine fauna; hormones and enzymes from domestic animals; **antibiotics** from fungi and microbes.
5. **Recreation** — bird watching, fishing, trekking.
6. **Indirect benefits:**
   - **carbon fixation** and **pollination**;
   - the **water cycle**, groundwater recharge and **watershed protection**;
   - buffering floods and drought;
   - **soil formation**, nutrient cycles and **decomposing pollutants**;
   - **climate regulation**;
   - aesthetic, cultural, scientific and ethical value.

## Characteristics and Depletion of Resources

**Characteristics of resources:**

1. They **satisfy human needs** through goods and services.
2. They are **scarce**. Scarcity can be:
   - **physical** (limited availability);
   - **geopolitical** (trade barriers);
   - **economic** (supply-chain, distribution or market problems);
   - a matter of **quality**.
3. They can be **depleted** — **exhaustion of raw materials** in a region. Even **renewable** resources are depleted when **extraction exceeds the renewal rate**.

Resources also provide a **sink function**: they **absorb and recycle wastes**. Growing demand plus better technology has stressed the environment, causing:

- rising **greenhouse gases**;
- **deforestation**;
- **biodiversity loss**;
- **land degradation**;
- **water and air pollution**.

These show up as **climate change**, **ozone depletion**, **sea-level rise**, **food shortages and famine**.

### Soil degradation and erosion

Soil is degraded by:

- **accelerated erosion** by water and wind, and changes in soil structure;
- **salinisation** from waterlogging;
- **chemical and industrial pollution**;
- **nutrient loss** (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, organic matter) faster than replacement;
- **non-farm uses** — flooding for reservoirs, **paving** for highways, airports and car parks.

**Erosion is the major form** of soil degradation — the washing or blowing away of surface soil, sometimes down to bedrock.

- **Natural erosion** is slow and balanced by soil formation.
- Removing vegetation for cultivation, grazing, burning or bulldozing **accelerates** it, especially on **steep and long slopes**.
- Rates range from **0.001 t/ha/year** on flat, vegetated land to **1–5 t/ha/year** in mountainous regions.
- About **75 billion tonnes of fertile soil** are lost from world agriculture each year (Pimentel and Burgess 2013).

**Effects of erosion:**

1. **Lower crop yields** and higher cultivation costs.
2. Less **water-holding capacity** — crops suffer **water stress**.
3. **Loss of plant nutrients** (topsoil is richest), so **fertiliser** is needed.
4. **Water pollution and siltation** — sediment fills drains, ditches, reservoirs and rivers, which must be **dredged** at high cost.

### Deforestation

**Deforestation** is the **permanent destruction of indigenous forests and woodlands**. It is a cause of **climate change and biodiversity loss**. World forest cover fell from **4,128 to 3,999 million hectares** between **1990 and 2015** (FAO 2015).

- **Causes:** timber harvesting, conversion to **agriculture**, **road building**, felling for **firewood** and building material.
- **Consequences:**
  - lost species;
  - **carbon released as CO₂** when trees are burnt;
  - a **drier climate** — trees return groundwater to the air;
  - bare soil, **flooding**, **silting** of rivers and dams;
  - poorer water quality;
  - **desertification**.

**The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN)** reports:

- about **one-third of known species** are threatened with extinction;
- **75% of the genetic diversity of crops** has been lost;
- **75% of world fisheries** are fully or over-exploited;
- **70% of species** would be at risk if global temperature rose by **more than 3.5 °C**;
- **one-third of reef-building corals** are threatened;
- over **350 million people** suffer **severe water scarcity**.

**Who is losing?** Biodiversity **rose 10%** in **high-income** countries, **fell 18%** in **middle-income** countries and **fell 58%** in **low-income** countries. Yet high-income countries **consume more than nature can replenish**: their **per capita ecological footprint** exceeds the available **biocapacity** — the ecosystem's ability to produce useful materials and absorb CO₂.

**Causes of biodiversity loss:**

- **habitat destruction** — deforestation, overpopulation, pollution, warming. Large species and forest and ocean dwellers are most affected; **freshwater ecosystems are the most threatened**;
- **over-exploitation** — over-fishing, hunting, logging, illegal wildlife trade;
- **invasive species** — species that natural barriers used to keep out. When humans remove the barriers, they invade and **destroy native species**;
- **genetic pollution** — uncontrolled **hybridisation** and **gene swamping**, when abundant species interbreed with rare ones;
- **climate change** — **warming oceans destroy coral reefs**. Ocean life also suffers from **de-oxygenation and acidification**.

Freshwater ecosystems — ponds, lakes, rivers and wetlands — cover about **15% of continental surface**. They are damaged by:

- **dams, barrages and dykes**, which block fish migration and sediment movement;
- land-use change and drainage;
- pollution;
- floods and droughts.

### Climate change

Climate varies naturally, e.g. with **orbital variations**. But the **Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC)** concluded in **1995** that climate had **changed significantly over the past century, mainly due to human activities**.

- **Projection:** a **1–3.5 °C** rise in global mean temperature by **2100** if trends continued.
- **Greenhouse gases:** **carbon dioxide, methane, chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), nitrous oxide**. Their sources include:
  - **fossil fuels** and **cement production**;
  - **burning biomass** and **landfills**;
  - **pipeline leaks** and **soil denitrification**.

  These gases **absorb infrared radiation** and warm the atmosphere.
- **Effects:**
  - more **floods and droughts**;
  - **habitat and biodiversity loss**;
  - **water and sanitation scarcity**;
  - **falling food production** in the tropics;
  - **rising sea levels** and storm damage on coasts;
  - the spread of **insect-borne diseases**;
  - **heat stress** and weakened immune systems;
  - damage to **coral reefs**.

*(Beyond the textbook: the **Paris Agreement (2015)** aims to keep warming **well below 2 °C**, and ideally **1.5 °C**, above pre-industrial levels.)*

## Sustainability

**Sustainability** is the process of **improving the quality of human life within the limits of the global environment**. It improves welfare **without degrading the environment** or harming others.

The **Brundtland Commission's report *Our Common Future* (1987)** gives the classic definition: meeting **"the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs."** Sustainability rests on three ideas (Mensah and Castro 2004):

1. **living within the limits** of the Earth's capacity to maintain life;
2. understanding the **interconnections** among **economy, society and environment**;
3. a **fair distribution** of resources and opportunities for **present and future generations**.

| Perspective | Sustainability means… |
|---|---|
| **Environmental** | Maintaining rates of **renewable harvest, pollution and non-renewable depletion** that **can continue indefinitely** (Melville 2010) |
| **Economic** | Supporting a defined level of **economic production indefinitely** (Anand and Sen 2000) |
| **Social** | A social system functioning at a defined level of **social well-being indefinitely** (Hutchins and Sutherland 2008) |

![A Venn diagram of three overlapping circles labelled Environment, Society and Economy; the overlaps are labelled Bearable (environment and society), Viable (environment and economy) and Equitable (society and economy), and the centre where all three meet is labelled Sustainable](/images/ges103/sustainability-pillars.svg)

*The three pillars of sustainability (drawn for this lesson). **Munasinghe (1993)** lists the elements of each pillar:*

- *economic — **growth, efficiency, stability**;*
- *social — **poverty, equity, participation, empowerment, cultural heritage**;*
- *environmental — **biodiversity and resilience, natural resources, pollution**.*

*Linking them are **intra-generational equity**, **inter-generational equity** and targeted employment. In business the idea is the **triple bottom line**.*

## Measuring Sustainability

Measuring sustainable development must integrate three areas:

- **technical and economic** activity (jobs, goods and services for a growing population);
- the **ecological footprint** (protecting resources);
- **socially acceptable** decisions (environmental justice, political realities, community participation).

Indicators track **drivers** (the processes behind a phenomenon) and **outcomes** (the results). There are three approaches:

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Capital approach">
The capital approach seeks a **balance among categories of capital** — **financial, natural, produced, human and social** — and their interactions (UNECE 2009). Stocks and flows are monitored in a **System of Integrated Environmental and Economic Accounts**.

**Weak sustainability:**

- natural and man-made capital are **highly substitutable**;
- as a resource grows scarce, its further use must bring **greater benefits** to be justified;
- early in development, exploitation **degrades** the environment. Past a **turning point**, richer societies **restore** it;
- man-made capital builds **knowledge and technology**, and **new technologies** compensate for losses such as biodiversity.

**Strong sustainability:**

- natural and man-made capital are **complements, not substitutes**;
- unlimited substitution is **impossible**;
- so **critical levels of environmental services must be maintained**.
</Tab>
<Tab label="Planetary boundaries">
Outcome-level estimates of **unsustainability** set **tipping points** for **nine Earth processes**, beyond which change becomes **uncontrollable**:

1. **Climate change** (CO₂ emissions and energy imbalance)
2. **Ocean acidification**
3. **Stratospheric ozone depletion**
4. **Atmospheric aerosol loading**
5. **Biochemical flows** (phosphorus and nitrogen cycles)
6. **Global freshwater use**
7. **Land-system change**
8. **Biodiversity loss**
9. **Chemical pollution**
</Tab>
<Tab label="Ecological footprint">
The **ecological footprint** estimates human impact — **resources consumed and waste to be absorbed** (Global Footprint Network 2010). It expresses everything in one unit: the **land area needed**, in **"global hectares"**.

It is a robust **measure of unsustainability**. It shows the **trade-offs** between:

- **present and future** generations;
- **"brown" agendas** (urban pollution, sanitation) and **"green" agendas** (ecosystems).
</Tab>
</Tabs>

### The environmental Kuznets curve

Weak sustainability is often pictured as an **inverted U**:

- as **per capita income rises**, environmental **degradation first increases**;
- after a **turning point**, degradation **falls** as richer societies clean up (Yandle, Bhattarai and Vijayaraghavan).

## Resource Use and Sustainable Development

**Development** was once seen narrowly as **economic growth** — capital formation from savings and investment — while social and cultural factors were ignored.

**Human development** is the alternative. It puts **people at the centre** and is about **enlarging people's choices**:

- **education, health care and income** — a decent standard of living;
- **freedom of expression, association and movement**;
- **social justice** and **protection from discrimination**;
- the ability to **influence decisions** (National Human Development Report 2016).

It was designed to **eradicate poverty and deprivation**, and it must itself be **sustainable**.

**Sustainable development** is economic development that **meets present needs and aspirations without compromising those of the future**. It achieves a quality of life that can last **for many generations** because it is:

- **socially desirable** — meeting cultural, material and spiritual needs **equitably**;
- **economically viable** — **paying for itself**, with costs not exceeding income;
- **ecologically sustainable** — keeping supporting ecosystems viable in the long term.

In practice it means:

- using renewable resources **without eliminating or degrading them**;
- **more recycling** and **more renewable energy** (solar, geothermal);
- regulating economies so resources are used **optimally** and benefits shared **equitably** (Knox, Agnew and McCarthy 2008);
- better **education, health and welfare**, raising **environmental awareness**;
- a shift from globalisation towards **"localisation"** — economies based on **local needs and conditions**.

**The SDGs:** **17 goals and 169 targets** adopted at the UN summit on **25 September 2015**. They respond to:

- **extreme poverty**;
- **resource depletion**;
- **climate change**;
- **unemployment**.

They build on the MDGs, with emphasis on conserving **oceans, fresh water, forests, mountains, drylands, biodiversity and wildlife**.

**Technology can be a threat or a solution:**

- a threat when it is **resource-using**;
- a solution when it brings **efficient, clean, resource-saving** methods.

Developing countries need **efficient technologies**. Developed countries have them, but often put **profit** before environmental policy.

**Obstacles to sustainable development:**

1. **Heavy reliance on fossil fuels**, which perpetuates international inequality and drives **global warming**. Renewables are pursued **half-heartedly** because of the interests of **powerful governments that control fossil fuels**.
2. **Population growth** in less developed countries, with **hunger and malnutrition**. Sustainability is feasible only when population growth is **in harmony with ecosystems' productive capacity**.
3. **Inadequate institutional frameworks.**

<Reveal question="1. Define a resource and classify resources by four criteria.">
A resource is any part of the environment, created by natural processes, that can create wealth or satisfy human needs. By abundance: renewable (flow — water, forests, air, sun) versus non-renewable (stock — fossil fuels, minerals). By origin: biotic (living, from the biosphere) versus abiotic (non-living — air, land, water, minerals). By concentration: diffuse (forests) versus point (mineral deposits). By stage of development: potential (e.g. possible Lake Chad oil), actual (Niger Delta oil), reserve (extractable with current technology) and stock (not yet profitably developable). de Zeeuw adds six renewal-based classes.

<Reveal question="2. Explain the concepts of resource and resistance, with the role of culture.">
The same element can be a resource or a resistance. A resistance is a socio-cultural or physical obstacle to using a resource: culture, the technology available to exploit and process it, and physical barriers. Culture — beliefs, institutions and technology — shapes how people perceive and exploit their environment (e.g. taboos). Societies with low technology have limited ability to use resources compared with advanced ones.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Discuss the causes and effects of soil erosion and deforestation.">
Erosion is accelerated by removing vegetation (cultivation, grazing, burning, bulldozing), especially on steep and long slopes — about 75 billion tonnes of fertile soil are lost each year. Effects: lower yields and higher costs, reduced water-holding capacity, nutrient loss requiring fertiliser, and siltation and water pollution. Deforestation (4,128 to 3,999 million ha, 1990–2015) comes from timber harvesting, farming, roads and firewood. It causes species loss, CO₂ release, a drier climate, flooding, siltation, poorer water quality and desertification.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. What are the causes of biodiversity loss?">
Habitat destruction (deforestation, overpopulation, pollution, warming — freshwater ecosystems are the most threatened); over-exploitation (over-fishing, hunting, logging, illegal wildlife trade); invasive species that spread once humans remove natural barriers; genetic pollution (hybridisation and gene swamping); and climate change (coral reef destruction, ocean de-oxygenation and acidification). Dams and drainage disrupt freshwater systems. WWF (2014) records declines of 76% in freshwater, 39% in marine and 39% in terrestrial wildlife since 1970.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="5. Define sustainability and explain its three pillars.">
Sustainability means improving quality of life within the limits of the global environment — meeting present needs without compromising future generations (Brundtland 1987). Environmental: harvest, pollution and depletion rates that can continue indefinitely. Economic: a level of production that can be supported indefinitely. Social: well-being maintained indefinitely. Munasinghe's pillars — economic (growth, efficiency, stability), social (poverty, equity, participation, culture) and environmental (biodiversity, resources, pollution) — must be balanced; their overlap is sustainable development, or the triple bottom line.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="6. Describe three approaches to measuring sustainability.">
The capital approach balances financial, natural, produced, human and social capital. Weak sustainability assumes substitution and an environmental Kuznets curve (degradation first rises with income, then falls after a turning point); strong sustainability says natural capital is irreplaceable, so critical levels must be kept. Planetary boundaries set tipping points for nine Earth processes, including climate change, ocean acidification, ozone, aerosols, nitrogen and phosphorus flows, freshwater, land-system change, biodiversity and chemical pollution. The ecological footprint converts consumption and waste into global hectares.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- A **resource** is any part of the environment that can create wealth or satisfy needs; its use depends on **culture, technology and the economic system**.
- **Classification**: renewable/non-renewable; biotic/abiotic; diffuse/point; **potential/actual/reserve/stock**; de Zeeuw's six classes (fossil fuels → minerals → fish → forests → air/water/soil → sun/wind).
- **Resistance** = a cultural, technological or physical obstacle to resource use.
- **Resources**: minerals (finite; eight elements dominate the crust), energy (renewable vs fossil; biogas = methane), forests (about 32% of land; carbon sequestration), water (finite), **biodiversity** (genetic, species, ecosystem; 50% of species in tropical moist forests), **soil** (a living body; forms extremely slowly).
- **Depletion**: erosion (75 bn t/yr), deforestation, biodiversity loss (freshwater −76%), climate change (IPCC 1995).
- **Sustainability** (Brundtland 1987); pillars: economic, social, environmental (**Munasinghe**); triple bottom line.
- **Measurement**: capital approach (**weak vs strong** sustainability; **environmental Kuznets curve**), **planetary boundaries** (nine processes), **ecological footprint** (global hectares).
- **Human development** = enlarging choices; **SDGs** (25 Sept 2015, 17 goals, 169 targets); obstacles: **fossil fuels, population growth, weak institutions**.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Be able to give **one example for each class** of resource, especially de Zeeuw's six and potential/actual/reserve.
- Quote the **Brundtland definition** word for word — it is almost always rewarded.
- Know the **three perspectives** on sustainability, with their authors, and the difference between **weak and strong** sustainability.
- For essays on resource depletion, organise by **soil, forests, biodiversity and climate**, each with causes, effects and a statistic.
