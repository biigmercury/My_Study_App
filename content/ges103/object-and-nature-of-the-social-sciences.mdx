## What Is Social Science?

**Social science** is the **study of human relationships within the socio-cultural environment** — how people live together, organise themselves, make a living, govern themselves and behave as individuals and in groups.

Scholars define it from different angles:

| Scholar | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Charles Beard** | The body of knowledge and thought pertaining to **human affairs**, as distinguished from **sticks, stones, stars and physical objects** (Kochhar 1984) |
| **James High** | Those bodies of learning and study which recognise the **simultaneous and mutual action of physical and non-physical stimuli** which produce **social reaction** (Singh 2008) |
| **Hunt and Colander** | Simply, the **study of human relations** (2008) |

Put together: social science is the field of knowledge that studies **human society and human relationships** — the activities of people in their social and physical environment.

### The core components of human social behaviour

Social science looks at every part of social life:

- **economic** behaviour (producing, buying, selling, saving),
- **political** behaviour (voting, governing, protesting),
- **cultural** behaviour and **tradition**,
- **customs** and **social institutions** (family, marriage, school, religion),
- **religious faiths** and **ethics**,
- the **value patterns** followed in society.

<Analogy>
Physics can tell you how fast a ball falls from a roof. It cannot tell you why a crowd gathers to watch it, why one onlooker laughs and another is angry, or who will be blamed if it hits a car. Those are questions about **people in relation to one another** — the territory of the social sciences.
</Analogy>

## How the Social Sciences Began

Social science is a **product of the eighteenth (18th) century**, when it became a field of study in institutions of higher learning. It evolved as a **by-product of modernisation, industrialisation, the Renaissance, urbanisation, the growth of science** and related developments (Barker 1967; Ross 1991).

The eighteenth century and after brought changes in human life and living that had hardly been seen before:

| Development | Date |
|---|---|
| The **Renaissance** in Italy and other European countries | from the 14th–17th centuries |
| The **Industrial Revolution** | from the second half of the 18th century (the textbook dates it from 1767) |
| The **American War of Independence** | 1776 |
| The **French Revolution** | 1789 |
| New forms of **capitalism** and immense progress in the **natural sciences** | 18th–19th centuries |

These changes brought both **happiness and difficulties** to human society, and social science developed **as a response to the need to understand modernity** (Ross 1991). Classic early texts of the social sciences include:

- **Montesquieu**, *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748)
- **Adam Smith**, *The Wealth of Nations* (1776)
- **J. G. Herder**, *Ideas towards a Philosophy of History* (1784–91)
- **Condorcet**, *Outline of an Historical View of the Progress of the Human Mind* (1795)

### New problems, new questions

Rapid industrialisation and modernisation produced a multitude of **social problems** — diseases, **social alienation**, **exploitation of workers** — while modern science produced **germ warfare**, **atomic weapons** and hazardous chemical and nuclear reactions that altered, and continue to alter, human relationships.

Today's example is **climate change**, a product of science and human activity. It has contributed to **desertification** in many places, including **northern Nigeria**, reducing available land and fuelling **communal conflicts** (such as clashes between farmers and herders). Understanding and managing such problems is exactly what the social sciences are for.

## What Is "Scientific" About Social Science?

Social science is the **disciplined and systematic study of society and its institutions, and of how and why people behave as they do, both as individuals and in groups** (Hargittai 2009). Being **scientific** requires:

1. **A systematic, step-by-step approach** to acquiring and analysing knowledge.
2. **Verifiability** — knowledge must meet the standards laid down by the scientific community and be **open to verification at all times**.
3. **Objectivity** — the analysis of the subject matter must be objective, free from personal bias.

### The difficulty: people change

In the **hard sciences** (physics, chemistry) the objects of research can be **held constant** in a laboratory. In social science the subject of study — **human beings** — **changes** from time to time. Social scientists therefore state their findings with the condition **ceteris paribus**, meaning **"all things being equal"** (other factors remaining constant) (Galavotti 2003).

### The debate

Some scholars argue that society, its institutions and relationships **are not susceptible to scientific study**, and that the methods of the natural sciences should not be applied to social phenomena (Gareau 1987). A telling example: in the early **1980s** the **British government** renamed the **Social Science Research Council** the **Economic and Social Research Council** — as if "social" and "scientific" did not sit comfortably together. As one scholar put it, *"the message seemed to be: if it's social it can't be scientific!"* (Halloran 1998).

The social sciences answer that science is defined by its **method** (systematic, verifiable, objective), not its subject matter.

| | Natural (hard) sciences | Social sciences |
|---|---|---|
| **Subject matter** | Physical objects and natural phenomena ("sticks, stones, stars") | Human beings, groups and institutions |
| **Control** | Variables can usually be held constant in a laboratory | People change; findings hold *ceteris paribus* |
| **Prediction** | Often precise and universal | Probabilistic; depends on context and culture |
| **Researcher's position** | Outside the object of study | Part of the society being studied — must guard against bias |
| **Shared requirement** | Systematic method, verifiable evidence, objectivity | Systematic method, verifiable evidence, objectivity |

## The Scope of Social Science

The scope of social science is **as wide as the discipline itself**:

- It is concerned with **human relations**, and it is also a **product of history and development**. Knowledge of the **past** lets social scientists build **development models** that enhance human welfare — without understanding a society's history and present situation, it is difficult to construct a development agenda.
- It studies **man-made institutions** created to **regulate human behaviour** — the **family, marriage, politics, the economy**. Without them society would be chaotic: human beings love freedom, but **one person's freedom ends where another person's begins**. Institutions regulate relationships (for example, whom one may or may not marry) and help develop good citizens.
- It studies the **transmission of societal values** from generation to generation, which sustains societies, and provides organised generation and transmission of knowledge.
- It helps **predict behaviour related to new technologies** and prepare for their menaces, such as **cybercrime**.
- It studies **internationalism** — relationships beyond borders — which is vital in an age of **globalisation**.

## Why Study Social Science?

1. To **solve practical problems** — social, economic, political and health problems.
2. Many **health challenges are products of human behaviour and interaction**. Knowledge of behaviour patterns helps cut the link between people and disease — e.g. **HIV/AIDS**, which ravaged much of Africa and Asia in the late 1990s and early 2000s. (This is why the University of Ibadan introduced **GES 107**, on the causes, prevention and management of the disease.)
3. To develop **efficient citizens of democracy**.
4. To **form a person's social character**.
5. For the **moral progress** of society.
6. For the **development of institutions and communities**.
7. To develop an **international viewpoint** through knowledge generation and dissemination — information now exists on virtually all areas of human endeavour, helping decision-making by individuals and organisations.

### Objectives of social science

- To provide **knowledge of the natural and social environment**, so people know how to fulfil their needs.
- To **develop human qualities** through functional education, learning and **socialisation**, in which learners know their **duties towards society**.
- To develop **imagination, critical thinking and reasoning power**, and provide knowledge and understanding.
- To enable students to **work according to the environment** and develop **desirable attitudes** and good behaviour.
- To develop the proficiency to **compare, analyse, summarise and evaluate** phenomena.
- To develop the **personality** of the individual together with feelings of **cooperation**, **morality** and **universal brotherhood** — the hallmark of the Ibadan School of Social Sciences.

## The Ibadan School of Social Sciences

The **Ibadan School of Social Sciences (IBSSS)** — the Faculty of the Social Sciences, University of Ibadan — is as old as the university's history.

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Geography** | Started in **1948** as a foundational department, located in **both the Faculties of Arts and Science** until **1967**, when it joined the Faculty of the Social Sciences |
| **Economics** | The school commenced with the **Department of Economics in 1958** — a small department with few students |
| **Growth** | **Political Science** grew out of **Economics**; **Sociology** grew out of **Political Science**; **Law and Psychology** grew out of **Sociology** |
| **Size** | The Faculty consists of **six departments** |
| **Offspring** | Two faculties have emerged from it: the **Faculty of Economics** and the **Faculty of Environmental Design and Management** |
| **Philosophy** | It has **decolonised** the theories and perspectives of social analysis — using universal theories and methods but **contextualising** them for a culturally relevant curriculum |

## Conclusion: The Social Scientist's Stance

To serve people well, social scientists must **understand people**. They need to **detach themselves from common sense** — the perspective of the person on the street — but must not become so detached that they **impose their own (expert) perspective** without regard for the experience of those involved. They must be **as objective as possible** in their judgement.

<Reveal question="1. Define social science and list the core components of human social behaviour.">
Social science is the study of human relationships within the socio-cultural environment (simply, the study of human relations). The core components of social behaviour are economic behaviour, political behaviour, cultural behaviour and tradition, customs and social institutions, religious faiths and ethics, and the value patterns followed in society.

<Reveal question="2. What makes social science 'scientific'? Why is this sometimes disputed?">
It uses a systematic, stepwise approach to acquiring and analysing knowledge; its knowledge must be verifiable according to the standards of the scientific community; and its analysis must be objective. It is disputed because humans — unlike the objects of the hard sciences — change and cannot be held constant (hence *ceteris paribus*), and some scholars argue social phenomena are not susceptible to natural-science methods (e.g. the renaming of Britain's SSRC to the ESRC in the early 1980s).
</Reveal>

</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Give an account of the growth of the Ibadan School of Social Sciences.">
Geography began in 1948 (in Arts and Science until it joined the Faculty in 1967); the school began with Economics in 1958; Political Science grew out of Economics, Sociology out of Political Science, and Law and Psychology out of Sociology. The Faculty now has six departments, and the Faculties of Economics and of Environmental Design and Management emerged from it. It has decolonised social analysis by contextualising universal theories for a culturally relevant curriculum.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Social science** = the study of human relationships in the socio-cultural environment (Beard: human affairs vs sticks, stones and stars; High: mutual action of physical and non-physical stimuli producing social reaction; Hunt & Colander: study of human relations).
- It arose in the **18th century** from modernisation, industrialisation, the Renaissance, urbanisation, the French (1789) and American (1776) revolutions and the growth of science — to understand **modernity** and its problems.
- **Scientific** = systematic, verifiable, objective; humans change, so findings hold **ceteris paribus**; some dispute its scientific status (SSRC → ESRC).
- **Scope**: human relations, history and development, institutions that regulate behaviour, value transmission, technology's effects (cybercrime), internationalism.
- **Ibadan School**: Geography 1948, Economics 1958; Political Science ← Economics; Sociology ← Political Science; Law and Psychology ← Sociology; six departments.
</Takeaway>
