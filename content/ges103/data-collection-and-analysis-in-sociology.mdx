## Sociology as the Science of Society

**Comte (1798–1857)** was the **first to develop the concept of sociology**, which he called **"social physics"**. He likened sociology to the **natural sciences**: it would apply the scientific method used to study natural phenomena to the **social structure of society**.

In the hard sciences, without "nature" there is nothing for the scientist to do. The scientist **observes the way nature works** and supplies information on the **order** that seems to exist in it (**Ritzer 2008**). Sociology looks at society the same way:

- Physics and chemistry explain **natural laws**.
- Sociology investigates **social structure** — **the patterns of social relationships in society**.

In short, sociology is **the science of society**.

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Society** | An **organised group of individuals** who occupy a **defined geographical area** and are joined together by **relationships and patterns of behaviour (culture)** that distinguish them from others |
| **Science** | The **logical, systematic methods** by which knowledge is obtained, **and** the body of knowledge those methods produce (Igbo 2003) |
| **Sociology** | The **systematic study of human social relationships**, together with the patterns of behaviour and processes by which members relate and become distinctive as **individuals, groups, institutions and society** |

**Max Weber** offered an alternative view: sociology should be concerned with **understanding the social action of individuals**. **Social action** is action with **subjective meaning** built around the actor's conscious thought, **oriented towards the actions of others**. Sociology is therefore *"a science concerning itself with the **interpretive understanding of social action** and thereby with a **causal explanation of its course and consequences**."*

### Methodology — and the great divide

One of the sociologist's main roles is **scientific research** into human interaction, to build knowledge for the **development of social order**. So sociology has a **methodology** for generating and analysing data. **Methodology** covers two things (Haralambos and Holborn 2008):

- the **detailed research methods** through which data are collected;
- the **general philosophies** on which the collection and analysis of data are based.

Methodology is a **point of division** among sociologists:

| Positivist (scientific) | Humanistic (interpretive) |
|---|---|
| Apply the **methodology of the natural sciences** to sociological research | Studying human behaviour is **fundamentally different** from studying the natural world |
| **Quantitative** methods — numerical, statistical | **Qualitative** methods |

These are the **two extreme pillars** of sociology. Recent scholars increasingly **combine** them — **methodological pluralism**:

| Form of pluralism | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Triangulation** (the most popular) | Using **quantitative and qualitative** methods to **cross-check** each other's findings in one study |
| **Complementarity** | Two research strategies are used so that **different aspects of an investigation can be dovetailed** (Haralambos and Holborn 2008) |
| **Facilitation** | One method is used to **assist** another — e.g. **participant observation or interviews** generate ideas for the **questions in a questionnaire** |

The choice of methodology depends on the **orientation of the researcher** and the **peculiarity of the research topic**.

## What Are Data?

**Data** (plural of **datum**) means **a fact or statistic** (*Oxford Dictionary of Sociology*). Data are **records of observations** — IQ test scores, interview records, fieldwork diaries, taped interviews — from which **inferences may be drawn** through analysis (Scott and Marshall 2000). They are the **symbols, numbers and characters** used to describe attributes such as age, sex, productivity or optimism.

**Data are not the same as information** (Ogundipe et al. 2006):

- **data** are the **raw input**;
- **information** is **data that have been assembled, processed and interpreted** in a meaningful way.

**Data collection is a fundamental stage in carrying out research in sociology.** The way data are collected determines:

- the kind of **statistical analysis** that can be used;
- the **reliability and power** of the findings.

The **research design and methodology** determine which collection techniques are used.

### Types of data

| | Quantitative | Qualitative |
|---|---|---|
| **Form** | **Numerical** — statistics | **Written, visual or audio** — interview transcripts, newspapers, websites |
| **Note** | — | Qualitative data can still be **analysed and displayed numerically** |

| | Primary | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| **Collected by** | The researcher **first-hand**, designed for his specific research questions | **Previous researchers or organisations** — government, **WHO, UN, ECOWAS** |
| **Methods / sources** | **Social surveys (questionnaires), interviews, experiments, observation** | Quantitative: **official statistics**. Qualitative: government reports, newspapers, **diaries**, historical sources, life documents, mass media, online audio-visual content |
| **Strengths** | Tailored to the study | **Save time and money**; give access to **historical data** from before the researcher was born (Haralambos and Holborn 2008) |
| **Weakness** | Costly, slow | **Reliability and validity** sometimes questionable; may not give exactly the information required |

## Primary Methods (1): The Questionnaire

A **questionnaire** is **a set of questions on the variables under study**, set by the researcher and answered by **sampled respondents**.

- The **respondents** are the targeted subjects who answer.
- It may be administered **by the researcher**, or left to be **self-administered** — when there are very many questionnaires, or when the researcher's presence might affect the answers.
- Questionnaires must be **identical** — the same questions in the same order — so that **comparable data** come from every member of the sample.
- Questions may be framed as **items (indicators)** that measure specific **concepts or variables**.

### Open-ended and closed questions

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Open-ended">
The respondent **composes his own answer** instead of choosing from alternatives, e.g. *"How did you settle disagreements between friends in the same workplace?"*

- **For:** fairly **valid** data — respondents say what they mean **in their own words**.
- **But:** answers must be **classified and recoded** into quantitative form before statistical analysis. For example, the researcher might find that a certain percentage settled disagreements **amicably, without management or any third party**. This takes extra time and logic, so "lazy researchers" often avoid open questions.
- **Unavoidable when** there is **little literature** or no standardised items to measure a variable, when studying a **new topical area**, or when exploring **further dimensions** of a variable.
</Tab>
<Tab label="Closed / fixed-choice">
The respondent **selects from a given set of answers**, e.g.:

*What prompted you to go for Ifa therapy?*

- A. Modern health care failed
- B. Modern health care is too costly
- C. Lack of trust in the efficiency of modern health care
- D. Ifa priests are easily accessible

**Likert scale** items ask for a level of agreement, e.g. *"I have the opportunity to develop close friendships at my workplace"*:

1. Strongly agree
2. Agree
3. Undecided
4. Disagree
5. Strongly disagree

*(The textbook prints the second option as "Disagreed" — a misprint for "Agree".)*

Researchers may also use **score ranges** (1–10, 1–20 or more), e.g. to rate **optimism** against a preset score.

- **Weakness:** no **original answers** — a respondent's real answer may **not be among the options**, "leaving the data paralysed". It can be hard to know exactly what is being measured.
</Tab>
</Tabs>

### Administering questionnaires

**Interviewer-administered** — used for the **aged** and others unable to fill forms. Its drawbacks:

- **interviewer bias** — answers are influenced by the researcher's presence;
- higher **cost**, because trained interviewers must be recruited.

**Self-administered** — the **most common** method. It can be done in five ways:

| Method | How | Problems |
|---|---|---|
| **Face-to-face** | The researcher meets the respondent, explains the purpose, hands over the form and agrees a return date. Easy when respondents are **clustered** in one place | — |
| **Postal** | Mailed with a **stamped addressed envelope**. **Inexpensive** for respondents spread over a **wide area** | Return rate **rarely exceeds 50%, sometimes below 25%**. Returns may be **one-sided** — e.g. mostly working people or mostly women |
| **Telephone** | Questions asked by phone after prior agreement; common in **marketing research** | **Rarely used by sociologists**; unsuitable for long questionnaires; options must be repeated; response may be low |
| **E-mail** | Best for **very sensitive questions** needing privacy, e.g. **HIV/AIDS status** | Getting everyone's e-mail address; only people **with internet access** can take part — **unrepresentative**; low return rate |
| **Internet (web survey)** | The newest and easiest; the form is on a website, sometimes with incentives like **recharge cards** | Excludes people **without internet access** — uneven data |

**The problem common to all of them:** a large set of **less-privileged people are left out**, which can hurt the **reliability and validity** of the data.

### Advantages and disadvantages of questionnaires

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Large quantities of data** from many people in a **short time** | **Wording** may be understood differently — e.g. "get to know my co-workers". Avoid **double-barrelled** items such as *"Workplace friendship developed because of nearness **and** shared experience"* — a respondent may agree with one and not the other |
| Quick, efficient analysis with software such as **SPSS** | Respondents give only what is asked — **limited data**, and **new hypotheses** are hard to develop |
| Analysed more **scientifically and objectively** than qualitative data | **Operationalisation** can **distort** concepts through **researcher's imposition** of his own assumptions (Haralambos and Holborn 2008) |
| Easy **comparison** — for reliability tests and **prediction**, e.g. past election results used to hypothesise the next | **Reactivity** — respondents give the answers they think the researcher wants |
| **New hypotheses and theories** from well-prepared questions | Distance rules out understanding the **meanings and motives** of respondents |
| **Large samples** — representativeness and reliability | **Recoding** open answers into numbers **loses important information** |

## Primary Methods (2): The Interview

**Interviewing** engages subjects in **questioning and conversation** to obtain **qualitative information**. The interviewer — the researcher or a trained research interviewer — asks **predetermined research questions** of the **participant (interviewee)**.

| Form | Description |
|---|---|
| **Structured interview** | An **orderly list of questions** is followed; the questionnaire is the guide and **no deviation** is expected, but the interviewer may **probe** answers for clarification |
| **Unstructured interview** | Like a **conversation** with **no predetermined order** of questions. The researcher has a **schedule of topics** and steers the talk back if an area is missed |

Most interviews fall **between** these two extremes. Sociologists use **three interview tools**:

| Tool | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **In-depth interview (IDI)** | **Intensive individual interviews** with a **small number** of respondents to explore their perspectives. Useful for **detailed** information about thoughts and behaviour, for **new issues**, or to give **context** to other data | Ifa priests describing their experiences, thoughts and changes as part of the Ifa health system |
| **Key informant interview (KII)** | A **knowledgeable person** — through skills or position — gives deeper insight, especially when the information is **hard to get from those who experienced it** (sensitive topics) | A **pimp** as key informant in a study of prostitution, because prostitutes may fear the consequences of disclosure |
| **Focus group discussion (FGD)** | A **moderator/facilitator** engages a group of carefully selected participants on a topic. Bryman (2001): a **group interview** with a tightly defined topic, where the accent is on **interaction within the group** and the **joint construction of meaning** | **7 to 12 participants**. The moderator introduces topics, **times** the session and **records** conclusions (often with a **tape recorder**); an assistant may note **gestures**. FGDs are **more "natural"** than face-to-face interviews (Wilkinson 2004) |

| Advantages of interviews | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Larger samples** than observation, so generalisations are more justified | Answers may be **inaccurate** — participants may **lie, forget or lack** the information |
| **Versatile** — statistics and qualitative information; can discover things that **cannot be asked directly** | The interviewer's **presence influences** responses, reducing validity |
| **Misunderstood words can be clarified** (unlike a self-administered questionnaire) | **Interviewer bias** — participants may be consciously or unconsciously **led** to certain answers |
| Researcher and participant **agree on meanings**, removing researcher's imposition | — |
| **Partnership** and **critical reflection** by both sides | — |
| **Higher response rate** than questionnaires | — |

## Primary Methods (3): Observation

**Observation is one of the best data collection methods used by sociologists.** It involves **systematically selecting, watching and recording** the behaviour and characteristics of the subjects of a study. It can last **months or years**, until the observer is satisfied with the data.

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Non-participant observation** | The observer watches — **openly or concealed** — but **does not take part**, e.g. watching protesters or market women without joining them. It can give **more accurate** information on lifestyles than interviews and questionnaires |
| **Participant observation** | The observer **takes part** in the situation — joins the protesters or the market women — for deeper understanding and easier data collection. It is the main method of **ethnography**, e.g. **Bronisław Malinowski's** study of the **Trobriand Islands**. It may be **overt** (identity and purpose declared) or **covert** (hidden) — e.g. a criminologist posing as a prisoner |

**Covert observation versus ethics:** people may stop acting naturally in front of an outsider, which is why some researchers hide their identity. But covert research **violates the ethic of informed consent**. The textbook's solution is to observe openly for long enough: **within three to six months** people forget the observer and return to their normal lives.

| Advantages of observation | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Best way to get a **valid picture of social reality in its natural setting** | **Time-consuming** |
| **Validity and reliability** — it is hard for subjects to lie or mislead | Only **very small groups**, so hard to **generalise** |
| Participant observation gives the **subjective views** of subjects | **Risk** of physical and psychological danger |
| **Best means of studying interaction** | Hard to **compare or replicate** — "not scientific" |
| Promotes **objectivity**; useful for **testing and developing** hypotheses | The observer's **power of imposition** can manipulate the research |
| High **ecological validity** | Findings may be faulty if people **change their behaviour** during the study |

## Secondary Sources

Sociologists often use secondary sources for **practical reasons**: they are **cost-effective** and give access to **historical data** about events in the distant past.

| Source | Description | Strengths and weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Official statistics** | Existing statistics from government agencies and international bodies. Nigeria: **National Bureau of Statistics (NBS)**, **National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS)**, **National Population Commission** (census). International: **WHO, UN, UNESCO, AU, ECOWAS** | Only large organisations can gather data on **millions** of people. Used by **demographers** (birth rate, fertility, divorce) and **criminologists** (crime statistics). **Durkheim** used **suicide statistics** to relate suicide to other social facts. **For:** unobtrusive (no **reactivity**), readily available, cheap, **generalisable**. **Against:** limited in use and scope; possible **alteration**, intentional or not, by the source body |
| **2. Historical documents** | Needed when **past events** matter | Haralambos and Holborn: vital for studying **social change over extended periods** and how present patterns came about. Without them sociology would give a **static** view — e.g. **Weber's** work on **religion and the development of capitalism** could not have existed. But they are open to **subjective and ideological imposition** by their authors, so findings are risky to generalise |
| **3. Life documents** | Any document detailing a person's life and development — **letters, diaries, autobiographies, biographies** — historical or contemporary | Give a **qualitative view** of a person's inner state. But samples are **small**, the documents are open to **misinterpretation**, **unrepresentative**, **scarce** and hard to access |
| **4. Mass media** (including the **internet**) | Newspapers, broadcasts, websites; the internet is especially useful to student researchers | Criticised for **distorted images** of society — so content must be **thoroughly assessed**. Problems: **little or no scrutiny** before publication; doubtful **authority** of authors; **obsolete** material; **ideological orientation** of sources |

## Data Analysis

**Data analysis** is **a body of methods that help to describe facts, detect patterns, develop explanations and test hypotheses**. It is the process of looking at and summarising data to **extract useful information and develop conclusions**. There are **two main categories**, determined by the kind of data collected:

- **quantitative data** (from questionnaires and interviews) — analysed **statistically** with computer programmes;
- **qualitative data** — analysed by **content analysis** and **computer-assisted programmes**.

### Quantitative analysis

**Quantitative analysis** converts data to **numerical form** so they can be analysed statistically — *the numerical representation and manipulation of observations to describe and explain the phenomena they reflect*. The major tool is the **Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS)**, which handles large data sets and does both **descriptive** and **inferential** statistics.

<FlowStep title="2. Data entry">
Variables are defined and given **value labels**, then the questionnaires are entered **one after another**. Software used in social research includes **Microsoft Excel, Microsoft Access** and, above all, **SPSS**.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="3. Data cleaning">
Before analysis, **check for entry errors** — e.g. typing **22 instead of 2**. Such errors distort analysis, especially analyses sensitive to **outliers** (values far below or above the rest). **The best way to find errors in a categorical variable is to run descriptive frequencies.**
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="4. Analysis proper">
**Univariate** (descriptive) → **bivariate** → **multivariate**, with **inferential** statistics to generalise from the sample to the population.
</FlowStep>

### Levels of measurement

The codebook's "Measure" column refers to these levels *(definitions beyond the textbook)*:

| Level | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Nominal** | **Categories** with no order; numbers are just labels | Sex, religion, state of origin |
| **Ordinal** | **Ordered** categories, unequal gaps | Likert responses, academic rank |
| **Interval** | Equal intervals, **no true zero** | Temperature in °C, attitude scale scores |
| **Ratio** | Equal intervals **and a true zero** | Age, income, years of service |

**Categorical** variables are nominal or ordinal. **Continuous** variables are interval or ratio.

### Univariate analysis: descriptive statistics

**Univariate analysis** is the **analysis of a single variable for the purpose of description**. It is sometimes called **descriptive statistics** and includes:

- **frequency distributions**;
- **averages** (measures of central tendency);
- **measures of dispersion**.

Researchers present the **socio-demographic characteristics** of the sample (sex, age, religion) in one table of counts and percentages. This also helps check whether variables **violate the assumptions** of the tests they plan to use.

**Frequency distribution** — the number of times each value occurs, with **percentage** and **cumulative percentage**. It can also be shown as a **histogram, bar chart or pie chart**:

| Sex | Frequency | Percent | Cumulative percent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Male | 152 | 51.0 | 51.0 |
| Female | 146 | 49.0 | 100.0 |
| **Total** | **298** | **100.0** | |

**Measures of central tendency** — the **"averages"**, or typical value. Take five students' ages: **22, 18, 32, 18, 25**.

- **Mean** — add all the values and divide by how many there are: 115 ÷ 5 = **23** (the arithmetic mean).
- **Median** — the **middle value** when ranked (18, 18, **22**, 25, 32): **22**.
- **Mode** — the **most frequent value**: **18**.

**Measures of dispersion** — how spread out the values are around the average:

- **Range** — highest minus lowest; the ages above **range from 18 to 32**.
- **Standard deviation (SD)** — spread **around the mean**. In a normal distribution about **68%** of cases lie within **±1 SD**, **95%** within **±2 SD**, and nearly all within **±3 SD**. *(The textbook says 99.9%; the standard figure is **99.7%**.)* Example: mean age 30 and SD 10 means about 68% are aged **20 to 40**.
- A **small SD** means values cluster **tightly** around the mean; a **large SD** means they are **widely spread** (Babbie 2000).

**Which average for which variable?**

- **Categorical** data → **frequencies and the mode**.
- **Continuous** data → **measures of dispersion** as well.
- The textbook says **medians** are computed for **interval** data and **means** for **ratio** data. *(Most statistics texts use the mean, with the SD, for both interval and ratio data, and the median for ordinal or badly skewed data.)*

**Skewness and kurtosis** (requested in SPSS before parametric tests):

| Value | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **0** | Perfectly **normal** (rare in sociological data) |
| **Positive skewness** | Scores **clustered to the left**, at **low values** |
| **Negative skewness** | Scores **clustered at the high, right-hand end** |
| **Positive kurtosis** | **Peaked** distribution (clustered at the centre) with long thin tails |
| **Kurtosis below 0** | Relatively **flat** — too many cases at the extremes |

### Bivariate and multivariate analysis

| Analysis | What it does | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Univariate** | **One** variable, for **description** | Frequency of sex; mean age |
| **Bivariate** | **Two** variables **simultaneously**, to find **empirical relationships** and draw inferences | A **cross-tabulation** (simple percentages); a **correlation coefficient** |
| **Multivariate** | **More than two** variables at once — **simultaneous relationships** among several variables | Influence of **age, sex and institution** on the **job satisfaction** of academic staff |

A **contingency table** presents bivariate and multivariate relationships as **percentage distributions**.

### Inferential statistics

**Inferential statistics** make **judgements about population parameters** from a **sample**, through **hypothesis testing** and **confidence intervals**. There are two kinds:

| | Parametric | Non-parametric |
|---|---|---|
| **Name from** | **"Parameter"** — a characteristic of a population | — |
| **Requirement** | Scores must be **normally distributed**; variables preferably **continuous** (interval or ratio) | **Less stringent**; for **nominal and ordinal** (categorical) data and **very small samples**; used when data fail parametric assumptions |
| **Tools** | **Regression, Pearson correlation, t-test, ANOVA** | **Spearman's rank order correlation (rho), chi-square, Kruskal–Wallis** |
| **Power** | More powerful | **Less powerful** at detecting real differences — so collect continuous data where possible |

**Before parametric analysis**, researchers check the following:

- **Reliability of scales** — **internal consistency** (do the items "hang together"?), measured with **Cronbach's alpha**, ideally **above .7**. Short scales of fewer than ten items may give values around .5; then report the **mean inter-item correlation**.
- **Missing data** — few human-subject studies are complete. Assign a **distinctive code** for missing values (e.g. **9 or 0**, one not used for real attributes), calculate the percentage missing, and **report any pattern** (e.g. many skipped one sensitive question).
- **Normality** — the **bell-shaped curve**, assessed through **skewness and kurtosis**, SPSS's **Explore** option, or **histograms, scatter plots, box plots**.
- **Outliers** — parametric tests are **very sensitive** to them. Deal with them, or remove them if they are insignificant.
- **Manipulating data**:
  - **(a)** add up scale items into a **total score** — first **reverse negatively worded items**;
  - **(b)** **transform skewed variables**;
  - **(c)** **collapse continuous variables** (e.g. age into young, middle-aged and old);
  - **(d)** **reduce categories** (e.g. academic ranks into junior/senior lecturers).

### Choosing the right statistic

Statistical techniques in sociology have **two main purposes**: **exploring relationships** among variables, and **exploring differences** between groups.

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Exploring relationships">
| Technique | Use | Non-parametric alternative |
|---|---|---|
| **Pearson correlation (r)** | **Strength and direction** of the relationship between **two continuous variables**. Positive = both rise together; negative = one rises as the other falls | **Spearman's rank order correlation (rho)** |
| **Partial correlation** | Pearson's r while **controlling for a confounding variable** | None |
| **Multiple regression** | How well a **set of independent variables predicts one continuous dependent variable**, and each one's contribution. Needs a **large sample**: at least **50 + 8m** cases, where m = number of independent variables (Tabachnick and Fidell) | None |
| **Logistic regression** | How well predictors explain a **categorical** dependent variable (e.g. did the respondent report the experience or not?); sensitive to **multicollinearity** and outliers | None |
| **Factor analysis** | **Condenses many items into a few factors** by finding "clumps" of correlated items — used in **developing scales**; needs a large sample (e.g. **150+**, Pallant 2005) | None |
</Tab>
<Tab label="Comparing groups">
| Technique | Use | Non-parametric alternative |
|---|---|---|
| **Independent-samples t-test** | Compare the **means of two different groups** on a continuous variable, e.g. **UI vs Lead City University** staff on performance | **Mann–Whitney U test** |
| **Paired-samples t-test** | Compare the **same people at Time 1 and Time 2** (repeated measures), e.g. before and after an intervention | **Wilcoxon signed rank test** |
| **One-way between-groups ANOVA** | Compare the means of **three or more groups** on one continuous variable; **post hoc** tests show where the differences lie | **Kruskal–Wallis test** |
| **One-way repeated-measures ANOVA** | The same group measured on **three or more occasions** | **Friedman test** |
| **Two-way ANOVA** | **Two independent variables** on one dependent variable — tests the **main effects** and the **interaction effect** (e.g. turnover intention rises with age **only for females**) | None |
| **MANOVA** | Compare groups on **several related dependent variables** at once (e.g. rank's effect on satisfaction, performance, commitment and turnover intention) | None |
| **ANCOVA** | Statistically **removes the effect of a confounding variable** (covariate) | None |
| **Chi-square** | **Two categorical variables** — e.g. sex (male/female) vs workplace friendship (yes/no). Assumption: **no expected cell frequency below 5** | *(It is itself non-parametric; no parametric alternative)* |
</Tab>
</Tabs>

## Qualitative Analysis

**Qualitative analysis** determines and interprets observations in **written form** to develop their **inherent meanings and patterns of social relationships** — often with data **not meant to be quantified**. It is dear to sociologists in **field and historical research**.

Unlike quantitative analysis, which can "lose sight" of theory, qualitative **data collection and analysis are inseparable from the guiding theory**. That continuity demands **high skill and experience**.

### Content analysis

**Content analysis** is the study of **recorded and printed communications** — newspapers, books, paintings, drawings, web pages, letters, speeches — to discover their **underlying meanings**. It answers **"Who says what, to whom, why, how and with what effect?"** (Babbie 2000). For example, Nigerian newspapers can be content-analysed to uncover their hidden **political sentiments** on an issue such as **restructuring**.

**Coding** here transforms raw data into a **standardised form**, classified according to a **conceptual framework** — e.g.:

- novels as **comedy or tragedy**;
- editorials as **propagandistic or not**;
- speeches as **aggressive or appealing**.

**Conceptualisation and operationalisation** are crucial. After coding, the researcher counts and keeps records, so content analysis **can be quantitative**.

**Ray Pawson (1995)** describes **four approaches**:

| Approach | Focus |
|---|---|
| **1. Formal content analysis** | **Numerical** — counts of frequently appearing themes, e.g. newspapers' editorials classified from **very liberal** to **very conservative**, and **pro-** or **anti-government**. Appealing for its **simplicity and reliability** |
| **2. Thematic analysis** | Discovering the **ideological biases** of the author |
| **3. Textual analysis** | Examining **linguistic devices** — how texts shape a particular interpretation — through **semiotics** (the study of **signs**). E.g. words used inside a **cult group** mean something different in mainstream conversation |
| **4. Audience analysis** | Studying the **audience's responses** to media content — a check on the researcher's own interpretation |

### Finding patterns

*"A data set without any pattern is as useless as torn cloth."* **John and Lyn Lofland (1995)** suggest six ways to find patterns, illustrated with **quarrels among workplace friends**:

1. **Frequencies** — how often do quarrels occur?
2. **Magnitudes** — how serious were they?
3. **Structures** — what forms did they take (overt or covert)?
4. **Processes** — how did they start and end?
5. **Causes** — what caused them?
6. **Consequences** — how do they affect work relationships?

**Cross-case analysis** looks for patterns across several cases. **Miles and Huberman (1994)** give two strategies:

- **variable-oriented analysis** — explains a particular variable;
- **case-oriented analysis** — examines the details of each case.

Analysing cases inductively can lead to **grounded theory**.

**Grounded Theory Method (GTM)** was introduced by **Barney Glaser and Anselm Strauss**. It is an **inductive** approach in which **theories are generated solely from an examination of data**, rather than derived deductively — building theory "**from the ground up**, with no preconceptions".

### The process of qualitative data analysis

Take this interview excerpt: *"My expectation is that my employer will consider giving me a pay increase after a year of service, just like my co-workers predicted."*

| Step | What it is | From the excerpt |
|---|---|---|
| **Open coding** | Naming the **major concepts** in a piece of data; separating data into discrete parts to **compare and contrast** | "employer", "pay increase", "time of service" |
| **Axial coding** | **Re-coding** the open codes to find more **analytical concepts** | "perceived fairness", "power relation" (the employer controls pay) |
| **Selective coding** | Identifying the **central code** around which all others revolve | "**employer–employee relation**" |
| **Memoing** | Writing **notes** on the meaning of each code, interpretations, and relationships between variables — the report is derived from the memos | — |
| **Concept mapping** *(optional)* | A **graphical display of concepts and their interrelations**, useful for building theory (Babbie 2000) | — |

These steps can also be applied to **open-ended questionnaire answers** — which is how researchers get **originality** in their findings.

**Software for qualitative analysis** (as SPSS is for numbers):

- **The Ethnograph**, **NVIVO**, **ATLAS.ti** — NVIVO and ATLAS.ti are the most popular;
- **HyperQual**, **HyperRESEARCH**, **HyperSoft**;
- **NUD\*IST**, **Qualrus**, **QUALOG**, **TextbaseAlpha**, **SONAR**.

Skill with them comes only through **repeated training and use**.

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. Distinguish between (a) quantitative and qualitative data, (b) primary and secondary data, with examples.">
(a) Quantitative data are numerical (statistics, coded questionnaire responses); qualitative data are written, visual or audio (interview transcripts, newspapers, websites), though they can be displayed numerically. (b) Primary data are collected first-hand by the researcher through questionnaires, interviews, experiments and observation; secondary data were collected by others — government and agencies such as WHO, UN and ECOWAS — as official statistics, historical documents, life documents and mass media. Secondary data save time and money and give access to the past, but their reliability and validity may be questionable.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Discuss the questionnaire as a method of data collection, its types of questions, modes of administration, advantages and disadvantages.">
A questionnaire is a set of questions on the study variables answered by sampled respondents; it must be the same for all. Open-ended questions let respondents answer in their own words (valid, but they need recoding); closed questions offer fixed choices or Likert scales (easy to analyse, but may omit the respondent's true answer). It can be interviewer-administered (interviewer bias, cost) or self-administered — face-to-face, postal (return rate often below 50%), telephone, e-mail or internet (which exclude those without access). Advantages: large data quickly, SPSS analysis, objectivity, comparison, large samples. Disadvantages: ambiguous and double-barrelled wording, limited data, researcher's imposition, reactivity, loss of meaning, recoding losses.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Explain the three interview tools used by sociologists.">
In-depth interview (IDI): intensive individual interviews with a few respondents to explore their perspectives in detail (e.g. Ifa priests). Key informant interview (KII): a knowledgeable insider supplies information that is hard to obtain from those directly involved, especially on sensitive issues (e.g. a pimp in a study of prostitution). Focus group discussion (FGD): 7–12 selected participants discuss a tightly defined topic under a moderator who introduces topics, times the session and records it; the accent is on group interaction and the joint construction of meaning.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Compare participant and non-participant observation and state the advantages and disadvantages of observation.">
Non-participant: the observer watches, openly or concealed, without taking part. Participant: the observer joins the group (the ethnographic method, e.g. Malinowski in the Trobriand Islands), overtly or covertly — covert observation conflicts with informed consent. Advantages: a valid picture of reality in its natural setting; hard for subjects to mislead; subjective views; the best way to study interaction; ecological validity; useful for developing hypotheses. Disadvantages: time-consuming; only small groups, hard to generalise; risky; hard to replicate; observer imposition; people may change their behaviour.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="5. Describe the preliminary steps of quantitative analysis in SPSS and the three levels of analysis.">
Data coding (a codebook gives each variable an ID, label, numeric codes for its attributes and a measurement level); data entry (defining variables and value labels, entering the questionnaires); data cleaning (running frequencies to catch errors such as 22 for 2, and checking outliers and missing values). Univariate analysis describes one variable (frequencies, mean, median, mode, range, standard deviation); bivariate analysis relates two variables (cross-tabulation, correlation); multivariate analysis relates several at once (e.g. age, sex and institution on job satisfaction), presented in contingency tables.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="6. Distinguish parametric from non-parametric statistics and give three pairs of equivalent tests.">
Parametric tests assume normally distributed scores on continuous (interval or ratio) variables and are more powerful — Pearson correlation, t-tests, ANOVA, regression. Non-parametric tests have less stringent requirements, suit nominal and ordinal data and small samples, but are less powerful. Pairs: Pearson r → Spearman's rho; independent t-test → Mann–Whitney U; paired t-test → Wilcoxon signed rank; one-way ANOVA → Kruskal–Wallis; repeated-measures ANOVA → Friedman. Chi-square (two categorical variables, expected cell counts of at least 5) is itself non-parametric.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="7. Explain content analysis and the steps in qualitative data analysis.">
Content analysis studies recorded communications (newspapers, books, speeches) to find their underlying meanings — who says what, to whom, why, how and with what effect. Pawson's four approaches are formal (numerical counts), thematic (ideological bias), textual (semiotics — linguistic signs) and audience analysis. Steps: open coding (naming concepts), axial coding (re-coding into analytical concepts), selective coding (the central code), memoing (notes and interpretations), and optional concept mapping. Patterns are sought in frequencies, magnitudes, structures, processes, causes and consequences; inductive cross-case analysis leads to grounded theory (Glaser and Strauss). Software includes NVIVO, ATLAS.ti and The Ethnograph.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Comte** first developed sociology as **"social physics"**; **Weber**: understanding **social action**. **Methodology** divides **positivists** (quantitative) from **interpretivists** (qualitative); **pluralism** = triangulation, complementarity, facilitation.
- **Data** = records of observations (raw); **information** = processed data. **Data collection** is a fundamental stage.
- Data types: **quantitative/qualitative**, **primary/secondary**.
- **Questionnaire**: open vs closed (Likert); face-to-face, postal (under 50% return), telephone, e-mail, internet; watch for double-barrelled items, **reactivity** and **interviewer bias**.
- **Interview**: structured/unstructured; **IDI**, **KII**, **FGD (7–12)**.
- **Observation** (one of the best methods): participant (overt or covert; ethnography, **Malinowski**) vs non-participant.
- **Secondary**: official statistics (NBS, NPC; **Durkheim's suicide data**), historical documents (Weber on capitalism), life documents, mass media.
- **Quantitative analysis (SPSS)**: coding (**codebook**) → entry → **cleaning** → **univariate** (frequencies, mean/median/mode, range, SD) → **bivariate** → **multivariate**; **parametric** vs **non-parametric**; Cronbach's alpha above .7; chi-square cells at least 5.
- **Qualitative analysis**: **content analysis** (formal, thematic, textual, audience — Pawson); Lofland's six patterns; **open → axial → selective coding**, **memoing**, concept mapping; **grounded theory** (Glaser and Strauss); NVIVO, ATLAS.ti.
</Takeaway>
