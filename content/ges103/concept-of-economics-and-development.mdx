## The Subject Matter of Economics

Economics traces its origins to **moral philosophy, law and religion**. It became a **distinct discipline** with **Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776)**.

Economists disagree on many definitions, but there is **consensus that scarcity is the subject matter of economics**:

- At any time, **human wants are unlimited**, while the **means (resources)** to satisfy them are **limited**.
- So **choices must be made**.
- Economic theory assumes that individuals choose **rationally**. It also recognises that decisions are affected by family background, socio-cultural environment and peer groups.
- **Choosing one want means giving up others** — the idea of **opportunity cost**.

### Production, consumption, distribution and exchange

The **"means"** side of economics is **production** (creating goods and services). The **"wants"** side is **consumption** (using them up).

**Production** raises four questions:

1. **What** commodities are to be produced?
2. **What quantity** of resources is to be committed?
3. **How** are they to be produced — what **mixture of capital and labour**?
4. **How is production to be organised?**

**Consumption** can be classified in three ways:

| Distinction | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **For further production vs final** | Some consumption creates new goods (capital, material and labour inputs are "used up" in production); **final consumption** ends the good without creating another | Flour used by a bakery vs bread eaten at home |
| **Durable vs non-durable** | How **long** the good gives satisfaction | Cars and electronics vs food and daily needs |
| **Necessity vs luxury** | Crucial to **survival and basic welfare**, or avoidable without threat | Food, shelter vs jewellery |

**The law of demand:** economic theory proposes an **inverse relationship** between the **price** of a commodity and the **quantity demanded** — **the higher the price, the lower the quantity demanded**.

- A change in the good's **own price** causes a **movement along** the demand curve.
- Other factors **shift** the whole curve:
  - the prices of **related goods** — **substitutes** or **complements**;
  - the consumer's **income**;
  - **taxes** on goods and services;
  - **tastes and preferences**.

**Distribution and exchange** connect production and consumption, which are often separated in two ways:

- **In space** — recharge cards are **produced** in a few cities but **consumed** all over Nigeria.
- **In time** — crops are **harvested seasonally** but **eaten all year**.

Distribution and exchange **synchronise** production and consumption. They raise questions such as: *which part of production is to be exchanged, and on what terms?*

**Adjusting to demand:**

- A **temporary** rise in demand (e.g. **soft drinks in the dry season**, festive periods) is met by **using more of the installed capacity** — longer hours, **overtime**.
- A **permanent** rise (e.g. from **population growth**) is met by **capital formation** — **new investment** in plants.
- A permanent fall may lead to **relocating or discarding** capacity.

What sets economics apart is its **methodology** — **scientific**, looking at issues in terms of **opportunity costs**. Its scope now includes **institutions** and **environmental consequences** such as **climate change and global warming**, which are treated as **environmental externalities** of production.

Its techniques apply to almost every field, which is why we have:

- agricultural, engineering, monetary and health economics;
- the economics of education, environmental economics;
- international and managerial economics.

## Definitions of Economics

| Economist | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Adam Smith** | *"An inquiry into the **nature and causes of the wealth of nations**"* |
| **J. S. Mill** | *"The **practical science of the production and distribution of wealth**"* |
| **Alfred Marshall** | *"The study of **mankind in the ordinary business of life**"* |
| **Lionel Robbins** (the textbook writes "Prof. L. Robin") | *"A **social science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses**"* — one of the most accepted definitions |
| **Modern economists** | *"The study of how man **allocates scarce resources, which have alternative uses**, to achieve given ends"*; or *"how **limited resources** are used to try to satisfy **unlimited wants**"*; or *"the study of how **choices** are made to cope with **scarcity**"* |

Combining these, economics is **a social science** dealing with:

- the **creation of wealth** from scarce resources;
- the **production and distribution** of goods and services for consumption;
- the **behaviour, interaction and welfare** of the groups involved;
- the **trade-offs** in production and consumption.

### Economics as a science

**Science** is **the systematic study of how the world works**. Economics is a science because it establishes **generalisations about economic relationships** and uses them to explain how economies perform. The method runs like this:

1. It uses **models** — **simplified representations of reality** that focus on the issues being examined.
2. It starts by **observing regularities**.
3. It explains them with **generalisations**.
4. It derives **hypotheses** (testable implications).
5. It **tests** the hypotheses against **facts and data**.

| Positive economics | Normative economics |
|---|---|
| **What is** — tested propositions that are **confirmed or refuted** by facts | **What ought to be** — based on the **value judgement** of the observer |
| **Free of value judgements**; statements are in principle **true or false** | Asks whether an outcome or policy is **desirable** and how to achieve the **"best"** outcome |
| *"Raising the fuel price by 50% reduced petrol consumption by 10%."* | *"Government should remove the fuel subsidy."* |

**Economics as a social science:** human behaviour is characterised by **choices**. Because behaviour can be **erratic**, economists study it **ceteris paribus** — **"other things being equal"**. They assume individuals are **rational**: they set goals, weigh available information, and choose the alternative with the **greatest net gain**. Hence the **economic person** — a theoretical abstraction of a person acting **solely to maximise profit or benefit**.

## Basic Concepts

### Economic agents

The **economy** is the **mechanism through which scarce resources are organised to produce goods and services**. It has **three decision-making units**:

| Agent | Role | Objective |
|---|---|---|
| **Households** | Assumed (1) to **consume** goods and services, and (2) to **own the factors of production** — **land, labour, capital and entrepreneurship** | **Maximise utility** (satisfaction) |
| **Firms** | **Produce** goods and services, **buying factor services** from households | **Maximise profit** |
| **Government** | Provides the **framework of rules and laws**; takes part in **production** to varying degrees; **redistributes** resources | **Improve collective welfare** |

### Needs, wants, means, scarcity and choice

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Needs** | Goods and services **essential for life** |
| **Wants** | Goods and services desired to **improve the quality of life**, not necessarily essential. They are **insatiable** — people always prefer more |
| **Means** | **Resources** that satisfy wants — **time, money or materials** (a student needs **time and books** to prepare for an exam) |
| **Scarcity** | The condition in which resources, goods and services — **however plentiful** — are **not sufficient for all who desire them**. "Scarce" means **limited in supply relative to wants**, not "rarely found in nature". The imbalance between **unlimited wants and limited resources** is **the basic economic problem** |
| **Choice** | **Scarcity forces choice** — the range of options open to a household, firm or government. It leads to **economising behaviour**: choosing the **best use** of resources. Choices depend on **tastes/preferences** (not observable) and **opportunities/constraints** (measurable) |
| **Opportunity cost** | **The value of the most desirable alternative forgone** when making a choice — *not* what is chosen, but **what is given up**. E.g. government spends its budget on **roads rather than schools**. Opportunity cost is **why goods are not free** |
| **Scale of preference** | **The list of wants arranged in order of priority**, from the most to the least pressing — the order in which a person will satisfy them one after another |

### Efficiency

Economic agents respond to **incentives**. They weigh **costs against benefits** — the **net gain**. An **economically efficient choice maximises net gain**: **maximum benefit for a given cost**, or **minimum cost for a given benefit**. There are two types:

| Productive efficiency | Allocative efficiency |
|---|---|
| **Maximum output from available inputs** — no resources wasted | Using resources to produce the goods of **highest possible value** |

Together they answer the basic economic questions: **what** to produce, **how** to produce, and **for whom** to produce.

## Branches of Economics

| Microeconomics | Macroeconomics |
|---|---|
| The study of **individuals, households, firms and markets** | The study of **the economy as a whole** |
| How households and firms make **consumption and production decisions**, and the **market structures** in which they interact | The determinants of **total output** and the **general price level** |
| Analysis is mainly **partial** — everything else is held **fixed** except the market studied | **Economic growth, inflation, exchange rates** and the **business cycle** (ups and downs of total output and employment) |

## Economic Systems

Countries solve the basic economic problems through different **institutional frameworks**:

| System | Features | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Capitalist (market economy)** | **Free enterprise**; resources allocated by the **price mechanism** — the forces of **demand and supply**. Households and firms answer the basic questions; the state's role is **absent or minimal** (laws and order) | **United States** |
| **Socialist (planned / command economy)** | The **state dominates** economic decisions. The **means of production are owned and controlled by the state**; a **central planning authority** sets production targets and distributes goods — **it replaces the price mechanism** | The defunct **USSR** |
| **Communist** | An **advanced stage of socialism** — **all means of production are owned by the masses**; promotes an **equal economic base** and discourages **economic classes** | — |
| **Mixed** | **Combines** market forces and state control. The price mechanism operates, and government intervenes to correct **market failure** | **Most countries**, especially developing ones — **Nigeria, Ghana** |

The **driving force of a capitalist economy** is the **price mechanism**, powered by the **profit motive**.

### Market failure and government failure *(beyond the textbook, but examined)*

**Market failure** is a **breakdown of the price system**: left alone, the market fails to allocate resources efficiently. Its common causes:

| Cause | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Externalities** | **Costs or benefits not fully covered by prices** — they fall on third parties | A factory's pollution (negative); a neighbour's vaccination (positive) |
| **Public goods** | Non-rival and non-excludable, so private firms cannot charge for them (the **free-rider** problem) | Street lights, national defence |
| **Merit goods** | Goods whose **benefits are underestimated by consumers**, so too little is consumed | Education, health care, immunisation |
| **Demerit goods** | Goods whose **harms are underestimated**, so too much is consumed | Cigarettes, hard drugs |
| **Monopoly power** and **imperfect information** | Prices and output are distorted | A sole supplier of a service |

**Government failure** results when **government intervenes to correct market failure and the intervention creates another set of problems** — waste, corruption, distorted prices, or shortages under price controls.

## Economic Growth and Economic Development

**Economic growth does not necessarily mean economic development, but development presupposes growth.** Growth is a **subset** of development — development is **far more** than growth.

### Economic growth

**Todaro (2015)** defines economic growth as *"a **steady process** by which the **productive capacity of the economy is increased over time** to bring about **rising levels of national income**"*. Two things are primary:

- the increase must be **sustained** over an appreciable period;
- it must come from **expanded productive capacity** that is permanent.

| Measure | Definition |
|---|---|
| **GDP — Gross Domestic Product** | The total value of **final output produced within an economy**, by **residents and non-residents** |
| **GNP — Gross National Product** | The total domestic **and foreign** value added claimed by a country's **residents**, without deducting depreciation. **GNP = GDP + factor income received from abroad − factor income paid to non-residents** |
| **Per capita** | The value **per person** (per head) — the measure divided by the population. **Per capita income** is also called **income per head** |

If **population grows faster than the economy**, GDP can rise while **per capita GDP falls**. That is why economists care about **changes in per capita GNP or GDP**. The sheet below uses hypothetical figures:

### Economic development

**Traditionally**, development was the capacity of a static economy to generate and **sustain an annual increase in national output of about 5% or more**. It has been **redefined** as the **reduction or elimination of poverty, inequality and unemployment** within a growing economy. **Development occurs only if all three have significantly declined.**

Development also goes beyond physical reality. It includes the **transformation of the state of mind** of society and **structural transformation** of the economy, society and culture. It is:

> "a **multidimensional process** involving major changes in **social structures, popular attitudes and national institutions**, as well as the **acceleration of economic growth**, the **reduction of inequality** and the **eradication of poverty**."

In short: the **sustained elevation of an entire society towards a "better" or "more humane" life**.

### The Human Development Index (HDI)

To overcome the shortcomings of per capita income, the **United Nations Development Programme (UNDP)** developed the **HDI**. It focuses on **human development** — **enlarging people's choices**. It combines three indices:

1. **Life expectancy at birth** (a long and healthy life);
2. **Educational attainment** — **mean years of schooling and adult literacy**;
3. **Standard of living** — **per capita income**.

*(Beyond the textbook: since 2010 the UNDP measures education by **mean and expected years of schooling**, and living standards by **GNI per capita (PPP)**.)*

Building the index involves three steps:

- **separating ends from means** when picking indicators;
- **translating indicators to a common denominator**;
- **weighting** them in a composite index.

**Objectives of development:**

1. **Increase the availability and widen the distribution** of basic life-sustaining goods — **food, shelter, health and protection**.
2. **Raise levels of living** — higher incomes, **more jobs**, **better education**, and attention to cultural and humanistic values. This enhances material well-being and generates **individual and national self-esteem**.
3. **Expand the range of economic and social choices**, freeing people and nations from **servitude and dependence**, ignorance and misery.

### Common characteristics of developing countries

| Characteristic | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Low levels of living** | Low incomes, **high inequality**, poor health, inadequate education. **Absolute poverty** = income below the minimum needed for basic physical needs. **Relative poverty** = income of the poor relative to the national average. Malnutrition, disease and illiteracy are common |
| **Low capital accumulation** | Low **savings and investment** lead to low productivity and poverty |
| **Low labour productivity** | Lack of **complementary inputs** such as physical capital. **Law of diminishing marginal productivity**: as more of a variable factor is added to a fixed factor (land, capital), its extra output eventually **declines** |
| **High population growth and dependency burden** | The labour force (**ages 15–64**) must support many **children and older people** — the **economic dependency burden** |
| **High unemployment and underemployment** | **Underemployment**: working less than one could, or so unproductively that cutting hours barely affects output. **Open unemployment**: able, willing and eager to work, but **no suitable jobs** |
| **Dependence on agriculture and primary exports** | Production and exports dominated by **primary products** (cash crops, crude oil, raw materials) rather than **manufacturing (secondary)** and **services (tertiary)**. Little value added; peasant farming dominates; a **very small share of world trade** |

## The MDGs and the SDGs

### Millennium Development Goals (2000–2015)

The MDGs came out of the **UN Millennium Summit, September 2000**. There were **eight goals** with specific **targets** (18 in the textbook's count), to be achieved by **2015**:

| Goal | Targets |
|---|---|
| 1. Eradicate **extreme poverty and hunger** | 2 |
| 2. Achieve **universal primary education** | 1 |
| 3. Promote **gender equality** and empower women | 1 |
| 4. Reduce **child mortality** | 1 |
| 5. Improve **maternal health** | 1 |
| 6. Combat **HIV/AIDS, malaria and other diseases** | 2 |
| 7. Ensure **environmental sustainability** | 3 |
| 8. Develop a **global partnership for development** — the **means** to achieve the other seven | 7 |

*(The student summary misprints goal 5 as "mental health" and goal 7 as "environmental sanitation" — they are **maternal** health and environmental **sustainability**.)*

### Sustainable Development Goals (2016–2030)

The SDGs are officially titled **"Transforming our World"** (built on **"The Future We Want"**). They have **17 global goals with 169 targets**, agreed by the UN's **193 member states** as the **post-2015 development agenda**. They go beyond ending extreme poverty to balance the **three dimensions of sustainable development — economic, social and environmental**. The target date is **2030**.

| # | Goal (targets) | # | Goal (targets) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **End poverty** in all its forms everywhere (7) | 10 | **Reduce inequality** within and among countries (10) |
| 2 | **End hunger**, food security, nutrition, sustainable agriculture (8) | 11 | **Sustainable cities** and human settlements (10) |
| 3 | **Healthy lives** and well-being for all at all ages (13) | 12 | Sustainable **consumption and production** (11) |
| 4 | Inclusive, equitable **quality education** and lifelong learning (10) | 13 | Urgent action on **climate change** (5) |
| 5 | **Gender equality**; empower all women and girls (9) | 14 | Conserve **oceans, seas and marine resources** (10) |
| 6 | **Clean water and sanitation** for all (8) | 15 | **Life on land** — forests, desertification, biodiversity (12) |
| 7 | Affordable, reliable, sustainable, **modern energy** (5) | 16 | **Peace, justice and strong institutions** (12) |
| 8 | Sustained growth, **decent work** for all (12) | 17 | **Partnerships** — means of implementation (19) |
| 9 | **Infrastructure, industrialisation and innovation** (8) | | |

**Conclusion:** growth and development are **related but different**. Growth concerns **national productivity**. Development extends to **human welfare, income distribution, education, health and freedom**. **Development is impossible without growth, but growth can occur without development.**

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. Explain the subject matter of economics and the basic questions it tries to answer.">
The subject matter is scarcity: human wants are unlimited while the resources to satisfy them are limited, so choices must be made, each carrying an opportunity cost. On the production side it asks what commodities to produce, what quantity of resources to commit, how to produce them (the mix of capital and labour), and how to organise production. On the consumption side it studies types of consumption and consumer behaviour (the law of demand). Distribution and exchange connect production and consumption across space and time.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Define economics according to four scholars and explain why economics is a science.">
Smith: an inquiry into the nature and causes of the wealth of nations. Mill: the practical science of the production and distribution of wealth. Marshall: the study of mankind in the ordinary business of life. Robbins: a social science studying human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means with alternative uses. It is a science because it observes regularities, builds generalisations and models, derives hypotheses and tests them against data (positive economics). Normative economics deals with value judgements about what ought to be.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Explain scarcity, choice, opportunity cost and scale of preference with examples.">
Scarcity: resources are insufficient relative to all wants — the basic economic problem. Choice: scarcity forces us to decide how to use resources, guided by tastes and constraints. Opportunity cost: the value of the best alternative forgone, e.g. a government building roads instead of schools. Scale of preference: wants ranked from most to least pressing and satisfied in that order — the first want left unsatisfied is the opportunity cost.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Compare the four economic systems.">
Capitalist (market): free enterprise, price mechanism, minimal state (USA). Socialist (command): state ownership and a central planning authority replacing the price mechanism (USSR). Communist: an advanced socialism with all means of production owned by the masses and no economic classes. Mixed: market forces plus state intervention to correct market failure (Nigeria, Ghana, most countries).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="5. Distinguish economic growth from economic development and explain how each is measured.">
Growth is a steady increase in the productive capacity of the economy that raises national income (Todaro), measured by GDP and GNP, preferably per capita, since population growth can cancel out total growth. Development is broader: growth plus a significant reduction in poverty, inequality and unemployment, and structural, social and attitudinal transformation towards a more humane life. It is measured by the UNDP's Human Development Index (life expectancy, education, income per head). Growth can occur without development, but not development without growth.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="6. State six characteristics of developing countries.">
Low levels of living (low income, high inequality, poor health and education, absolute and relative poverty); low capital accumulation (low savings and investment); low labour productivity (lack of complementary capital, diminishing marginal productivity); high population growth and dependency burden (many dependants per worker aged 15–64); high unemployment and underemployment; and dependence on agriculture and primary-product exports, with little value added and a tiny share of world trade.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="7. Compare the MDGs and the SDGs.">
The MDGs (Millennium Summit, 2000) had eight goals, 18 targets in the textbook's count, and a deadline of 2015. They focused on poverty, primary education, gender, child and maternal health, disease, environmental sustainability and global partnership. The SDGs ("Transforming our World", 193 member states) have 17 goals and 169 targets for 2030. They extend the MDGs to balance the economic, social and environmental dimensions of sustainable development — climate, oceans, land, cities, energy, decent work, inequality, and peace and justice.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- Economics began as a discipline with **Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776)**; its subject matter is **scarcity** (unlimited wants, limited means) → **choice** → **opportunity cost**.
- Definitions: Smith (wealth of nations), Mill (production and distribution of wealth), Marshall (ordinary business of life), **Robbins (ends and scarce means with alternative uses)**.
- **Law of demand**: price up → quantity demanded down; own price = movement along the curve; income, related prices, taxes, tastes = shift.
- **Positive** (what is) vs **normative** (what ought to be); **ceteris paribus**; the **economic person**.
- **Agents**: households (utility; own land, labour, capital, entrepreneurship), firms (profit), government (rules, redistribution).
- **Scale of preference** = ranked wants; **efficiency** = productive + allocative.
- **Micro** (individual units, markets) vs **macro** (whole economy: growth, inflation, exchange rates, business cycle).
- **Systems**: capitalist (price mechanism, USA), socialist (central planning, USSR), communist, **mixed (Nigeria)**; market failure (externalities, public/merit goods) and **government failure**.
- **Growth** (Todaro; GDP, GNP, per capita) vs **development** (less poverty, inequality, unemployment; **HDI**).
- **MDGs**: 8 goals, 2000–2015. **SDGs**: 17 goals, 169 targets, 193 states, 2030.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Match **definition → economist** (Smith, Mill, Marshall, Robbins) — a regular MCQ.
- Be clear that **opportunity cost is the best alternative forgone**, not the item chosen.
- Know the **three components of the HDI** and the **six characteristics** of developing countries.
- Remember the **numbers**: 8 MDGs (2015) vs **17 SDGs, 169 targets** (2030); labour force ages **15–64**.
- Use the formula **GNP = GDP + factor income from abroad − factor income paid abroad** in calculation questions, and always consider **per capita** figures.
