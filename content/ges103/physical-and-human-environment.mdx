## The Environment

**Environment** comes from the French ***environner*** — **"to encircle or surround"**. It means (Cunningham and Saigo 1999):

1. the **circumstances or conditions that surround an organism** or group of organisms; or
2. the **complex of social or cultural conditions** that affect an individual or community.

Its components are:

- **natural elements**:
  - **climate** — temperature, humidity, precipitation;
  - **terrain** — mountains, hills, plains, valleys, swamps;
  - **vegetation, soils, rivers**;
- **cultural elements** — **settlements, roads, farms**.

The **impact of human interference** depends on **both the environment and the humans** involved.

**Environmental science** studies the **interaction between humans and the natural environment**. It is **interdisciplinary**, integrating the **natural sciences, social sciences and humanities**. Understanding the environment is essential for a **healthy, rational relationship** between people and nature, and for using resources **sustainably**.

### The environmental system

The **environmental system** is the system of the **biosphere**, where **life interacts** with the **abiotic (non-living)** components of the **atmosphere, hydrosphere and lithosphere**.

- **Energy moves** from abiotic to **biotic** components — e.g. through **photosynthesis**.
- Energy also drives abiotic processes: **wind**, **weathering** of rock into soil, **precipitation**, and **tectonic uplift** of highlands.

**Why study it?** To:

- maintain **renewable resources** (timber, fish);
- conserve **non-renewable resources** (minerals);
- understand **natural hazards** (earthquakes, floods, windstorms) and reduce their impact;
- understand the environment's **components and processes**;
- understand the **impacts of development**, and how to **mitigate human impact**.

**The systems approach:** a **system** is **a set of interconnected parts which function together as a complex whole** (Smithson et al. 2008). Systems analysis stresses the **structure** of the environment and the **relationships** between and within its parts. It concerns biologists, biogeographers, botanists and zoologists, collectively called **environmentalists**.

## The Atmosphere

The **atmosphere** is the **layer of gases (air) surrounding the Earth**, held by **gravity**.

**Its importance:**

1. It contains the **air we breathe**.
2. It creates **pressure** that lets **liquid water** exist on the surface.
3. It **absorbs ultraviolet** solar radiation.
4. It **warms the surface** by retaining heat — the **greenhouse effect**.
5. It **reduces temperature extremes** between day and night (the **diurnal temperature variation**).

**Size and composition:** the atmosphere is about **480 km thick**, but **most of it lies within 16 km** of the surface. Its gases are **mixed, not chemically combined**:

| Gas | Share by volume |
|---|---|
| **Nitrogen** | **78%** |
| **Oxygen** | **21%** |
| **Argon** | **0.93%** |
| **Carbon dioxide** | **0.04%** |
| Neon, helium, methane, krypton, hydrogen | Traces |
| **Water vapour** | Variable — about **1% at sea level**, 0.4% over the whole atmosphere |

- Up to about **80 km** the composition is **uniform** — the **homosphere**. Above it, composition **varies** — the **heterosphere** (Ajayi 2013).
- **Air pressure and density decrease with altitude.**

### Five layers

The atmosphere is **layered by temperature**. It thins upward until the gases dissipate into space. There is **no sharp boundary** with space, but the **Kármán line**, about **100 km (62 miles)** up, is taken as its edge.

![The five layers of the atmosphere from the ground up: troposphere to about 12 km with weather and falling temperature; stratosphere to about 50 km with the ozone layer and rising temperature; mesosphere to about 85 km, the coldest layer where meteors burn up; thermosphere to about 600 km with auroras, satellites and the Kármán line at 100 km; and the exosphere where gases escape into space](/images/ges103/atmosphere-layers.svg)

*Layers of the atmosphere (drawn for this lesson; heights are approximate and not to scale).*

**The troposphere** is the most important for **life on Earth**:

- It is **7 to 20 km thick** — thinnest at the poles, thickest at the equator.
- It contains most of the atmosphere's mass — the textbook says **half**; the usual figure is about **75–80%**.
- **Temperature falls with altitude**, because the troposphere is **heated from the surface**. The average fall is about **6.5 °C per km** — the **environmental lapse rate**. It varies with **location and season**.
- In the **stratosphere** above, **temperature rises with altitude**.

## The Lithosphere

The **lithosphere** is the **rigid outer shell** of the Earth. It consists of:

- the **crust** — **cool, rigid and brittle**;
- the **uppermost part of the mantle**.

It is bounded by the **atmosphere above** and the **asthenosphere** (a soft, plastic part of the upper mantle) **below**. Its rock originated as **molten magma** from the interior.

![Cross-section of half the Earth showing the thin crust, the thick mantle, the liquid outer core and the solid inner core, with depths: crust to about 35 km, mantle to about 2,890 km, outer core to 5,150 km, inner core to the centre at about 6,371 km](/images/ges103/earth-interior.svg)

*Layers of the Earth's interior (drawn for this lesson; the crust is exaggerated). The textbook gives the mantle as extending **35–2,895 km** deep and the core **2,890–6,360 km**. These are **depths** below the surface. The core's own **radius** is about **3,480 km**, which is why test options quote the mantle and core as "2,895 km and 3,475 km".*

| Feature | Oceanic lithosphere | Continental lithosphere |
|---|---|---|
| **Crust thickness** | **5–10 km** | **20–70 km** |
| **Rock** | **Basalt** | Mainly lighter **granite** |
| **Density** | About **3.0 g/cm³** | About **2.7 g/cm³** |

More facts about the lithosphere:

- It is about **100 km thick** — **thickest below continents**, **thinnest at mid-ocean ridges and rift valleys**, where plates move apart and new seafloor forms.
- It is broken into **tectonic plates** that float on the mantle. **Convection currents** in the mantle move them slowly across the asthenosphere. The **movement of the lithosphere** is called **plate tectonics**.
- It is the zone of **earthquakes, mountain building, volcanoes and continental drift**.
- It includes the **continents, ocean basins, rocks and minerals**.
- The **pedosphere** is the top of the lithosphere that **reacts chemically** with the atmosphere, hydrosphere and biosphere through **soil formation**. Only about **1 km** of the lithosphere interacts with the biosphere (notably through **aquifers**).
- **Main elements of the crust:**
  - **oxygen 47%**, **silicon 28%**;
  - **aluminium 8%**, **iron 5%**;
  - **calcium 4%**, **sodium 3%**, **potassium 3%**, **magnesium 2%**.
- It is the **main source of pollutants** — raw materials are extracted from it — and a **permanent accumulator** of them. Some are released **naturally** (volcanic eruptions); others by **human extraction and burning** (fossil fuels).

## The Hydrosphere

The **hydrosphere** is **all the water on Earth in all its states** — solid, liquid and gas — plus the elements dissolved in it (sodium, magnesium, calcium, chloride, sulphate) (Rodrigue 2017). It includes:

- **oceans and seas**;
- **rivers and lakes**;
- **underground water**;
- water in the **atmosphere**.

**Water covers about 71% of the Earth's surface**; continents and islands cover **29%**. The hydrosphere **accumulates pollutants** and **spreads** them.

**Earth's water budget** (UNESCO 1998): about **1.386 billion km³** of water in all.

- **97.5% is salt water** in the oceans; average salinity is about **35 g of salt per kg** of seawater (**3.5%**).
- **Only 2.5% is fresh water**.

### The hydrological cycle

The **hydrological (water) cycle** moves water from one **state or reservoir** to another.

- **Reservoirs:**
  - **atmospheric moisture** (clouds, rain, snow);
  - **oceans**, **rivers and streams**, **lakes**;
  - **groundwater** and **subterranean aquifers**;
  - **polar ice caps**;
  - **saturated soil**.
- **Drivers:** **solar energy** (heat and light — **insolation**) and **gravity**, over periods from **hours to thousands of years**.
- **Most evaporation comes from the oceans** and returns as **rain or snow**.
- **Photosynthesis** occurs mainly at the **ocean surface**, where there is light — important for the **food supply** of organisms.

## The Biosphere

The **biosphere** is **the layer of the Earth where life exists**. It is **unique** — no life has yet been found elsewhere in the universe. Its study is the basis of **ecology**, the study of life and its interactions with the physical environment.

- **Life depends on the sun**:
  - **plants capture sunlight** in **photosynthesis**, turning **carbon dioxide** into **sugars** and releasing **oxygen**;
  - most animals, fungi, parasitic plants and many bacteria depend on photosynthesis **directly or indirectly**.
- **Where life is found:**
  - most **land life** lives between **3 m below ground and 30 m above it**;
  - most **aquatic life** lives from the **surface to about 200 m** deep.
- **Biomes** are areas with plants and animals of a similar nature — e.g. a **desert**, a **coral reef**.

### Ecosystems

An **ecosystem** is a **community of living organisms and non-living components** (air, water, mineral soil) that **interact with one another** and with their physical environment. It has three components:

1. **Biotic** — **living organisms**;
2. **Abiotic** — the **non-living** physical environment;
3. **Dead organic matter** on the ground, in the soil or in water.

| Terrestrial ecosystem | Aquatic ecosystem | Man-made ecosystem |
|---|---|---|
| **On land**; dominated by **higher (flowering) plants** — the flora, with the fauna | **In water bodies**; characterised by **small plants such as phytoplankton** | E.g. the **city**, where natural plants and organisms are greatly disturbed by human activities |

## The Human Environment

The **human environment** is **the complex continuum of matter and conditions that surround humans and human society**. It is about people living in different environments, their **activities**, and the **features they have created**.

The relationship is **two-way**:

- humans **change the environment**;
- the changed environment **shapes human perceptions and behaviour**.

A society's impact depends on its:

- **population size** and **social organisation**;
- **values**;
- **technology**;
- **wealth**, **education** and **knowledge**.

**Settlements** are **rural or urban**, classified by **population size, economic activities and spatial characteristics**. That is how **a city is differentiated from a rural settlement**.

**Spatial organisation** is **the aggregate pattern of the use of space**. In an area such as a local government, the towns, villages, farms, roads, markets, schools and hospitals each occupy specific locations. Together they show how space is organised there.

### Economic activities

Activities that **generate income** are **economic activities**:

| Level | What it involves | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | **Extracting and using the Earth's resources** — directly dependent on the environment | Hunting and gathering, **pastoralism, fishing, forestry, agriculture, mining and quarrying** |
| **Secondary** | **Adding value** by transforming raw materials into valuable products | **Manufacturing, processing, construction** |
| **Tertiary** | **Services**: **production** of services that are consumed, and **exchange** — **trade, transport and communication** | Retailing, markets, transport, banking, health care |
| **Quaternary** | Specialised tertiary activities in the **"knowledge sector"** — information-based; can be **outsourced**; not tied to resources | Fund managers, tax consultants, **software developers**, statisticians, teachers, doctors' offices |
| **Quinary** | **Creating, re-arranging and interpreting ideas**, data and new technologies — the **"gold-collar"** professions of the highest decision-makers | Senior executives, top **government officials**, **research scientists**, financial and legal consultants |

**Agriculture** is shaped by four kinds of factor:

- **natural** — climate (especially temperature and rainfall), topography, soil;
- **economic** — market, transport, labour, capital;
- **social** — land ownership and inheritance, crops grown, farming systems;
- **political** — e.g. **government-controlled** farming in **China** vs **private** farming in the **USA and Canada**; policies on land, irrigation, marketing, trade, subsidies, loans and tax.

### Location theories

| Theory | Key idea |
|---|---|
| **J. H. von Thünen (1826)** — agricultural land use | Land goes to the **highest bidder** among crops and farming systems — if cassava pays more than yam on a plot, cassava is grown there. The key concept is **economic rent**: the **surplus** obtained from a **better plot** over a poorer one with the same inputs |
| **Alfred Weber (1909)** — **least cost** | The best industrial location is where **total transport cost per unit of output is lowest**; otherwise transport disadvantages are offset by **agglomeration economies** and **cheap labour** |
| **August Lösch (1939)** — **profit maximisation / market area** | The best location **monopolises the largest possible market area** — maximum **sales and revenue** |
| **Allan Pred (1967)** — **behavioural** | The entrepreneur is a **"satisficer"**, not a perfect optimiser. **Information** and **ability to act** decide whether locations are more or less successful |
| **Walter Christaller** — **Central Place Theory** | How **cities provide market functions** to their **hinterlands**. The textbook dates it 1925; it was **published in 1933** |
| **Stine (1962)** — **periodic markets** | Based on central place theory: **rural markets meet periodically** because of **poor storage, poor transport and low population density**. Periodicity **concentrates demand** so traders survive, and **reduces the distance** buyers travel |

Industrial location also depends on:

- **capital**, **government policy** and **site requirements**;
- **historical factors**;
- **transport and communications**, **raw materials**, **energy**, **labour** and **market**;
- **water for cooling**, **political stability** and an attractive locality;
- increasingly, **globalisation** and **multinational companies**.

**Urban markets operate daily**; most **rural markets** in developing countries are **periodic**. The textbook gives intervals of **three, seven or nine days**. *(Beyond the textbook: in southern Nigeria the traditional market week is **four days** — e.g. the Igbo **Eke, Orie, Afọ, Nkwọ** — so many markets meet every 4 or 8 days.)*

## Human Impact on the Environment

### Two views of the human–environment relationship

| Environmental determinism | Possibilism |
|---|---|
| **Man is a product of the environment** — a **passive** creature **moulded by natural forces**; the environment is **predominant** | Man has **freedom of choice** within given environmental conditions. The environment offers a **range of opportunities** to which man reacts, and man **influences** the environment by exploiting them |
| Dominant from classical times; seemed confirmed by **Charles Darwin's *Origin of Species*** (man as a creature **adapted** to his environment) | **More acceptable today**, because man exploits the environment's possibilities to meet his needs |

### Population: the driving force

**Population** is **the total number of people living in an area at a specific time**. It is described by:

- **absolute size** (number);
- **density** (the man–land ratio).

Population changes through:

- **natural increase** — **births minus deaths**;
- **migration** — **immigration minus emigration** (a permanent change of residence).

**Globally, migration is unimportant**, because all movements stay within the world. **The greater the population, the greater the impact** on the environment and the demand for resources.

As population grows:

- more land goes to **agriculture**;
- resource use and **exploitation technology** increase;
- more **industrial waste, pesticides, fertilisers and household waste** is produced;
- more people in **cities** means more **carbon dioxide emissions** and **global warming**.

| Destructive impacts | Positive impacts |
|---|---|
| **Pollution** of land, air and water from **fossil fuels, vehicle exhausts** and **factory effluents** | **Afforestation** — trees improve **air quality** |
| **Overconsumption** — resource use outpacing the ecosystem's **sustainable capacity**, leading to **degradation** and loss of the resource base | **Water conservation** — no dumping of waste in rivers; **rainwater harvesting, watershed management, drip irrigation** |
| **Hunting** and **introduced predators** → **extinction** of species | **Soil conservation** — **contour ploughing, terracing, crop rotation, windbreaks, shelter belts** |
| **Mining** → **erosion, sinkholes**, loss of **biodiversity**, contaminated **soil and water** | **Conserving flora and fauna** — **national parks**, botanical gardens, wildlife sanctuaries, biosphere reserves |
| **Transport** — a major **energy (petroleum)** user; **nitrous oxides, particulates**, **CO₂** and global warming | **Renewable energy** — solar, wind, biofuels |
| **Deforestation** for farms, settlements and infrastructure → **erosion**, lost **habitats and biodiversity**, fewer **oxygen producers and CO₂ absorbers** | Removing **invasive species**; enforcing **environmental laws**; **selective breeding** (better yields); **domestication** of animals |
| — | **Introducing new species** — e.g. **rubber** taken from Brazil's **Amazon Basin** to **Malaysia**. *(The textbook calls Malaysia the world's largest producer; it was long the leader, but today **Thailand** produces the most.)* |

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. Define the environment and explain the importance of studying the environmental system.">
The environment (from French environner, to surround) is the set of conditions surrounding an organism, or the social and cultural conditions affecting a community (Cunningham and Saigo 1999). It includes natural elements (climate, terrain, vegetation, soils, rivers) and cultural elements (settlements, roads, farms). The environmental system is the biosphere interacting with the atmosphere, hydrosphere and lithosphere. We study it to maintain renewable resources, conserve non-renewable ones, understand and reduce natural hazards, understand environmental processes and the impacts of development, and mitigate human impact.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Describe the composition and layers of the atmosphere and explain its importance.">
Composition: nitrogen 78%, oxygen 21%, argon 0.93%, carbon dioxide 0.04%, traces of other gases and variable water vapour. It is uniform up to about 80 km (the homosphere) and variable above (the heterosphere). Layers: troposphere (7–20 km; weather; temperature falls about 6.5 °C per km), stratosphere (ozone; temperature rises), mesosphere, thermosphere (with the Kármán line at 100 km) and exosphere. Importance: air to breathe, pressure for liquid water, absorbing UV, the greenhouse warming effect, and moderating day–night temperature extremes.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Distinguish oceanic from continental lithosphere and explain plate tectonics.">
The lithosphere (about 100 km) is the crust plus the rigid upper mantle, resting on the plastic asthenosphere. Oceanic crust is 5–10 km of basalt at about 3.0 g/cm³; continental crust is 20–70 km of lighter granite at about 2.7 g/cm³. Both are thinnest at mid-ocean ridges and rift valleys. The lithosphere is broken into tectonic plates that mantle convection currents move across the asthenosphere — plate tectonics — producing earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain building and continental drift.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Describe Earth's water distribution and the hydrological cycle.">
There are about 1.386 billion km³ of water: 97.5% salt water in the oceans (salinity about 3.5%) and 2.5% fresh, of which 68.9% is ice and snow, 30.8% groundwater and only 0.3% in lakes and rivers. Water covers 71% of the surface. The hydrological cycle, driven by solar energy and gravity, moves water between reservoirs — the atmosphere, oceans, rivers, lakes, groundwater, aquifers, ice caps and saturated soil. Most evaporation comes from the oceans and returns as rain or snow.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="5. Classify economic activities, with examples.">
Primary: extracting resources — farming, fishing, forestry, mining and quarrying. Secondary: adding value — manufacturing, processing, construction. Tertiary: services and exchange — trade, transport, communication (e.g. petrol retailing). Quaternary: the knowledge sector — software developers, fund managers, tax consultants. Quinary: creating and interpreting ideas and top decision-making — senior executives, top officials, research scientists ("gold collar").
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="6. Discuss the negative and positive impacts of human activities on the environment.">
Population growth drives impact. Negative: pollution from fossil fuels, vehicles and factories; overconsumption beyond sustainable capacity; species extinction through hunting and introduced predators; mining (erosion, sinkholes, contamination); transport emissions and global warming; deforestation (erosion, loss of habitat and carbon sinks). Positive: afforestation; water conservation (rainwater harvesting, drip irrigation); soil conservation (contour ploughing, terracing, crop rotation, windbreaks); parks and reserves; renewable energy; removing invasive species; enforcing environmental laws; selective breeding; domestication; introducing useful species (rubber to Malaysia).
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Environment** (French *environner* — to surround); **environmental science** is interdisciplinary; a **system** is a set of interconnected parts working as a whole.
- **Atmosphere**: N₂ 78%, O₂ 21%, Ar 0.93%, CO₂ 0.04%; homosphere (to 80 km) and heterosphere; **five layers** (troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere, exosphere); Kármán line at 100 km; lapse rate about 6.5 °C/km.
- **Lithosphere**: crust + upper mantle (about 100 km) on the asthenosphere; **oceanic** (basalt, 5–10 km) vs **continental** (granite, 20–70 km); **plate tectonics**; pedosphere; crust O 47%, Si 28%.
- **Hydrosphere**: 71% of the surface; **97.5% salt / 2.5% fresh** (69% ice, 31% groundwater, 0.3% lakes and rivers); the hydrological cycle.
- **Biosphere**: where life exists; **ecosystem** = biotic + abiotic + dead organic matter; terrestrial, aquatic and man-made.
- **Human environment**: a two-way relationship; rural vs urban settlements; **primary → secondary → tertiary → quaternary → quinary**.
- **Theories**: von Thünen (1826, economic rent), Weber (least cost), Lösch (market area), Pred (satisficer), Christaller (central place), Stine (periodic markets).
- **Determinism** vs **possibilism**; population growth drives impact; destructive vs positive impacts.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- **Percentages** are favourite MCQs: 78/21/0.93/0.04 for the air; 71% water surface; 97.5/2.5 salt/fresh; 68.9/30.8/0.3 for fresh water.
- Learn the **five atmospheric layers in order** from the ground up, and which way temperature changes in each.
- Match **activities to their sector** — retailing and transport are **tertiary**; processing and assembly are **secondary**.
- Pair each **location theorist** with a **key phrase**: von Thünen — economic rent; Weber — least cost; Lösch — market area; Pred — satisficer.
