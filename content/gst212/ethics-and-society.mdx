## Ethics and the Good Life

The chapter's author, **Christopher Agulanna**, says ethics is **not just about clarifying moral concepts**. It also shows how to **imbibe ethical principles that enable us to live the good life** — **the life of happiness or flourishing**.

For **Socrates, Plato and Aristotle**, happiness and flourishing come only through **virtue (moral goodness)**. Virtues are **habits** that help us attain the goods of life — **health, strength and knowledge**.

### The four cardinal virtues

The classical philosophers named **four cardinal virtues** — the **basic norms of a virtuous life**:

| Virtue | Meaning | Aims at |
|---|---|---|
| **Temperance** | **Self-control** and moderation of desires and impulses | The individual's **character** |
| **Fortitude** | **Moral courage** — doing right, and enduring hardship, even when it is hard | The individual's **character** |
| **Prudence** | **Practical wisdom** — careful judgement and foresight before acting | The individual's **character** |
| **Justice** | **Fairness** — giving everyone their due | **Other persons' well-being** |

Other virtues that promote others' well-being include **charity, honesty and loyalty**. The opposites of virtues are **vices** — **stealing, lying, cheating**. **Examination malpractice** is a vice that disrupts the moral order of a school. At the **University of Ibadan**, the *Student Information Handbook* warns that a student involved in exam misconduct **may be expelled**.

## What Is Ethics?

Ethics has **no single definition**, but ethicists agree that it is **an inquiry into the nature of morality**. It studies **the principles and standards of human conduct** — and values in general.

**Etymology:** from the Greek ***ethikos***, from the root ***ethos*** — **custom, habit, character or moral disposition**.

Ethics examines what it means to say an action is **right or wrong, good or bad, just or unjust**. It asks:

- **How should we live?**
- What do "right" and "good" mean?
- How can we **measure goodness and badness**?
- What are our **rights and responsibilities**?
- Are some things **always wrong**, or does rightness depend on the **situation**?

Ethics is also called **moral philosophy**, or **the science of morals**.

### Ethics focuses on action

Ethics analyses concepts such as:

- **good/bad**, **right/wrong**, **virtue/vice**;
- **duty/obligation**, **justice/injustice**;
- **moral responsibility**.

It asks what actions we should perform — the most important question of all: ***"How should I lead my life?"***

**Moral responsibility:** when we **blame or praise** someone, we assume **they could have acted otherwise**. Blame and praise make sense only if actions are **free and voluntary**. Hence ethics is also described as **the search for the morally good life**.

## Where Did Ethics Come From?

| Theory of origin | Claim | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| **Religion — the divine command theory** | **God is the author of morality**: good acts are **commanded** by God, bad acts **forbidden**. E.g. the **Ten Commandments** given to **Moses on Mount Sinai**; the **Golden Rule** — *"do unto others what you would have them do unto you."* **Stealing is wrong because God prohibits it** | **Atheists and agnostics** may not accept it |
| **Conscience** | Morality comes from **conscience** — "the complex of ethical and moral principles that controls or inhibits the actions or thoughts of an individual"; an inner **law enforcer** that reprimands or commends (hence *"conscience-stricken"*) | **Not a reliable guide**. Some consciences are **untrained**, or **seared and hardened**: European **slave traders**, and their African collaborators, felt nothing wrong. Consciences **differ between people**, and conscience gives **no principles for settling moral conflicts** |
| **Society** | **Thomas Hobbes:** ethics began when our ancestors made a **social contract** to leave the **state of nature** and form civil society. **Peter Singer:** ethics began when people started to **reflect on the best way to live** — long after societies had **customary** standards of right and wrong. *"Virtually every human society has some form of myth to explain the origin of morality"* | — |

**Socrates** began **systematic ethical reflection in the West**:

- *"The unexamined life is not worth living"* — humans can make **conscious ethical choices**.
- The pursuit of **wisdom through inquiry and argument** is the best way to live well.
- **"Knowledge is virtue"** (Omoregbe 2005): knowledge equals right conduct, so **ignorance is the cause of wrongdoing**. Socrates compared the good to the **sun**: as the sun gives light and life, **knowledge enables us to live virtuously**.

## The Scope of Ethics

| Branch | Concern | Typical question |
|---|---|---|
| **Normative ethics** | Sets **norms or standards of conduct**. Are actions judged by **consequences**, or by the **motive** of the actor or the **nature of the act**? | *What is the good life? How are actions morally justified?* |
| **Meta-ethics** (analytic moral philosophy) | **Clarifies ethical terms** — good, bad, wrong, duty, obligation, virtue, vice; the **nature** of ethical statements; **where morality came from** | *What is a good act? Does morality exist **independently of human beings**?* (Lynch and Lynch 2009) |
| **Applied ethics** | **Applies ethical principles to practical issues** — abortion, the **environment**, **feminist rights**, **animal welfare**. It answers the charge that philosophy is abstract and inaccessible | *Is abortion permissible? How should we treat animals?* |

<SortingGame title="Normative, meta-ethics or applied ethics?">

```sort
@buckets Normative ethics | Meta-ethics | Applied ethics
What standard should decide whether an action is right — its consequences or its motive? => Normative ethics :: Setting the standards of right conduct.
What does the word "good" mean when we say "a good person"? => Meta-ethics :: Clarifying ethical terms.
Should Nigeria legalise abortion? => Applied ethics :: A concrete social issue.
Does morality exist independently of human beings? => Meta-ethics :: The nature and origin of morality.
Is it wrong to keep animals in poor conditions for profit? => Applied ethics :: Animal welfare is an applied-ethics issue.
What is the good life, and how should we live it? => Normative ethics :: The central normative question.
```

## Ethics and Morality

The words are often used **interchangeably**, but strictly they differ:

| Morality | Ethics |
|---|---|
| The **customs, principles or codes** by which a society lives | The **systematic study of morality** and moral principles |
| **The actual practice** of right conduct and the good life | **Theorising** about right conduct and the good life |

Children are taught by parents and teachers to **avoid** some actions (labelled **bad**) and **embrace** others (labelled **good**), usually **without explanation**. So most people reach adulthood full of moral terms — "good", "bad", "right", "wrong" — that they have **never reflected on**. **Ethics is the systematic study of exactly these terms.**

## Why Ethics Matters

**"Ethics counts in everything"** — the sticker of the West African Bioethics Programme (now the Centre for Bioethics and Research).

Imagine a world with **no moral rules**, where people act anyhow. **Thomas Hobbes** (17th century) imagined a **state of nature** without common power or political authority: a **war "of every man against every man."** For Hobbes, ethics:

- creates **stability and order**;
- helps people **build trust** and close relationships.

**Ethics is the backbone of society** — it **moulds character** and promotes the good life. **Society** is a group of people bound by **enduring relationships or common ideals**, interacting constantly, with institutions such as the **family, education, politics, the economy and religion**.

- No individual can **flourish outside society**, and **no society can thrive while ignoring ethics**.
- **Joseph Omoregbe:** **Nigeria is "morally sick"**. Tackling its socio-economic problems without addressing its moral failures is like **treating the symptoms instead of the disease**. **"Morality is the health of society"**, and **"the only lasting cure for Nigeria's ailment is a moral regeneration."**
- Politics, governance, health care, sport, marriage, family and religion — **everything depends on ethics**.

### What makes a question moral?

- **Eating food** is **amoral** — outside morality.
- But **one person eating the food meant for the whole group** is a **moral** problem.
- Should society pay the medical costs of **old people who, through improvidence or bad luck, cannot**? (Compare the US **Medicare** scheme.) That is a moral question.

**Brand Blanshard (1966):** a moral question is one **"whose decision depends on a choice between values. A non-moral question becomes moral the instant values are introduced."** A **value** is what is **good, desirable or useful** to someone or a group.

Other examples:

- a student who receives upkeep money but **neither attends classes nor studies**;
- a teacher who spends lectures on **idle talk**.

Whether you are a nurse, doctor, cobbler or trader, **only by practising ethical norms can we achieve our social goals**.

## Four Ethical Principles

A **principle** in ethics is a general statement setting out **the conditions under which an action is right or wrong** — a generalisation used in moral reasoning (Timmons 2002). The four together are called **principlism**, or the **Four Principles Approach**: ethical decisions are made by **weighing the principles against each other**.

| Principle | Meaning | It requires us to… |
|---|---|---|
| **(a) Respect for persons** (human dignity) | Every person has **intrinsic worth irrespective of achievements** and must **never be treated as a mere expendable** (Bennett-Woods) | **Tell the truth**; **respect privacy**; **protect confidential information**; let people make **reasoned, informed choices** — e.g. obtaining patients' **consent** |
| **(b) Nonmaleficence** | We must **not harm, or increase the risk of harm to, others**; where harm is unavoidable, **minimise it** — **"First, do no harm"** | Not kill; not cause **pain or suffering**; not **incapacitate**; not cause **offence**; not **deprive others of the goods of life** |
| **(c) Beneficence** | The obligation to **promote good over evil** — not just avoiding harm but **actively securing others' well-being** | **Protect others' rights**; **prevent** and **remove** harm; **help disabled persons**; **rescue** people in danger |
| **(d) Justice** | **Fairness** — **benefits, risks and costs distributed equitably**; no individual or group **favoured** above others | Share by one of the **five principles below** |

The five principles of fair distribution are:

1. **To each an equal share.**
2. **To each according to effort.**
3. **To each according to need.**
4. **To each according to contribution.**
5. **To each according to merit.**

## Four Ethical Rules

| Rule | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Veracity** | **Truth-telling**, honesty, integrity, reliability — not lying or deliberately misinforming. It can also be violated **by omission**: withholding all or part of the truth, or **cloaking information in jargon** that fails to inform or deliberately misleads (Harris 1966) |
| **Privacy** | A person's **right to remain private** and not disclose information — **freedom from the attention of others** |
| **Confidentiality** | Sharing information **only on a "need to know" basis** |
| **Fidelity** | **Loyalty, faithfulness and dependability** — the duty to **care for all**, whoever they are or whatever they have done |

Like ethical principles, these rules **build up the moral life** and promote **harmony and order**. They can be **taught in formal education**, as some developed societies do.

<SortingGame title="Which ethical rule is being kept or broken?">

```sort
@buckets Veracity | Privacy | Confidentiality | Fidelity
A seller tells a buyer that the used car has a faulty gearbox => Veracity :: Truth-telling.
A doctor fills a report with jargon so the patient will not understand the bad news => Veracity :: Cloaking information in jargon violates veracity by omission.
A celebrity refuses to discuss her marriage with journalists => Privacy :: The right not to disclose information and to be free from others' attention.
An HR officer shares a staff member's medical report only with the officer who must approve sick leave => Confidentiality :: Information shared on a need-to-know basis.
A nurse gives the same careful care to a prisoner as to any other patient => Fidelity :: The duty to care for all, whatever they have done.
```

## Conclusion and Recommendations

Ethics **moulds character**, promotes the **good life** and is essential for society's **peace, harmony and progress**. Among other things, the chapter recommends:

1. **teaching ethics** in Nigerian educational institutions;
2. **establishing institutions** that teach students — and Nigerians generally — **basic ethical principles**;
3. making **ethics training a condition for appointment into public office**;
4. **research into African ethics**, with its findings included in **curricula** at all levels.

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. What is ethics? Explain its focus on action and moral responsibility.">
Ethics (from Greek ethikos or ethos — custom, habit, character) is an inquiry into the nature of morality: the systematic study of the principles and standards of human conduct and of values. It asks how we should live, what "right" and "good" mean, and what our rights and responsibilities are. It focuses on action — "How should I lead my life?" — and on moral responsibility: praise and blame presuppose that people act freely and could have done otherwise. It is also called moral philosophy.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Discuss three theories of the origin of ethics.">
Religion (the divine command theory): God authors morality — the Ten Commandments, the Golden Rule — but atheists and agnostics may reject it. Conscience: an inner moral sense that reprimands and commends — but consciences can be untrained or seared (slave traders), differ between people and offer no principles for resolving conflict. Society: Hobbes traced ethics to the social contract that ended the state of nature; Singer to human reflection on the best way to live. Socrates began systematic Western ethics — "the unexamined life is not worth living", "knowledge is virtue".
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Distinguish ethics from morality and explain the three branches of ethics.">
Morality is the customs, principles and codes by which a society lives — the practice of right conduct. Ethics is the systematic study and theory of morality. Normative ethics sets standards of conduct (consequences versus motives or the nature of acts). Meta-ethics clarifies ethical terms and the nature and origin of morality. Applied ethics applies principles to practical issues such as abortion, the environment, feminist rights and animal welfare.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Why is ethics important to society? Use Hobbes and Omoregbe.">
Without moral rules, life would resemble Hobbes' state of nature — a war of every man against every man. Ethics creates stability and order and builds trust. It is the backbone of society: no individual flourishes outside society, and no society thrives while ignoring ethics. Omoregbe says Nigeria is morally sick; solving its problems without moral regeneration treats symptoms rather than causes, because "morality is the health of society". Everything — governance, health care, family, religion — depends on ethics.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="5. Explain the four ethical principles and the four ethical rules.">
Principles: respect for persons (intrinsic worth — truth-telling, privacy, confidentiality, informed consent); nonmaleficence ("first, do no harm" — no killing, pain, incapacitation, offence or deprivation; minimise unavoidable harm); beneficence (promote good — protect rights, prevent and remove harm, help the disabled, rescue); justice (fair distribution by equality, effort, need, contribution or merit). Together they form principlism. Rules: veracity (truth-telling, including avoiding omission and jargon), privacy (the right not to disclose), confidentiality (need-to-know sharing) and fidelity (loyal care for all).
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- Ethics aims at **the good life (happiness, flourishing)** through **virtue** (Socrates, Plato, Aristotle). **Cardinal virtues: temperance, fortitude, prudence, justice**; vices include stealing, lying, cheating and exam malpractice.
- **Ethics** (*ethikos/ethos* = custom, character) = inquiry into morality = **moral philosophy**; it focuses on **action** and **moral responsibility**.
- **Origins**: **divine command** (Ten Commandments, Golden Rule), **conscience** (unreliable), **society** (Hobbes' social contract; Singer); **Socrates**: "knowledge is virtue", ignorance causes wrongdoing.
- **Branches**: normative, meta-ethics, applied. **Morality** = practice; **ethics** = systematic study.
- Why ethics matters: Hobbes' **state of nature**; **Omoregbe** — "morality is the health of society". **Blanshard**: a question becomes moral when **values** enter.
- **Four principles (principlism)**: **respect for persons, nonmaleficence, beneficence, justice** (equal, effort, need, contribution, merit).
- **Four rules**: **veracity, privacy, confidentiality, fidelity**.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- The **four cardinal virtues** and the **four principles** are easy marks — learn them and one example each.
- Distinguish **nonmaleficence (avoid harm)** from **beneficence (do good)** — a common trap.
- Know **who said what**: Hobbes — state of nature and social contract; Singer — reflection on how to live; Omoregbe — Nigeria "morally sick"; **Blanshard** — moral questions involve a choice between values.
- If asked why *"ethics is what my feelings tell me"* is inadequate, answer that **feelings (like conscience) can lead us astray** and differ between people.
