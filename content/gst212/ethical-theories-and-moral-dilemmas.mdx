## Why Ethics Matters More Than Ever

Ethics asks:

- **What values should we hold?**
- **How should we treat those around us?**
- **Are we free to do whatever we like?**
- **How should we handle social problems** — stealing, killing (**suicide, abortion, euthanasia, genocide, war**), homosexuality, divorce?

Traditionally ethics dealt with **good and bad conduct**, **right and wrong actions**, and being a **virtuous or bad person**. Modern life has **broadened its scope** to include:

- our duties to the **environment and non-human animals**;
- whether **men and women** see morality the same way;
- right conduct in **business and the professions**;
- how to treat the **terminally ill**;
- how to relate to people of **different sexual orientations**;
- ethics on the **Internet**.

**Moral education then and now:**

- In **traditional African societies**, communal living and strong family ties meant children **absorbed moral values from moonlight tales** and the watchful attention of elders.
- Today, busy lives, divorce, wars and unrest have **broken down family life** — yet **the family is meant to be the custodian of moral values**.

The **moral decline** shows in:

- **fraud**, **abductions** and the **embezzlement of public funds**;
- **militancy, insurgency and terrorism**;
- **sexually transmitted diseases**, social unrest, and religious and sectional crises;
- **scams and cybercrime**.

That is why we must examine our ideas of morality more seriously than ever.

**Applied ethics** is the branch of ethics that **examines the moral status of concrete social issues** — whether they are sometimes justifiable or absolutely condemnable. Its divisions include:

- **bioethics** and **genethics**;
- **business ethics** and **professional ethics**;
- **sexual ethics** and **gender ethics**;
- **animal-rights ethics** and **environmental ethics**;
- **infoethics**.

## Ethical Theories

**Ethical theories** are **the standards by which moral issues are weighed** to decide whether they are right or wrong. There are **three types**:

| Type | How it judges an action | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **(a) Consequentialist (teleological)** | By its **result, outcome or consequences** — a good result means a good action | **Utilitarianism, egoism, hedonism, altruism** |
| **(b) Deontological** | **Not** by outcomes, but by **rules, logical consistency, principles and regulations** | **Kant's categorical imperative**, **social contract theory**, **divine command theory** |
| **(c) Virtue ethics** | By the **character of the moral agent**. **Good action can only come from a good person**; a bad person is not capable of a good action | **Aristotle's doctrine of the mean** |

*(Normative ethics is therefore often said to divide into **teleological** and **deontological** theories, with virtue ethics as a third approach.)*

### Six popular theories

| Theory | Core idea |
|---|---|
| **Utilitarianism** | Rightness depends **entirely on consequences**: an act is right if it **maximises the greatest good for the greatest number** (Parsons and Parsons 2008) |
| **Social contract theory** | People **ought to follow moral rules on condition that others follow them too**. The social contract is an **implicit agreement about the basic principles** of a group, and the web of such agreements forms society's **ground rules** (Rost 1993) |
| **Situation ethics** | **No intrinsic morality**: **the situation is the ultimate criterion** of right and wrong. Laws are **illuminating guides**, not commands, and there are **no absolute moral laws** — except **love**. Its one injunction: **"Always do the loving thing"** (Babor 2006) |
| **Kant's categorical imperative** | **(1)** Always act on the **maxim you could will to become a universal law**. **(2)** Treat **humanity, in your own person or another's, always as an end and never merely as a means**. Rational beings take the categorical imperative as a **constraint upon themselves** (Becker 1993) |
| **Feminist or care ethics** | **Gender matters** to how moral problems, moral agency and moral concepts are understood (Westlund 2009). It stresses **care, responsibility, nurturance and compassion**, and de-emphasises **duty, justice, rules and selfishness**, which it sees as **androcentric** (male-centred) |
| **Aristotle's doctrine of the mean** | Virtue is a **mean between two vices** — one of **excess**, one of **deficiency** *(details beyond the textbook)*. **Courage** lies between **cowardice** and **rashness**; **generosity** between **stinginess** and **extravagance**; **temperance** between **insensibility** and **self-indulgence** |

<FlowDiagram title="Testing an action with Kant's categorical imperative">
<FlowStep title="1. State your maxim">
Put your plan as a general rule, e.g. *"When I need money, I will borrow it and promise to repay, knowing I never will."*
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="2. Universalise it">
Imagine **everyone** acting on it: everyone makes false promises whenever it suits them.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="3. Check for contradiction">
In such a world **no one would believe a promise**, so false promising could not even work. The maxim **cannot be willed as a universal law** — so the act is **wrong**.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="4. Check the humanity formula">
Am I treating the lender **merely as a means** to my ends, rather than as a rational person who could consent? Deceiving them does exactly that — **wrong again**.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="5. Verdict">
The duty holds **regardless of consequences** — the mark of a **deontological** theory.
</FlowStep>
</FlowDiagram>

Social contract theory => Deontological :: Rules agreed on condition that others keep them too.

<SortingGame title="Which theory is speaking?">

```sort
@buckets Utilitarianism | Social contract | Situation ethics | Kant | Care ethics
"Build the road that benefits the most people, even if a few lose out." => Utilitarianism :: The greatest good for the greatest number.

```

</SortingGame>

## Moral Dilemmas

- In everyday speech, a **dilemma** is a choice between **two unsatisfactory alternatives**, one of which must be chosen.
- A **moral dilemma** is a moral choice in which, **whichever alternative you take, you cannot satisfy all the moral rules** that apply.

**Davis (2005):** in many real and literary situations, people **"face insoluble conflicts between equally compelling but directly competing moral requirements, and thus have to violate or fail to satisfy at least one of them."**

**Moral issues are not empirically verifiable**, so they have no straightforward answers. The arguments for each side can both seem compelling — but **one cannot choose "a" and "not-a" at the same time**. Ethics does not merely endorse or condemn; it **helps determine the moral status of issues** by examining arguments.

## Dilemma 1: Abortion

| Pro-life (conservatives, anti-abortion) | Pro-choice (liberals) |
|---|---|
| The **fertilised egg is already an individuated human being or person** (MacGuigan 1994) | A **right not to be killed belongs to actual persons** — humans **born alive** |
| **Contemporary Catholic teaching:** life must be preserved **from the moment of conception** | Personhood depends on **characteristics** that entitle one to **membership of the moral community** (Marietta 1997) |
| **Foetuses have a right to life** because they are **potential or genetic persons**, even if not yet actual persons | Abortion is a **woman's right**. **Radical feminists** stress a woman's **freedom, liberty and autonomy** over her own body |

**The conflict:** a woman's **liberty** against a **foetus's right to life**.

**Bad consequences** that a continued pregnancy might bring (MacKinnon 2011):

- loss of a **job** or other opportunities;
- the **suffering of the future child**;
- the **burden of caring** for the child in difficult circumstances;
- **social stigma**.

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Teni's case">
**Teni**, a **200-level student**, was **raped** by an intruder in her room four weeks ago and is now **pregnant**; she does not know the father.

- She sees abortion as the surest way out, but **fears she would be killing a person like herself**.
- Keeping the pregnancy could **truncate her studies and ambitions**.
- It could also **worsen her widowed mother's high blood pressure** — her mother is her **sole sponsor**.

*Would you advise Teni to have an abortion? Does it suffice to consider only the mother's health, or should other bad consequences count too?*
</Tab>
<Tab label="A utilitarian might say">
Weigh the **total happiness and suffering**: Teni's trauma, education and future; her mother's health; the life and prospects of the child. If abortion produces **less overall suffering**, it may be justified — though the utilitarian must still decide **whether the foetus's interests count**, and how much.
</Tab>
<Tab label="A pro-life view might say">
If the foetus is **already a person from conception**, its **right to life** does not depend on how it was conceived. The wrong of the rape does not justify **killing an innocent third party**. Support, adoption and care are the moral options.
</Tab>
<Tab label="A liberal or feminist view might say">
Teni did **not consent** to sex or pregnancy. Forcing her to carry it would violate her **bodily autonomy** a second time. On the liberal view the foetus is **not yet an actual person** with a right not to be killed.
</Tab>
<Tab label="Care or situation ethics might say">
Look at the **concrete relationships** — Teni, her sick mother, the potential child — and ask what the **most caring, loving** response is in *this* situation, rather than applying one rule to all abortions.
</Tab>
</Tabs>

## Dilemma 2: Surrogacy — "Mothering as Contract"

Society places **great value on children**. Infertile couples who cannot conceive may turn to **surrogacy (contract motherhood)**. Infertility is **not a necessary condition** for it, but many see infertile couples' pain as a compelling reason to permit it (Gibson 1994).

**Elliot Dorff (1998)** lists **six combinations of gametes** for a surrogate pregnancy:

1. the **husband's and wife's** gametes;
2. the **husband's sperm and a donated egg**;
3. the **wife's egg and donated sperm**;
4. a **donated egg and donated sperm**;
5. the **surrogate's own egg**, inseminated with the **husband's sperm**;
6. the **surrogate's egg** with **donated sperm**.

<Analogy>
**The Paros and the Gambos.**

- **Mrs Paro** lost four pregnancies and could no longer conceive. Under family pressure, and horrified by a story of a childless widow stripped of her property, she resolved to become a mother at any cost.
- **Mrs Gambo**, a mother of four whose husband had lost his job, needed money for food and school fees.
- A lawyer linked them — **"women helping women"**. After artificial insemination, Mrs Gambo conceived **twins**.
- Then tragedy struck: the Gambos **lost all four of their children in a car crash**. Now Mrs Gambo is **reluctant to hand over the twins**. She asks to keep **at least one**, and offers to write off the unpaid balance. **The Paros insist on both.**

Who has the stronger claim — and **whose interest should come first**: the surrogate's, the commissioning parents', or the children's?
</Analogy>

**Emma Lycett (2009):** of all assisted reproduction procedures, **"surrogacy remains the most contentious."** The main positions:

- **Some feminist bioethicists:** surrogacy **enhances women's reproductive autonomy**.
- **Other feminists:** it is **degrading and exploitative**. All women involved may be exploited or controlled by **society, their husbands, themselves, or all of these** (Roberts 1998).
- **A worry:** some will use surrogates not for **physical inability** but for **convenience** — ambitious professional women unwilling to interrupt careers and social lives (Kirejczyk 2000).
- **The strongest objection:** surrogacy is **baby-selling**, and **no one has a right to sell a human being**.

**The dilemma:** what is right seems to lie **somewhere between the interests of the surrogate, the commissioning parents, and the child** to be born.

## Dilemma 3: Public versus Private Morality

| Private morality | Public morality |
|---|---|
| How one behaves **within one's house or home**, outside the control of government and corporate bodies | How one is **expected to behave in public** — in government, the corporate world, and so on |
| Guided by **feelings, sentiments and emotions** — affiliation, fraternity, **care, pity, trust, faith**. Families overlook members' wrongs; religions discourage **vengeance** | A **formal sphere** of many unrelated people. The state and corporate bodies dictate **objective standards**, enforced by **threats of punishment, sanctions and force** |

**The problem:** what happens when **private ideals enter the public sphere**? Is it always wrong?

- **The policeman:** he has **legally inadmissible** evidence that a suspect is planning a **terrorist outrage**. Arrest now and the case would fail, letting the terrorist go free to bomb again — and sue for wrongful detention. **Off duty, he ambushes and kills the terrorist.**
- **The office-holder:** a public official uses **public resources** to fly a friend's child abroad for **heart treatment**.
- **The interview panel:** your **relative**, not the best-qualified candidate, desperately needs the job to care for a sickly spouse and an aged parent. Is your first duty to the **establishment** or to your **relative**?

<SortingGame title="Private or public morality?">

```sort
@buckets Private morality | Public morality
Forgiving a brother who broke your phone because "he is family" => Private morality :: Guided by affection and loyalty within the home.
A civil servant must follow procurement rules or face sanctions => Public morality :: Objective standards enforced by penalties.
Choosing the best-qualified candidate even though your cousin also applied => Public morality :: Impartial public standards over family ties.
A mother overlooks her child's rudeness out of love => Private morality :: Feelings of care guide the private sphere.
A company sacks a manager who leaked customer data => Public morality :: Corporate standards backed by sanctions.
```

</SortingGame>

## Dilemma 4: Terrorism

Since the **11 September 2001 attacks** in the USA, terrorism has become a **global concern**. It raises several dilemmas:

1. **Who is a terrorist?** Might **opponents of a government or its policies** be labelled terrorists and treated as such?
2. **War or crime?** The war on terror **"does not sit easily either in the category of war or in the category of crime"** (Smith 2008). Must security agencies follow **rules of engagement**, or is **anything goes** acceptable if it works? **Bakaoukas (2009):** as **domestic police violence** can be legitimate if it serves **just, specified goals** and is **governed by rules**, so can the state's violence against threats — **if the ends are just and the means properly limited**.
3. **Democracy's bind:** a democratic state cannot easily turn on **its own citizens**, who elect it, the force it uses on external enemies. Yet can it **tolerate** a group that inflicts suffering and **refuses to negotiate**? And do some turn to terror because they feel **ignored in a democracy**? *"Support for terrorism cannot usually be regarded as illegitimate on the grounds that it undercuts democratic debate, since democratic debate is commonly inadequate to resolving the issues"* (Gilbert 1992).
4. **Innocents nearby:** is it justifiable to **kill or maim innocent people** in order to capture terrorists hiding among them?

<Analogy>
**The Gbajamo raid.**

- **Bala's** troop was ordered to smash a terrorist group. At **4.30 a.m.** on a Tuesday they reached **Gbajamo**, where intelligence placed the hideout. The terrorists had **already fled**.
- The commander attacked the village anyway, calling it the **host community**. Men, women and children were **killed, wounded and raped**, and homes were razed.
- **His justification:** some villagers were **relatives of terrorists**, so it was "only fair" to make terrorists' families suffer as their victims' families do.
- **Bala's defence:** the soldiers were **obeying orders**, and **national security outweighs that of a minority**.

Were the soldiers at fault? Did the people of Gbajamo have a **fundamental moral right not to be punished for sins they never committed**?
</Analogy>

**Conclusion:** there are **many sides to any moral issue**. When options are subjected to the **scrutiny of reason**, some prove **weightier** than others. Ethics urges us to choose the options that **can be convincingly defended in the court of rationality**.

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. Explain the three types of ethical theory with examples.">
Consequentialist (teleological) theories judge an action by its outcome — good results make a good action (utilitarianism, egoism, hedonism, altruism). Deontological theories ignore outcomes and judge by rules, principles and logical consistency (Kant's categorical imperative, social contract theory, divine command theory). Virtue ethics judges by the character of the agent — good actions come only from good persons (Aristotle's doctrine of the mean, where virtue lies between excess and deficiency).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Explain Kant's categorical imperative, situation ethics and feminist care ethics.">
Kant: act only on a maxim you can will as a universal law, and treat humanity, in yourself and others, always as an end and never merely as a means — duties that bind regardless of consequences. Situation ethics: there is no intrinsic morality; the situation decides; laws are guides and love is the only absolute ("always do the loving thing"). Care ethics: gender shapes moral understanding; it values care, responsibility, nurturance and compassion over the "androcentric" emphasis on duty, justice and rules.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Discuss abortion as a moral dilemma, using Teni's case.">
Pro-life conservatives hold that the fertilised egg is a person (Catholic teaching: life from conception), so the foetus, as a potential or genetic person, has a right to life. Liberals hold that only actual, born persons have a right not to be killed, and that abortion is a woman's right; radical feminists stress autonomy. Teni, pregnant after rape, fears killing a person but also faces the loss of her studies and her sick mother's health. Utilitarian, pro-life, liberal and care perspectives weigh these differently; the conflict is between the woman's liberty and the foetus's right to life, plus other consequences (MacKinnon).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. What ethical issues does surrogacy raise?">
Surrogacy can involve six gamete combinations (Dorff). Supporters say it enhances reproductive autonomy and relieves infertile couples. Critics say it degrades and exploits women (Roberts), may be used for convenience rather than need, and amounts to baby-selling. The Paro–Gambo case shows the clash when a surrogate, having lost her own children, wants to keep one of the twins: rightness lies between the interests of the surrogate, the commissioning parents and the child — "the most contentious" assisted reproduction procedure (Lycett).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="5. Distinguish private from public morality and discuss the dilemmas where they meet.">
Private morality governs behaviour at home, guided by feelings of care, loyalty, pity and trust (families overlook wrongs, religions discourage vengeance). Public morality governs behaviour in government and corporate life through objective standards enforced by sanctions and force. Dilemmas arise when private ideals enter public life: a policeman killing a terrorist on inadmissible evidence, an official using public funds for a friend's sick child, or a panellist favouring a needy relative over a better candidate.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="6. Discuss the moral dilemmas of dealing with terrorism.">
Who counts as a terrorist (might government opponents be labelled)? Is terrorism war or crime — and so must forces follow rules of engagement (Bakaoukas: legitimate if ends are just and means limited)? Democracies struggle to use force on citizens yet cannot tolerate groups that refuse to negotiate; some turn to terror because democratic debate fails them (Gilbert). And may innocents be killed to reach hidden terrorists? The Gbajamo raid — collective punishment of a village, defended by "obeying orders" and "national security" — raises the right not to be punished for others' crimes.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- Ethics has **broadened** (environment, animals, gender, business, the terminally ill, sexuality, the Internet). **Moral decline** (fraud, terrorism, cybercrime) makes it urgent; the **family** should be the custodian of values.
- **Applied ethics** examines the **moral status of concrete social issues** (bioethics, business, environmental, info-ethics…).
- **Three types**: **consequentialist/teleological** (utilitarianism, egoism, hedonism, altruism), **deontological** (Kant, social contract, divine command), **virtue ethics** (Aristotle's **mean**).
- **Theories**: utilitarianism (greatest good), social contract (conditional rules), **situation ethics** (love is the only absolute), **Kant** (universal law; persons as ends), **care ethics** (care over "androcentric" rules).
- **Moral dilemma** = insoluble conflict between **equally compelling, competing** moral requirements (Davis).
- **Abortion**: pro-life (a person from conception) vs liberal (only actual persons; autonomy). **Surrogacy**: autonomy vs exploitation and baby-selling; the **surrogate, parents and child**. **Public vs private morality**. **Terrorism**: war or crime, rules of engagement, innocents.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Know which theories are **consequentialist vs deontological** — a regular MCQ.
- Memorise the injunctions: *"Always do the loving thing"* (situation ethics); *universal law* and *ends, never merely means* (Kant); *greatest good for the greatest number* (utilitarianism).
- The past-question definitions are worth learning **word for word**: moral dilemma (Davis); applied ethics (moral status of concrete social issues); private morality (behaviour at home, outside government and corporate control).
- For dilemma essays, **present at least two theories' verdicts** and end with a reasoned judgement.
