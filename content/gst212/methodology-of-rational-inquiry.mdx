## What Is Inquiry?

In ordinary usage, **inquiry** is **an examination or quest for knowledge or truth** — **reasoning from data** and applying skills to real problems (Padilla 2010). **We inquire when we seek truth, information or knowledge by raising questions.** Inquiry begins by **gathering information through the senses** — seeing, hearing, touching, tasting and smelling.

---

## Scientific versus Philosophical Inquiry

| | Scientific inquiry | Philosophical inquiry |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | The diverse ways scientists **study the natural world** and **propose explanations based on evidence** | The attempt to answer **the most fundamental questions about human existence** |
| **Typical questions** | *How does this disease spread? What causes the tides?* | *What is the meaning of life? What is the universe made of? How can we know? What is the right thing to do?* |
| **Method** | **Observing, questioning**, checking what is known, **planning investigations**, using tools to **gather and analyse data**, proposing **explanations and predictions**, **communicating** results. It needs identification of assumptions, **critical and logical thinking**, and **alternative explanations** | **Rational justification** of beliefs — conceptual analysis, logical coherence and argument. Other disciplines ask these questions too, but philosophers insist on **rational justification** |
| **Standing** | Develops the ability to think and act scientifically (Froschauer 2010) | **The foundation of all other inquiries** — the ground on which other disciplines stand. **Philosophy remains the foundation** of the natural sciences and of disciplines today |

**Three methods of scientific inquiry:**

| Method | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Retroduction** | **Guessing the conditions** under which a kind of phenomenon appears — reasoning about **why things happen**, including **why the data look as they do** |
| **Induction** | Reasoning **from data to generality** |
| **Colligation** | **Linking facts together** by a general description, or by a hypothesis that applies to them all |

The inquiry process thrives on **the natural human desire to make sense of the world** — **curiosity is its fuel**.

<SortingGame title="Retroduction, induction or colligation?">

```sort
@buckets Retroduction | Induction | Colligation
Every sample of this metal tested so far conducts electricity, so all samples probably do => Induction :: From particular data to a generalisation.
The river is unusually muddy today — perhaps it rained heavily upstream last night => Retroduction :: Guessing the conditions that would explain why the data look this way.
Kepler links Mars's recorded positions together under one idea: its orbit is an ellipse => Colligation :: Binding separate facts together under one general description.
Students who miss lectures seem to score lower; a researcher asks what conditions would produce this pattern => Retroduction :: Reasoning about why the data appear as they do.
After observing hundreds of black crows, we conclude that all crows are black => Induction :: A generalisation from observed cases.
```

</SortingGame>

### Dewey's theory of inquiry

**John Dewey (1859–1952)**, the American philosopher and educator, stressed **learning by doing things and trying ideas out**, not by memorising. In *Logic: The Theory of Inquiry* he defined inquiry as:

> "the **controlled or directed transformation of an indeterminate situation** into one that is so **determinate** in its constituent distinctions and relations as to convert the elements of the original situation into a **unified whole**" (Hickman 1997).

<FlowDiagram title="Dewey: from an indeterminate to a determinate situation">
<FlowStep title="1. Something is out of balance">
We sense that a situation is **indeterminate** or problematic — anything from trivial to life-threatening. For Dewey the **whole experienced situation**, not just the person, is problematic (he avoided subjectivism).
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="2. Analyse the situation">
Identify **which elements are relevant** to the problem.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="3. Reconstruct">
Rebuild those elements. We do not know in advance whether to **alter** things outside us or **accommodate** ourselves to them — usually it is a **blend of both**.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="4. Adjustment">
**Harmony and balance are restored**: the situation becomes a **determinate, unified whole**.
</FlowStep>
</FlowDiagram>

### From doubt to settled belief *(beyond the surviving text)*

Rational inquiry is often pictured as a journey **from doubt to justified belief**:

- **René Descartes**, the father of modern philosophy, used **methodic (systematic) doubt** to strip away every belief that could be doubted. He found one certainty: **"I think, therefore I am"** (*cogito ergo sum*).
- **C. S. Peirce** described inquiry as the struggle that begins with the **"irritation of doubt"** and ends in the **"fixation of belief"**. The belief must be **justified** and able to survive rigorous questioning — not merely comfortable.

A rational inquirer is **open-minded, logically rigorous and intellectually honest**, and demands **evidence and justification** before accepting a claim.

<SortingGame title="Scientific or philosophical inquiry?">

```sort
@buckets Scientific inquiry | Philosophical inquiry
Does this new malaria drug reduce fever faster than the old one? => Scientific inquiry :: An empirical question answered by controlled testing.
What makes a human life meaningful? => Philosophical inquiry :: A fundamental question about existence.
How fast does sound travel in water? => Scientific inquiry :: Measurable by observation and experiment.
Is it ever right to break a promise? => Philosophical inquiry :: A question needing rational justification, not a lab test.
What is the universe ultimately made of — matter, mind or both? => Philosophical inquiry :: A metaphysical question.
Which fertiliser gives the highest cassava yield in Oyo State? => Scientific inquiry :: A question for field experiments.
```

## Myth, Philosophy and Rational Inquiry

**Anthony Mercatante:** a **myth** is **"an anonymous traditional story, orally passed on from one generation to the next, believed to be literally true by the culture that produced it, about gods and goddesses, heroes, heroines, and other real and fantastic creatures, taking place at primeval or remote times."** **Mythology** is a **body of myths**, especially of a people's gods and heroes.

People receive myths through different **thought-patterns**:

| Thought-pattern | How myth is taken |
|---|---|
| **Mythical thinking** | Myth as a **true, objective story** — a **participatory** kind of thinking that immerses the person through the mind's imaginative power |
| **Rational thinking** | Myth as a kind of **allegory** |
| **Symbolic thinking** | Myth as a source of **symbols** that reveal the **deep and true side of things** |

**Mythical thinking is itself a way of knowing and understanding.** "Myth" may mean a **story of beginnings**, an intuitive, personified view of life, or an image, parable or allegory used to explain life. Some scholars see myths as **existential intuitions**, or as the **wise, reflective observations of gifted individuals** trying to understand their world.

### Demythicisation

**Mircea Eliade**, the historian of religion, found a **conscious process of "demythicisation"** in:

- **pre-Socratic Greece**;
- **Upanishadic India**;
- **Egypt**.

At some point people **lost faith in the divine history transmitted by myths** while still believing in the gods. Myths were **emptied of religious meaning** and became **legends or nursery tales** — though some myths remained in force.

**Attitudes to myth:**

- Myths are preserved through **rites** and tradition **backed by authority**. Since the **Sophists**, "myth" has had a **pejorative** meaning: unreliable folk stories (Madu 1992).
- Yet **Plato** used myths as **allegories** in his dialogues, and **Aristotle** allowed that some mythical data about the **heavenly bodies** might correspond to reality.
- **Solomon and Higgins:** the first Greek philosophers were **"steeped in mythology"**. Breakthroughs by **Pythagoras, Parmenides and Plato** involved **rejecting materialist explanations**, and they often **"sounded like mystic poets."**
- **Modern critics:** myth **"can embrace contradictions and absurdities"**, and new knowledge comes only through **intellectual intuition or deduction**.
- **But modern science did not arise as a total rebellion against myth.** The **Ionian naturalists** held views close to **creation myths**, and the founders of modern science — **Nicholas of Cusa, Kepler, Newton, Leibniz** — were absorbed by **metaphysical problems of an evidently mythological character** (*Encyclopaedia Britannica* 2000).

<SortingGame title="Mythical, rational or symbolic thinking?">

```sort
@buckets Mythical thinking | Rational thinking | Symbolic thinking
"Sango really does hurl thunderbolts at wrongdoers — the story is literally true." => Mythical thinking :: The myth taken as a true, objective story.
"The tortoise tales are allegories that teach children about greed and cunning." => Rational thinking :: The myth read as an allegory.

"Our ancestors truly descended from the sky on a chain." => Mythical thinking :: Belief in the literal truth of the story.
```

</SortingGame>

## Scepticism and Rational Inquiry

**Epistemology did not begin until the Greek Sophists showed sceptical tendencies** by denying the possibility of objective knowledge. **Scepticism has been the propelling force behind epistemology.** **A. J. Ayer (1956):** scepticism **"supplies the main subject matter for what is called theory of knowledge."** Different philosophical standpoints are defined by which stages of the sceptic's argument they accept or deny.

### A short history of scepticism

| When | Who | Sceptical idea |
|---|---|---|
| **5th century BC** | **The Eleatics** (Parmenides and followers) | Reduced reality to a **static One**; **questioned the reality of the sensory world**, of change and plurality |
| **5th century BC** | **Heraclitus** of Ephesus and his pupil **Cratylus** | The world is in such **flux** that **no permanent, unchangeable truth** can be found |
| **5th century BC** | **Xenophanes**, wandering poet-philosopher | **Doubted whether man could distinguish true from false knowledge** |
| **5th–4th century BC** | **Socrates** | Constantly **questioned others' knowledge claims**; in the *Apology*: **all he really knew was that he knew nothing** |
| **5th century BC** | **Protagoras** (Sophist) | **"Man is the measure of all things"** — **sceptical relativism**: no view is ultimately true, each is one man's opinion; also **agnostic about the gods** |
| **5th century BC** | **Gorgias** (Sophist) | **(i) Nothing exists; (ii) if anything exists, it cannot be known; (iii) if it can be known, it cannot be communicated** |
| **c. 360–c. 272 BC** | **Pyrrho of Elis** — the **father of Greek scepticism** | Tried to **live as a sceptic**: committed to no view, acted only on **appearances**, and sought **happiness or mental peace**. He confronted every belief with a **plausible opposite belief** |
| **3rd century BC** | **Academic scepticism** in **Plato's Academy** — **Arcesilaus** and **Carneades** | **Nothing can be known**, and there are **no criteria** for telling true from false — only **reasonable or probable** standards. It attacked the **Stoics and Epicureans**. Our source is **Cicero's *Academica*** |
| **Later** | **Pyrrhonism** — **Aenesidemus**, **Sextus Empiricus** (2nd or 3rd century AD; *Outlines of Pyrrhonism*, *Adversus Mathematicos*) | Criticised the Academics for claiming even that nothing can be known, or that some things are more probable. It used **tropes** to bring about ***epoche*** — **suspension of judgement**. **Aenesidemus' ten tropes** showed that sense-based judgements are unreliable because perceptions **vary between humans and animals** |
| **18th century** | **David Hume** | Attacked **induction** as an error of reasoning: it wrongly assumes **the future will resemble the past** |

**Plato's reply to Protagoras:** relativism is **self-refuting**. If all beliefs are equally true, then the belief *"some beliefs are not true"* is **just as true** as any other.

**The problem of the criterion:** to settle any disagreement we need a **criterion of truth**. But any criterion must rest on **another criterion** (an **infinite regress**), or on **itself** (**circular**).

### The sceptic's main arguments

1. **The senses deceive us** — experience shows our senses are sometimes mistaken, so **indubitable knowledge** through them seems impossible.
2. **Reasoning errs too** — we make mistakes in even the **simplest deductive and mathematical inferences**, so how can we be sure of any of them?
3. **The dream argument** — it is hard to tell **waking reality from dreaming**, so perhaps all experience is a dream.
4. **Equal reasons** — there is **no more reason to believe X exists than that it does not**, so belief is unreasonable (Slote 1970).

| Positive (mild) scepticism | Negative (universal) scepticism |
|---|---|
| The desire to **question the naive realism of ordinary life** — to be **careful and meticulous** with all beliefs | The **pessimistic, dogmatic refusal** to accept **any** knowledge claim as certain, now or ever — **all knowledge lacks total certainty** |
| Aims **not to deny knowledge** but to **raise the standard and quality** of knowledge claims | Leads to **anarchy and nihilism** in knowledge and social life |
| **Inevitable and necessary** for philosophy | **Cannot be sustained**: claiming "there is no justifiable knowledge" is **self-contradictory** — awareness of universal ignorance is itself knowledge, and the idea of ignorance **depends on the idea of knowledge** |

- **John Russell (1907):** universal scepticism is a **reaction to the quest for absolute, final truth**. Treat truth and knowledge as **processes of human experience, open to change**, and universal scepticism will not arise.
- **Richard Popkin (1969):** the **absolutist dogmatist** and the **destructive sceptic** are **both extremes**. The answer is **constructive scepticism**.

```sort
@buckets Protagoras | Gorgias | Pyrrho | Heraclitus | Socrates | Hume
"Man is the measure of all things." => Protagoras :: Sceptical relativism — truth is each person's opinion.
"Nothing exists; if it did, it could not be known; if known, it could not be communicated." => Gorgias :: The three sceptical-nihilist theses.
"I commit to no belief; I simply follow appearances, and so find peace of mind." => Pyrrho :: The father of Greek scepticism, living by appearances.
"Everything flows — no permanent truth about the world can be found." => Heraclitus :: The philosopher of flux.
"All I really know is that I know nothing." => Socrates :: From Plato's Apology.

```

</SortingGame>

## Francis Bacon and the Methods of a New Science

**Francis Bacon** (1561–1626) sought methods to **offset our most familiar human failings** in the search for truth. Reflecting his **Puritan** background, he said:

- the mind's relation to nature was meant to be as **natural and unimpeded** as that of the first humans with God in **Eden**;
- in our **original state of innocence**, the mind was **like a mirror** that clearly reflected nature's light;
- that mirror is now **"dirty, rough, and uneven"** from the **passions** and the **errors of tradition**, so it **distorts** nature and mixes in its own notions (Lawhead 2002).

To restore it, we must get rid of the **four idols**:

| Idol | What distorts the mind |
|---|---|
| **Idols of the Cave** | The **individual's** tendency to favour **his or her own intuitions and experience** — distortions from **personal preferences, biases, education, conditioning, circumstances and social status** |
| **Idols of the Tribe** | Failings **shared by all humans**: **sloth, pride, vanity, gullibility**, and the craving for **security, control and relief from uncertainty**. We wrongly treat **human sense as the measure of things** |
| **Idols of the Theatre** | Preconceptions from the **dogmas of philosophical systems** and wrong laws of demonstration — *"all the received systems are but so many **stage-plays**"* |
| **Idols of the Marketplace** | **Obscurity and vagueness of words** in human communication — an **"ill and unfit choice of words"** that obstructs understanding. **The most troublesome of all**: we think reason governs words, but **words react on the understanding** |

**Bacon's rules of thumb** for overcoming the idols:

- **turn away from established authorities** and adopt a **"try-it-and-see"** approach to discovery;
- **rely on first-hand investigation**, accumulate **systematically acquired experience**, and make **several competing proposals** tentatively;
- **strive for degrees or grades of certainty**;
- **reject absolutist claims** to inviolable knowledge;
- use **mathematical representation** for **clarity and precision**.

**Science for society:** Bacon did not see good science as detached from worldly concerns. He wanted the institutions of the **New Science** to be **civic partnerships** of entrepreneurs, scholars and rulers, **judged by their results in advancing social welfare**. He promoted self-disciplined experimental scientists as the **best advisers to government**.

- **The Royal Society:** his vision inspired the **Royal Society of London** (founded 1660, chartered 1662; the textbook says 1661), about **35 years after his death**.
- ***New Atlantis*** (1624): a **utopia where scientists are the chief administrators**, political strategy is replaced by **scientific expertise**, and scientists decide **what results to promulgate** to society. It anticipated modern **research institutions** and **technocratic culture**.

<SortingGame title="Which of Bacon's idols is at work?">

```sort
@buckets Idol of the Cave | Idol of the Tribe | Idol of the Theatre | Idol of the Marketplace
"I grew up hearing that twins bring bad luck, so I read every mishap as proof." => Idol of the Cave :: A personal bias from one's own upbringing and experience.
Most people accept a comforting rumour quickly because uncertainty feels unbearable => Idol of the Tribe :: A failing shared by all human nature — gullibility and the need for security.
"The master's philosophical system says so, therefore it must be true." => Idol of the Theatre :: Blind loyalty to a received system of thought — a "stage-play".
A debate collapses because each side means something different by "freedom" => Idol of the Marketplace :: Confusion from vague, ill-chosen words.
We assume nature works exactly as it appears to our senses => Idol of the Tribe :: Treating human sense as the measure of all things.
A scientist rejects any evidence that conflicts with the theory he has championed for years => Idol of the Cave :: Individual preference and conditioning distorting observation.
```

</SortingGame>

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. Distinguish scientific from philosophical inquiry and explain the three methods of scientific inquiry.">
Scientific inquiry studies the natural world and proposes evidence-based explanations: observing, questioning, investigating, analysing data, predicting and communicating results. Philosophical inquiry addresses the most fundamental questions — meaning, reality, knowledge, right action — and demands rational justification; it is the foundation of all other inquiries. Scientific inquiry uses retroduction (guessing the conditions that explain why phenomena and data appear as they do), induction (data to generality) and colligation (linking facts under one general description or hypothesis).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Explain Dewey's theory of inquiry.">
Dewey (1859–1952) defined inquiry as the controlled or directed transformation of an indeterminate situation into a determinate, unified whole. Inquiry arises whenever something is out of balance; the whole situation, not just the subject, is problematic. We analyse it to find the relevant elements, then reconstruct them — by altering external things, accommodating ourselves, or usually both ("adjustment") — to restore harmony. He also stressed learning by doing rather than memorising.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. What is myth, and how has it related to philosophy and science?">
Mercatante: an anonymous, orally transmitted traditional story believed literally true, about gods, heroes and creatures in primeval times. It can be taken mythically (as literally true), rationally (as allegory) or symbolically (as a source of deep symbols). Eliade traced demythicisation in pre-Socratic Greece, Upanishadic India and Egypt. Since the Sophists myth has had a negative sense, yet Plato used myths as allegories, the early Greeks were "steeped in mythology", and modern science did not simply rebel against myth — the Ionians and Cusa, Kepler, Newton and Leibniz engaged mythological and metaphysical themes.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Trace the history of scepticism and state the main sceptical arguments.">
The Eleatics doubted the sensory world; Heraclitus and Cratylus held that flux prevents permanent truth; Xenophanes doubted we can tell true from false. Socrates knew that he knew nothing; Protagoras taught relativism (man is the measure); Gorgias held that nothing exists, is knowable or is communicable. Pyrrho lived by appearances; the Academics (Arcesilaus, Carneades) denied any criterion of truth; the Pyrrhonists (Aenesidemus, Sextus Empiricus) used tropes for epoche; Hume attacked induction. Arguments: the senses deceive, reasoning errs, dreaming is indistinguishable from waking, the problem of the criterion (regress or circularity), and equal reasons for and against.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="5. Distinguish positive from negative scepticism and evaluate universal scepticism.">
Positive (mild) scepticism questions naive realism and scrutinises beliefs to raise the quality of knowledge claims — necessary for philosophy. Negative (universal) scepticism dogmatically denies that any knowledge is or will be certain. It is self-contradictory (knowing that we know nothing is knowledge; the idea of ignorance presupposes knowledge) and leads to anarchy and nihilism. Russell saw it as a reaction to the quest for absolute truth, cured by treating truth as a revisable process; Popkin called for constructive scepticism between the dogmatist and the destructive sceptic.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="6. Explain Bacon's four idols and his proposals for a New Science.">
The mind, once a clear mirror of nature, has been distorted. Idols of the Cave: individual biases from one's experience, education and status. Idols of the Tribe: failings common to human nature (sloth, pride, gullibility, the need for security). Idols of the Theatre: dogmas of received philosophical systems ("stage-plays"). Idols of the Marketplace: vague, ill-chosen words — the most troublesome. Remedies: turn from authority to try-it-and-see discovery; rely on first-hand, systematic investigation with competing tentative proposals; seek degrees of certainty; reject absolutism; use mathematical precision. Science should serve social welfare (New Atlantis; it inspired the Royal Society).
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Inquiry** = a quest for knowledge by raising questions, starting from the senses. **Scientific inquiry** (evidence-based; **retroduction, induction, colligation**) vs **philosophical inquiry** (fundamental questions, rational justification — the **foundation of all inquiries**).
- **Dewey**: inquiry turns an **indeterminate situation** into a **determinate unified whole** (analysis, reconstruction, adjustment). **Descartes** (methodic doubt, *cogito*) and **Peirce** (doubt → **fixation of belief**).
- **Myth** (Mercatante): mythical, rational and symbolic thinking; **Eliade's demythicisation**; philosophy and science grew **alongside** myth.
- **Scepticism** drives epistemology (Ayer): Eleatics, **Heraclitus**, Xenophanes, **Socrates**, **Protagoras** ("man is the measure"), **Gorgias** (three theses), **Pyrrho** (father), Academics, **Sextus/Aenesidemus** (tropes, *epoche*), **Hume** (induction). **Positive** (necessary) vs **negative universal** (self-refuting) scepticism.
- **Bacon's four idols**: **Cave** (individual), **Tribe** (human nature), **Theatre** (systems), **Marketplace** (words — the worst); remedies: experiment, degrees of certainty, mathematics; science for social welfare (*New Atlantis*, the Royal Society).
</Takeaway>
