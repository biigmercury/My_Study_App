## Why Solve Puzzles?

**Francis Offor** begins with a claim about human nature:

> The ability to **distinguish good and consistent reasoning from bad and inconsistent reasoning is natural** in humans. What training in logic does is to **sharpen this natural ability** by clearing our heads of the **"mental cobwebs"** that may be befogging our reasoning.

**Logical puzzles** are **exercises** meant to develop or sharpen our natural ability to use **good arguments in resolving our problems**. The process of solving them is what the phrase **"exercise in reasoning"** means.

## Puzzles and Riddles

In most societies there are sayings, proverbs and maxims that, in **Alfred North Whitehead's** phrase, **"call into play the higher faculties of the brain"** (cited in Copi). Decoding them demands **full mental alertness and concentration**. Such expressions **abound in traditional African societies**: families would **gather in their compounds at moonlight** to show off their mental prowess with puzzles and riddles.

**A. G. A. Bello (2000)** defines a logical puzzle as:

> a **specific question or series of questions**, accompanied by a **mass of information or propositions given as true** in the statement of the puzzle. Its solution consists in **finding answers to the questions posed**, and proffering **arguments whose premises are contained in the statement of the puzzle and whose conclusions are the answers**.

| | **Logical puzzle** | **Riddle** |
|---|---|---|
| **Information** | Comes with **clues** — propositions given as true | Needs **no additional information** |
| **Answer** | **One correct answer**, reached by **argument** from the clues | May have **more than one acceptable answer**, if its proponent can argue for it — even **arbitrarily** |
| **What it trains** | Our natural ability to use **good arguments** to resolve problems | **Mental alertness** |
| **Main value** | **Reasoning** | **Entertainment** |

**A West African riddle (a "dilemma tale").** In a **Loma** tale from **Liberia** (Bascom 1972, cited by Isidore Okpewho), one man shot through a rock and killed an elephant. A second followed the shot, butchered the elephant and carried it back through the hole. A third picked a louse from his head, skinned it, and sewed up the elephant in the louse's skin. The tale is called **"Who did the biggest stunt?"** — and listeners argue about it, because **there is no single right answer**.

## An African Puzzle: The Lion, the Goat and the Yam

A puzzle popular among the **western and eastern peoples of southern Nigeria** (Offor 2016): a man must take **a lion, a goat and a yam tuber** across a small river. The clues:

1. The boat holds **the man and only one item** at a time.
2. **Lions are carnivores**, so they eat goats.
3. **Goats eat yams.**

Try it before reading the solution:

<RiverCrossing title="Lion, goat and yam" items="lion, goat, yam" eats="lion>goat, goat>yam" />

<Reveal question="Show the textbook's reasoning and solution">
**Inferences:** if the man takes the **lion** first, the goat eats the yam. If he takes the **yam** first, the lion eats the goat. So he must take the **goat** first — the lion does not eat yams.

1. Take the **goat** across; return alone.
2. Take the **yam** across. He cannot leave it with the goat, so he **brings the goat back**.
3. Leave the goat, take the **lion** across (the lion and the yam are safe together); return alone.
4. Finally, take the **goat** across.

**Seven crossings.** *(Beyond the textbook: taking the lion across in step 2 instead of the yam, and the yam in step 3, also works in seven crossings.)*

"Our fathers didn't need any formal training in logic to be able to resolve such puzzles" — but training strengthens the ability.
</Reveal>

## A Method for Logical Puzzles

<FlowDiagram title="Solving a logical puzzle">
<FlowStep title="1. List the clues">
Number every clue. Treat each as **true** — puzzle clues are premises, not opinions.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="2. Unpack hidden information">
Most clues carry an **implied fact**. "Was an **only child**" → has no brother or sister. "Married **X's sister**" → X has a sister, and the speaker is not X. "Earns **more than** the manager" → is not the manager, and does not earn the least.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="3. Draw a grid">
Put the people in rows and the categories in columns — the **matrix method**. Mark ✗ for every possibility a clue **rules out**.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="4. Eliminate">
When a row or column has only **one blank cell left**, that cell must be ✓. A ✓ then rules out the rest of its row and column.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="5. Assume and test">
If you get stuck, **suppose** one possibility and follow it through. If it leads to a **contradiction**, reject it. This is a *reductio ad absurdum*, used in the murder puzzles below.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="6. Check against every clue">
A correct solution must satisfy **all** the clues at once.
</FlowStep>
</FlowDiagram>

## The Textbook's Worked Puzzles

### I. The oil company

<LogicGrid title="Puzzle I — who holds which post?" rows="Adeshina, Okwudili, Olusegun" cols="Position: Accountant, Manager, Cashier" answer="Adeshina: Cashier; Okwudili: Manager; Olusegun: Accountant">

In an oil-prospecting company in the Niger Delta, the posts of **accountant, manager and cashier** are held by **Adeshina, Okwudili and Olusegun**, not necessarily in that order.

1. The **cashier**, who was an **only child**, earns the **least**.
2. **Olusegun**, who **married Okwudili's sister**, earns **more than the manager**.

</LogicGrid>

<Reveal question="Solution to Puzzle I">
- Olusegun earns more than the manager, so he is **not the manager**. Earning more than someone, he does not earn the least, so he is **not the cashier** either. **Olusegun is the accountant.**
- Okwudili has a sister, so he is **not an only child**, and therefore **not the cashier**. He is not the accountant (Olusegun is). **Okwudili is the manager.**
- By elimination, **Adeshina is the cashier.**
</Reveal>

### II. The election report

An election-monitoring officer, reporting on a ward made up **only of young married couples and their children**, stated:

1. There were **more parents than children**.
2. **Every boy had a sister.**
3. There were **more boys than girls**.
4. There were **no childless couples**.

His report was **rejected** and he was reprimanded. **Why?**

<Reveal question="Solution to Puzzle II">
Let the number of families (couples) be **F**, so there are **2F parents**.

- No couple is childless (4), and every boy has a sister (2). So **every family has at least one girl** — either an only child or the sister of the family's boys. Hence **girls ≥ F**.
- There are more boys than girls (3), so **boys > girls ≥ F**. The total number of children is **boys + girls > 2F**.
- So there are **more children than parents**, which contradicts statement 1. The report is **inconsistent**: its four statements cannot all be true together.
</Reveal>

### III. The hotel murder

**Alhaji Babangida Adebayo** was found shot dead in his hotel room, and three men were arrested. They said:

| Dokunbo | Uwazuruike | Mustapha |
|---|---|---|
| 1. "I didn't do it." | 4. "I didn't do it." | 7. "I didn't do it." |
| 2. "I never saw Uwazuruike before." | 5. "Dokunbo and Mustapha are both friends of mine." | 8. "Dokunbo lied when he said he'd never seen Uwazuruike before." |
| 3. "Sure, I knew Alhaji Adebayo." | 6. "Dokunbo never killed anybody." | 9. "I don't know who did it." |

**Exactly one of each man's three statements is false, and exactly one man is guilty. Who is the murderer?** Test each suspect in turn:

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Suppose Mustapha did it">
Then 7 ("I didn't do it") is **false**, and 9 ("I don't know who did it") is **false** as well — he would know. That is **two false statements** from one man. ❌ **Impossible**, so Mustapha is innocent.
</Tab>
<Tab label="Suppose Dokunbo did it">
Then 1 is false, so **2 and 3 must be true**. Also 6 ("Dokunbo never killed anybody") is false, so **4 and 5 must be true**. But 2 ("I never saw Uwazuruike") and 5 ("Dokunbo is my friend") **cannot both be true**. ❌ **Contradiction**, so Dokunbo is innocent.
</Tab>
<Tab label="Suppose Uwazuruike did it">
Then 4 is false, so **5 and 6 are true**: Dokunbo is his friend. So 2 is **false**, and Dokunbo's 1 and 3 are true. Mustapha's 7 is true and 8 is true (Dokunbo did lie), so his **9 is the false one** — he knows who did it. ✅ **Every man has exactly one false statement.** **Uwazuruike is the murderer.**
</Tab>
</Tabs>

### IV. Politicians and non-politicians

In a riverine community in Delta State, **politicians never tell the truth** and **non-politicians always do**. A stranger, **Mr Don Bosco**, asks the first of three natives, **"Are you a politician?"** The **second native** reports that **the first denied being a politician**. The **third native** says that **the first is a politician**. **How many of the three are politicians?**

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Native 1">
If he is a **politician**, he lies — so he **denies** being one. If he is **not a politician**, he tells the truth — so he **denies** being one. **Either way, native 1 denied being a politician.**
</Tab>
<Tab label="Native 2">
He reported that native 1 denied being a politician — which, as we just saw, is **true**. So native 2 told the truth: he is a **non-politician**.
</Tab>
<Tab label="Native 3">
He says native 1 is a politician. If native 1 **is** a politician, native 3 told the truth and is a **non-politician**. If native 1 is **not**, native 3 lied and **is** a politician. So **exactly one of natives 1 and 3** is a politician.
</Tab>
<Tab label="Answer">
Native 2 is a non-politician, and exactly one of natives 1 and 3 is a politician. **There is exactly one politician among the three** — though we cannot tell which of natives 1 and 3 it is.
</Tab>
</Tabs>

### V. The casino owner

**Chief Dosumu Alakia**, who ran a casino in the Alakia area of Ibadan, was shot dead. Five suspects were arraigned. Each made **three statements: two true and one false**.

| Suspect | Statements |
|---|---|
| **Seyi Akande** | (a) "I did not kill Chief Alakia." (b) "I never owned a revolver in all my life." (c) "Nosa Igunbor did it." |
| **Nduka Okoye** | (a) "I did not kill Chief Alakia." (b) "I never owned a revolver." (c) "The others are all passing the buck." |
| **Musa Yahaya** | (a) "I am innocent." (b) "I never saw Wale Akindele before." (c) "Nosa Igunbor is guilty." |
| **Nosa Igunbor** | (a) "I am innocent." (b) "Wale Akindele is the guilty one." (c) "Seyi Akande did not tell the truth when he said I did it." |
| **Wale Akindele** | (a) "I did not kill Chief Alakia." (b) "Nduka Okoye is the guilty one." (c) "Musa Yahaya and I are old pals." |

<Reveal question="Who is guilty? (show the reasoning)">
1. Nosa's (a) "I am innocent" and (c) "Seyi lied when he said I did it" **mean the same thing**, so they are **both true or both false**. Only one of his statements is false, so **both are true**: Nosa is innocent, and his (b) is the false one.
2. So Musa's (c) "Nosa Igunbor is guilty" is **false**, which makes Musa's (a) and (b) **true**: Musa is innocent and **has never seen Wale**.
3. So Wale's (c) "Musa Yahaya and I are old pals" is **false**, which makes Wale's (a) and (b) **true** — and (b) says **Nduka Okoye is the guilty one**.
4. Check Nduka: his (a) is false, so (b) and (c) must be true. They can be — he never owned a revolver, and the others (Seyi, Musa) were indeed blaming someone else. Seyi's false statement is (c). Every suspect has exactly one false statement. ✅

**Nduka Okoye is guilty.**
</Reveal>

### VI. The flight crew

The textbook's clue 2 reads **"Tam, who is married to Umukoro's sister…"**. "Tam" is a **misprint**: no Tam is on the crew, and the book's own solution treats the clue as being about **Gbenga**.

<LogicGrid title="Puzzle VI — pilot, co-pilot, flight engineer" rows="Osagie, Umukoro, Gbenga" cols="Position: Pilot, Co-pilot, Flight engineer" answer="Osagie: Co-pilot; Umukoro: Pilot; Gbenga: Flight engineer">

On a certain flight, the positions of **pilot, co-pilot and flight engineer** are held by **Osagie, Umukoro and Gbenga**, not necessarily in that order.

1. The **co-pilot**, who was an **only child**, earns the **least**.
2. **Gbenga**, who is married to **Umukoro's sister**, earns **more than the pilot**.

</LogicGrid>

<Reveal question="Solution to Puzzle VI">
- Gbenga earns more than the pilot, so he is **not the pilot**; and since he earns more than someone, he does not earn the least, so he is **not the co-pilot**. **Gbenga is the flight engineer.**

- By elimination, **Umukoro is the pilot.**
</Reveal>

<LogicGrid title="Puzzle VII — six posts in Benin City" rows="Mr Oyeshile, Mr Nduka, Mrs Alozie, Miss Bello, Mr Okoye, Miss Idemudia" cols="Position: Manager, Assistant manager, Cashier, Accountant, Front desk officer, Clerk" answer="Mr Oyeshile: Assistant manager; Mr Nduka: Cashier; Mrs Alozie: Manager; Miss Bello: Clerk; Mr Okoye: Accountant; Miss Idemudia: Front desk officer">

1. The **assistant manager** is the **manager's grandson**.
2. The **cashier** is the **accountant's son-in-law**.
3. **Mr Oyeshile** is a **bachelor**.
4. **Mr Nduka** is **twenty-two** years old.
5. **Miss Bello** is the **front desk officer's step-sister**.
6. **Mr Okoye** is the **manager's neighbour**.

</LogicGrid>

<Reveal question="Solution to Puzzle VII">
Puzzle convention: take the titles at face value. A bachelor or a "Miss" is **unmarried**, and unmarried people are assumed to have **no children or grandchildren**.

1. **Manager** (has a grandson): not Mr Nduka (22 — too young), not Mr Oyeshile (bachelor), not Mr Okoye (the manager's neighbour), not the two unmarried Misses. **Mrs Alozie is the manager.**
2. **Accountant** (has a married daughter): not Oyeshile (unmarried), not Nduka (too young), not the Misses. **Mr Okoye is the accountant.**
3. **Cashier** (the accountant's son-in-law, so a married man): not Oyeshile (bachelor), not the Misses. **Mr Nduka is the cashier.**
4. **Assistant manager** (the manager's grandson): the only man left is **Mr Oyeshile**.
5. **Front desk officer:** not Miss Bello (she is the officer's step-sister). **Miss Idemudia is the front desk officer.**
6. **Clerk:** **Miss Bello.**
</Reveal>

### Exercise 1 — the bus crew

On a luxurious bus to Kaduna, the crew are the **driver, conductor and engineer**: **Adebola, Balogun and Oladipo**. Three passengers have the same names: **Mr Adebola, Mr Balogun and Mr Oladipo**. *(The textbook slips and says "on the train" — the puzzle is adapted from Copi's famous train puzzle.)*

1. Mr Balogun lives in **Lokoja**.
2. The **driver** lives **halfway between Lokoja and Ilorin**.
3. Mr Adebola earns exactly **₦20,000** a year.
4. **Oladipo** once **beat the conductor** at chess.
5. The driver's **next-door neighbour**, one of the passengers, earns **exactly three times** as much as the driver.
6. The passenger living in **Ilorin** has **the same name as the driver**.

**What is the engineer's name?**

<Reveal question="Solution to Exercise 1">
- The driver's neighbour lives **halfway** (where the driver lives). That is not Mr Balogun (Lokoja). It is not Mr Adebola either: ₦20,000 is **not exactly divisible by 3**, so it cannot be exactly three times a (whole-naira) salary. **The neighbour is Mr Oladipo.**
- So **Mr Adebola lives in Ilorin**. The Ilorin passenger shares the driver's name, so **the driver is Adebola**.
- Oladipo beat the conductor, so **Oladipo is not the conductor**; he is not the driver either. **Oladipo is the engineer** (and Balogun the conductor).
</Reveal>

### Exercise 2 — four professionals

<LogicGrid title="Exercise 2 — occupation and dress" rows="Dupe, Ebun, Chinyere, Tolu" cols="Occupation: Doctor, Lawyer, Pilot, Professor | Dress: White, Yellow, Pink, Blue" answer="Dupe: Pilot, Yellow; Ebun: Professor, Pink; Chinyere: Lawyer, White; Tolu: Doctor, Blue">

Dupe, Ebun, Chinyere and Tolu are a **doctor, lawyer, pilot and professor**, and each wore a **white, yellow, pink or blue** dress.

1. The **doctor** beats **Ebun** at ludo.
2. **Chinyere and the pilot** often played Monopoly **with the women in pink and blue**.
3. **Dupe and the professor** envied the woman in **blue**…
4. …but she was **not the lawyer**, who **always wore white**.

</LogicGrid>

<Reveal question="Solution to Exercise 2">
- From clue 2, Chinyere is not the pilot, and **neither Chinyere nor the pilot wears pink or blue**. So between them they wear **white and yellow**.
- The lawyer wears white (clue 4). The pilot is not the lawyer, so **Chinyere wears white and is the lawyer**, and **the pilot wears yellow**.
- The **doctor and the professor** wear pink and blue. The professor doesn't wear blue (clue 3), so **professor → pink, doctor → blue**.
- Dupe is not the professor, and doesn't wear blue (so is not the doctor). **Dupe is the pilot (yellow).** Ebun is not the doctor (clue 1), so **Ebun is the professor (pink)**, and **Tolu is the doctor (blue)**.
</Reveal>

### Exercise 3 — four artistes

<LogicGrid title="Exercise 3 — each man's field" rows="Abel, Babs, Chris, Dani" cols="Field: Dancer, Painter, Singer, Writer" answer="Abel: Dancer; Babs: Singer; Chris: Writer; Dani: Painter">

1. Abel and Chris were **in the audience** the night the **singer** made his debut.
2. Both **Babs and the writer** have had their portraits painted from life **by the painter**.
3. The **writer**, whose **biography of Dani** was a bestseller, is planning to write **the biography of Abel**.
4. **Abel has never heard of Chris.**

</LogicGrid>

<Reveal question="Solution to Exercise 3">
- The writer is not Babs (clue 2), not Dani or Abel (their biographer, clue 3). **Chris is the writer.**
- The singer is not Abel or Chris (clue 1).
- The painter is not Babs (clue 2) and not Chris (the writer). Nor is he Abel: the painter painted the writer (Chris) from life, so he knows Chris, yet Abel has **never heard of Chris** (clue 4). **Dani is the painter.**
- So **Babs is the singer** and **Abel is the dancer**.
</Reveal>

### Exercise 4 — five war buddies

<LogicGrid title="Exercise 4 — occupation and city" rows="Wole, Bola, Peju, Haruna, Nosa" cols="Occupation: Printer, Writer, Barber, Neurologist, Heating-contractor | City: Wole Plains, Bolaville, Pejuburg, Haruna Ferry, Nosaville" answer="Wole: Barber, Haruna Ferry; Bola: Neurologist, Wole Plains; Peju: Heating-contractor, Bolaville; Haruna: Printer, Nosaville; Nosa: Writer, Pejuburg">

1. **No man lives in the city with a name like his**, and **no man's occupation has the same initial** as his name **or** his city.
2. The **barber** doesn't live in **Pejuburg**.
3. **Bola** is neither the heating-contractor nor the printer, and doesn't live in Pejuburg or Haruna Ferry.
4. **Mr Haruna lives in Nosaville** and is neither the barber nor the writer.
5. **Wole** does not live in Bolaville; nor does **Nosa**, who is neither a barber nor a heating-contractor.

**Can you determine the city in which Nosa lives?**

</LogicGrid>

<Reveal question="Solution to Exercise 4">
- **Haruna** (Nosaville): not barber or writer (clue 4), not heating-contractor (H = his initial) or neurologist (N = his city's initial). **Haruna is the printer.**
- **Bola**: not heating-contractor or printer, and not barber (B = his initial), so he is the writer or the neurologist. His city is not Bolaville, Pejuburg, Haruna Ferry or Nosaville, so **Bola lives in Wole Plains**. Then he can't be the writer (W), so **Bola is the neurologist**.
- **Nosa**: not barber or heating-contractor; the printer and neurologist are taken. **Nosa is the writer.**
- Bolaville can't be Wole's or Nosa's (clue 5), and Peju can't live in Pejuburg, so **Peju lives in Bolaville**. He can't be the barber (B), so **Peju is the heating-contractor**, and **Wole is the barber**.
- The barber doesn't live in Pejuburg, so **Wole lives in Haruna Ferry**, leaving **Nosa in Pejuburg**.

**Yes — Nosa lives in Pejuburg.**
</Reveal>

### Exercise 5 — who ate the apple?

After someone in the Garden of Eden ate the apple, **only one resident answered every question truthfully**. The others all told some lies, **no two of them the same number**.

| Speaker | (i) | (ii) | (iii) | (iv) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Adam** | The snake ate the apple. | The snake was in the garden. | Eve has not weeded the garden. | Abel failed to do his chores. |
| **Cain** | Abel ate the apple. | Abel doesn't always tell the truth. | Mother has always preferred Abel. | The snake never lies. |
| **Abel** | Cain ate the apple. | The snake can't see over the hedge. | I have done my chores. | The snake ate the apple. |
| **Snake** | I was not in the garden. | Eve ate the apple. | Cain is Eve's favourite son. | I can't see over the hedge. |
| **Eve** | Adam ate the apple. | We are not all equally truthful. | I was out weeding the garden. | The snake lied. |

<Reveal question="Solution to Exercise 5">
Four liars with **different** numbers of lies, out of four statements each, must have told **1, 2, 3 and 4 lies**. One speaker told none.

- **Cain can't be the truthful one:** he says "the snake never lies", which would make **two** truthful speakers.
- **Abel can't be:** he says both Cain and the snake ate the apple.
- **Adam can't be:** then Eve has 2 lies, Cain would need 3, and Abel could have only 2 or 3 — one number too few to go round.
- **The snake can't be:** then Cain has 2 lies, Eve must have 3, and Adam and Abel can each have only 2 or 3 — again impossible.
- **Eve is truthful**, and it works. Adam ate the apple, Eve weeded the garden, and the snake lied. The snake then has **1** lie (only "Eve ate the apple" is false). Cain has **3**: (i), (iii) and (iv) are false; (ii) is true. Adam has **4**: all false, so Abel did do his chores. Abel has **2**: (i) and (iv) are false.

The counts 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 are all different. ✅ **Adam ate the apple.**
</Reveal>

### Exercise 6 — shopping at UTC Stores

<LogicGrid title="Exercise 6 — what did each woman buy?" rows="Mrs Adamu, Ms Bako, Ms Dudu, Ms Emman, Ms Fika, Ms Tafi" cols="Bought: Book, Dress, Handbag, Necktie, Hat, Lamp" answer="Mrs Adamu: Book; Ms Bako: Dress; Ms Dudu: Handbag; Ms Emman: Necktie; Ms Fika: Lamp; Ms Tafi: Hat">

Six women each went straight to the floor selling the one article she wanted: a **book, dress, handbag, necktie, hat or lamp**.

1. **All except Mrs Adamu** entered the lift on the **first floor**; **two men** also got in.
2. **Ms Dudu and the necktie buyer** got off at the **2nd floor**. **Dresses** are sold on the **3rd**. The **two men** got off at the **4th**.
3. The **lamp buyer** got off at the **5th**, leaving **Ms Tafi** alone to the **6th**.

- The lift carried 7 people. Leaving in order: 2 at the 2nd floor, then either one woman at the 3rd and two men at the 4th, or three at the 4th. Either way **the 6th person out is the lamp buyer** at the 5th floor. **Ms Fika bought the lamp.**

### Exercise 12 — the Artful Omego

<LogicGrid title="Exercise 12 — where, and how much?" rows="Mr Azubuike, Mr Onyeka, Mr Osasuwa, Mr Ajayi, Mr Abu" cols="Place: Owelle Quarters, Broad Street, Nnamdi Crescent, Ojukwu Square, Okpala Square | Cash (₦): 1 000, 2 000, 4 000, 8 000, 10 000" answer="Mr Azubuike: Owelle Quarters, 8 000; Mr Onyeka: Broad Street, 2 000; Mr Osasuwa: Nnamdi Crescent, 10 000; Mr Ajayi: Ojukwu Square, 1 000; Mr Abu: Okpala Square, 4 000">

## Conclusion

Puzzles are **more intellectually demanding than riddles** because they supply **clues** from which answers must be **argued**. Riddles challenge the intellect, but their **ultimate significance is entertainment**. Logical puzzles exercise and sharpen our natural capacity to **use good arguments in resolving our problems**. Logic clears away the **mental cobwebs** that befog our reasoning.

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. Distinguish a logical puzzle from a riddle, with examples.">
A logical puzzle (Bello 2000) is a question or series of questions with a mass of information given as true; the solution consists of answers supported by arguments whose premises come from the puzzle's statement (e.g. finding who is the cashier from clues about salaries and siblings). A riddle needs no extra information, may have more than one acceptable answer if its proponent can argue for it, sharpens mental alertness and is valued mainly for entertainment (e.g. "Where will the survivors be buried?"; the Loma dilemma tale "Who did the biggest stunt?").
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Solve the lion–goat–yam puzzle and explain your reasoning.">
The boat holds the man and one item; lions eat goats and goats eat yams. The goat must go first, because leaving it with either other item is fatal. Take the goat across and return; take the yam across and bring the goat back; take the lion across and return; take the goat across — seven crossings, with nothing ever eaten.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Describe a general method for solving logical puzzles.">
List and number the clues, treating them as true. Unpack the hidden facts ("only child" means no siblings; "earns more than the manager" means neither the manager nor the lowest earner). Draw a grid of people against categories and mark the possibilities each clue rules out. Use elimination: a row or column with one blank left must be ✓. When stuck, assume one possibility and follow it through — reject it if it leads to a contradiction. Finally, check the solution against every clue.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Explain why the election officer's report (Puzzle II) was rejected.">
With F families there are 2F parents. No childless couples and "every boy had a sister" mean every family has at least one girl, so girls ≥ F. More boys than girls means boys > girls ≥ F, so children > 2F — more children than parents, contradicting the first statement. The report is inconsistent.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Logic sharpens a natural ability** — it clears the **"mental cobwebs"** (Offor). Solving puzzles is **"exercise in reasoning"**.
- **Puzzle** (Bello): questions plus **clues given as true**; one answer, **argued from the clues**. **Riddle**: no extra clues; possibly **several arguable answers**; valued for **entertainment**.
- African tradition is rich in both: **moonlight riddle sessions**, **dilemma tales**, the **lion–goat–yam** crossing (goat first; seven crossings).
- **Method:** list the clues → unpack hidden facts → **grid** → **eliminate** → **assume and test** (reductio) → check every clue.
- Worked answers: the oil company (Olusegun accountant, Okwudili manager, Adeshina cashier); the election report (inconsistent); the hotel murder (**Uwazuruike**); politicians (**exactly one**); the casino (**Nduka Okoye**); the flight crew (Gbenga engineer, Osagie co-pilot, Umukoro pilot); the micro-finance company (Mrs Alozie manager).
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Know **Bello's definition of a logical puzzle** and at least **three differences** between puzzles and riddles.
- Be able to **solve the lion–goat–yam puzzle** and explain **why the goat goes first**.
- For "who is guilty?" puzzles, use **assume and test**: suppose each suspect is guilty in turn and count the false statements.
- **Show your reasoning.** Examiners reward the chain of inferences, not just the final answer.
