## Why Philosophical Problems Never Go Away

**Philosophical problems** concern **the basic questions of how we should live our lives**. They arise **when principles, beliefs or ideas conflict**, so that similar facts are interpreted in different and seemingly inconsistent ways (Woodhouse 1994).

They are **perennial** (recurring) problems:

- they have **no generally accepted solutions** (Kekes 2014);
- people hold **conflicting viewpoints** on them — e.g. *what is the best way to live?*

The chapter's author is **Bolatito Lanre-Abass**. The point is **not to find one final answer**. It is to **understand the problems and sharpen our conceptual and reflective abilities** for life's dilemmas.

This lesson surveys problems in three branches: **epistemology, metaphysics and ethics**.

## Epistemological Problems

**Epistemology** enquires into the **limits, scope and extent of human knowledge**:

- *What is knowledge? What kinds are there? What are the conditions for true knowledge?*
- It distinguishes **empirical knowledge** (from **experience** alone) from **a priori knowledge** (from **reason** alone), and **direct** from **indirect** knowledge.

### The challenge of skepticism

**Skepticism** is the claim that **knowledge is impossible** — that we are **never justified in claiming to know anything for certain** (Lacey 1976). It **denies the possibility of any knowledge claim**, so philosophers have tried to set **criteria for when something counts as true or false**.

The two schools of the sources of knowledge each cast doubt on the other:

- **Rationalist** (e.g. **Plato**): only **reason** gives reliable knowledge, so the **senses** are doubtful.
- **Empiricist** (e.g. **David Hume**): only the **senses** give reliable knowledge, so **reason alone** is doubtful.

**The root of the skeptical challenge: the belief that knowledge requires certainty.** If any possibility of error nullifies a knowledge claim, then **illusion and hallucination** become major obstacles. And if we cannot rule out error, we **cannot know the external world**.

| Type of skepticism | Claim |
|---|---|
| **Philosophical skepticism** | Since we cannot rule out error, **we cannot have knowledge of the external world** |
| **Methodological skepticism** | We **can** know some things, but must **doubt everything** we claim to know until we reach **what cannot be doubted** — the method of the French rationalist **René Descartes** |
| **Global skepticism** | We **cannot know anything at all** — we can never be sure whether anything is true or false |
| **Local skepticism** | Even if knowledge is possible elsewhere, **for special reasons it is unavailable in some particular area** (Dancy 1991) |

<SortingGame title="Which kind of skepticism?">

```sort
@buckets Philosophical | Methodological | Global | Local
"I will doubt everything until I find something I cannot doubt." => Methodological :: Descartes' method — doubt as a route to certainty.
"We can never know anything whatsoever to be true or false." => Global :: Knowledge is denied across the board.
"Because illusions and hallucinations are always possible, we cannot know the external world." => Philosophical :: Error cannot be ruled out, so external-world knowledge is denied.
"We may know many things, but we can never know what happens after death." => Local :: Knowledge is denied only in one area.
```

</SortingGame>

### Knowing versus feeling certain

**Feeling certain is not the same as knowing:**

- we can **feel certain and still be mistaken**, but we **cannot be mistaken about what we actually know**;
- feeling certain only measures **how strongly we hold a belief**, and a strongly held belief may be false. *"A feeling of certitude, even one of great conviction, may be misleading"* (David Lund 2003).

We have **knowledge** when we have a **relevantly justified true belief**. The evidence must be **conclusive**. If there is **any** possibility that the belief is false, complete certainty is **not rationally justified**.

<Analogy>
You are certain you locked your hostel door — you can "see" yourself doing it. Your roommate returns and finds it open. Your feeling of certainty was real, but it was not knowledge. Knowledge needs **truth plus adequate justification**, not just a strong feeling.
</Analogy>

## Metaphysical Problems

**Metaphysics** studies **the ultimate constituents of reality**. It has two kinds of inquiry (Segal 2000):

- the **most general investigation into the nature of reality**;
- an inquiry into **what is ultimately real**, often in sharp contrast to everyday experience.

Its central concern is **what existence is**. It is closely tied to **ontology** — *"What is existence?"*, *"What types of things exist?"* A philosopher's system for naming, categorising and ordering what exists is **his or her ontology**.

### What exists?

| School | View |
|---|---|
| **Nihilism** | **Nothing (really) exists** — no God, no justice; all is illusion (**descriptive** nihilism). Or: nothing **ought** to exist, **nothing is good**, so we ought to undo everything (**prescriptive** nihilism) |
| **Materialism** | **All that exists is material or physical**; all phenomena, **including the mind**, are caused by material agencies |
| **Idealism** | **Everything that exists, exists in the mind** — of human beings or of God. Reality consists **only of minds and ideas**. **Eliminative idealists:** there are **no material objects** — only experiences and ideas. **Reductive idealists:** material objects exist but are **nothing other than complexes of experience**. *(Beyond the textbook: **George Berkeley** summed up idealism as **esse est percipi — "to be is to be perceived."**)* |

### (a) The problem of universals

Are **all objects concrete, particular things**, or are some of them **universals**?

- A **unicorn**, if it existed, would be a **concrete particular** in space and time, like a horse.
- Some putative objects are **abstract**: if real, they are **timeless and unchangeable** — e.g. the **number five**, or the **class of all dogs**. There is only one number five, though there are many groups of five things.

**Russell:** to know what **justice** is, examine several just acts and find **what they have in common** — that common nature, **"justice itself"**, is found in whatever is just.

| Particulars | Universals |
|---|---|
| **Cannot be in more than one place at a time** | Can be **wholly present in many places at once** |
| **Instantiate** properties, but **nothing instantiates a particular** — it exists **"in itself"** | Have **many instances** in particular things — exist **"in" something else** (Staniland 1972) |

**The example of five blue balls:**

- They **differ** because each is a **different particular**.
- **Realist:** sameness is as real as difference, so the **single entity "blueness"** has an instance in each ball. **Universals exist objectively.**
- **Nominalist:** there are **no universals**. The balls simply **resemble** each other in colour, and that resemblance is **basic**. **Universals are only names** applied to particulars, which alone exist.

<SortingGame title="Realist or nominalist about universals?">

```sort
@buckets Realist | Nominalist
"Blueness is a real entity present in every blue ball." => Realist :: Universals have real, objective existence.
"'Justice' is just a name we give to acts that resemble one another." => Nominalist :: Universals are only names for particulars.
"There is one number five, over and above all the groups of five things." => Realist :: Abstract universals exist.
"Only individual physical things exist; general words do not name real objects." => Nominalist :: Only particulars exist objectively.
```

</SortingGame>

### (b) The mind–body problem

**What is the relationship between the mind and the body?** What is it for a mind to be **housed in a body**, and for a body to **belong to a particular subject**?

- **Dualism:** a person is **two entities** — a **material body** (extended in space and time) and an **immaterial, un-extended mind**. Neither can be reduced to the other.
- **Monism:** a person is **only one entity** — **either** mind **or** body, not both.

| Theory | Claim | Difficulty |
|---|---|---|
| **Interactionism** (Descartes — a **dualist**) | Mind and body are two substances that **causally influence each other**. For Descartes they interact through the **pineal gland**. This matches everyday experience: the world affects my senses; my thoughts affect my speech and actions | How can the **un-extended** affect the **extended**? *"Contact seems incompatible with a thing being immaterial"* (Anscombe and Geach 1971) |
| **Property dualism** | One brain process has **two properties**: a **physical** one (an electrochemical disturbance) and a **mental** one (e.g. the **taste of a sweet**, observable only by its owner) (Lund 2003) | The brain is then **not a purely physical system** |
| **Occasionalism** (**Malebranche**) | **God intervenes** on each occasion: when I hit my foot, **God makes me feel pain** | Invents **superfluous entities** — Herbert Feigl called such laws **"nomological danglers"** |
| **Psycho-physical parallelism** (**Leibniz**) | Mental and physical events run like **two parallel lines** or **two clocks perfectly synchronised by God**, with no interaction | The same objection — God as a "mind–body regulator" |
| **Materialism / central-state materialism** (monist) | We are **purely material beings**; the **mind is nothing but the brain**. **Francis Crick (1994):** your joys, sorrows, memories, ambitions, **sense of identity and free will** "are in fact no more than the behaviour of a vast assembly of nerve cells" | Seems to leave out the felt, first-person side of experience |
| **Functionalism** | **Mental states are functional states**, defined by their **causal relations** — typical causes (e.g. sensory input) and effects (e.g. behaviour). The totality of these is the state's **functional role** | Tries to salvage materialism |
| **Epiphenomenalism** (monist) | **Mental events are caused by brain events but cause nothing physical**. Behaviour comes from muscles and neural impulses alone | **Implausible**: thinking of fried rice makes me **salivate**; anger makes me **break things**. It seems incredible that the mental cannot cause the physical |

<SortingGame title="Which mind–body theory?">

```sort
@buckets Interactionism | Occasionalism | Parallelism | Property dualism | Materialism | Epiphenomenalism
"Mind and body are separate substances that act on each other through the pineal gland." => Interactionism :: Descartes' dualist interactionism.
"When I stub my toe, God steps in so that I feel pain." => Occasionalism :: Malebranche — God as the mind–body regulator.
"Mind and body run like two clocks set by God, never touching." => Parallelism :: Leibniz's psycho-physical parallelism.
"Tasting a sweet is one brain event with a physical side and a mental side." => Property dualism :: One process, two properties.
"Your joys and sorrows are no more than the behaviour of nerve cells." => Materialism :: Crick's "astonishing hypothesis" — the mind is the brain.
"Thoughts are produced by the brain like smoke from a train — they cause nothing." => Epiphenomenalism :: Mental events have no physical effects.
```

</SortingGame>

### (c) Does God exist?

This remains a **metaphysical question and problem**. The main views (Evans and Manis 2009):

| View | Belief |
|---|---|
| **Polytheism** | **Many personal gods** — common among tribal peoples; seen in Greek and Nordic mythology |
| **Dualism** (theological) | **Two opposed gods** — usually one **good**, one **evil** |
| **Henotheism** | Many gods exist, but **allegiance to one** — because it is superior, or the god of **one's own tribe or people** |
| **Monotheism (theism)** | **Only one God** — a **personal** being, supreme in **power, knowledge and moral worth**, who **created everything out of nothing** |
| **Deism** | One God, but He **does not or cannot involve Himself in His creation** |
| **Pantheism** | God is **not a personal being**. God is **identical with nature or the universe as a whole** (associated with some forms of Hinduism and Eastern religions) |
| **Panentheism / absolute monism** | **The universe is in God, but God is more than the universe** (panentheism). **Absolute monism:** God is an **absolute unity** manifested in a **less-than-fully-real** world of apparent plurality |
| **Agnosticism** | The truth about God **cannot be or is not known**, so **suspend judgement** |
| **Atheism** | **Denies God's existence** |
| **Naturalism** | A worldview that **entails atheism** — **no supernatural realm**; the natural order simply exists **"on its own"** |

<SortingGame title="Name the view about God">

```sort
@buckets Polytheism | Henotheism | Monotheism | Deism | Pantheism | Agnosticism | Atheism
"There are many gods, each ruling a part of nature." => Polytheism :: A plurality of personal gods.
"Many gods exist, but we serve only the god of our own people." => Henotheism :: Allegiance to one god among many.
"One personal God created all things out of nothing and is all-powerful and all-good." => Monotheism :: Theism.
"God made the world like a watchmaker, then left it to run by itself." => Deism :: God does not involve Himself in creation.
"God and nature are one; the universe itself is divine." => Pantheism :: God is identical with the universe as a whole.
"We cannot know whether God exists, so I suspend judgement." => Agnosticism :: The truth about God is not known.
"There is no God." => Atheism :: It denies God's existence.
```

</SortingGame>

## Ethical Problems

**Ethics** is the **systematic examination of human conduct** — the systems of value and custom in people's lives (Crisp 2000). It can be approached in four ways:

| Approach | Concern |
|---|---|
| **Normative ethics** | **How people ought to behave** — prescribes principles of conduct |
| **Descriptive ethics** | **How people actually behave** in different societies — a **scientific** description and comparison of moralities |
| **Meta-ethics** | The **meaning and justification** of ethical terms and statements |
| **Applied ethics** | Dilemmas arising from social problems — **suicide, rape, war, terrorism, prostitution** |

Problems arise from questions like these:

- How do we tell **right from wrong**?
- What makes an issue **moral**?
- **Why should I be moral?**
- How do I **justify** my moral claims?
- Are moral statements **factual or value-laden**?

### (a) How do we justify moral claims?

When we say good or bad, right or wrong, beautiful or ugly, we make **value judgements**. Are values **in things**, independent of us?

| Value subjectivism | Value objectivism |
|---|---|
| **Value is contributed by the subject of experience**, not found in the object itself (Lund 2003). Value talk **expresses our feelings and attitudes** | Value statements **describe their objects**, which have **"value properties"** — a beautiful artwork has beauty **as it has shape and size**, independent of anyone's experience |

On the subjectivist analysis, a **conscious being is necessary but not sufficient** for value to exist. The being must also have **feelings, concerns, desires and purposes**. Then:

- **good or right** is what **furthers its interests** — enjoyment;
- **bad or wrong** is what **frustrates** them — suffering.

### (b) Why should I live a moral life?

| Reason | Argument |
|---|---|
| **Sociological** | **Without morality, social life is nearly impossible**. Our actions affect others, so we should be moral in order to affect them positively |
| **Psychological** | People care about **reputation** (what others think) or **conscience** (doing right); either way it is sensible to live morally |
| **Theological** | **God has commanded** us to do good; many religions teach an **afterlife of reward or punishment** |
| **"Law of nemesis"** | *Do unto others what you would want them to do unto you* — to avoid being **"paid back in our own wicked coin"** |
| **Self-respect** | **Being bad is self-disrespecting**; **self-respect is necessary for happiness**; so **being good is necessary for happiness**. Self-respect also needs honest **self-knowledge** of one's moral acts |
| **Seeing true value** | **Good people have good values** — they value what **deserves** to be valued |

### (c) Judging the rightness of actions

| Theory | Claim |
|---|---|
| **Deontology** | Rightness depends on **duty and obligation**; actions are **right or wrong in themselves, regardless of consequences**. **Rule deontology:** acts are right because they **conform to moral rules**. **Act deontology:** we **intuit** the rightness of each particular act (Fox and DeMarco 1990) |
| **Consequentialism** | Rightness depends **solely on consequences**. It includes **utilitarianism, egoism and altruism** |
| **Utilitarianism** | Actions are good insofar as they **increase happiness** and reduce suffering — the **principle of utility**: *"the greatest happiness for the greatest number."* **Act utilitarianism:** assess the consequences of **each act**. **Rule utilitarianism:** obey the **rules** that produce the greatest happiness |
| **Egoism** | Priority to **self-interest**. **Ethical egoism** (normative): people **ought** to pursue their own interest. **Psychological egoism** (descriptive): people **in fact always** pursue their own interest |
| **Ethical relativism** | Moral principles are valid **only relative to a place and time**. Its version **moral subjectivism** says good and right are distinguished **only by individual feeling** — contrasted with **moral objectivism**, which holds there is **one correct moral code**, with some judgements objectively true |
| **Situation ethics** | Rightness depends on the **circumstances** of the action |
| **Virtue ethics** | Focuses on the **character of the actor**, not the act. **Aristotle's** virtues include compassion, veracity, trustworthiness, integrity, honesty, courage, humility and justice |
| **Hedonism** | **Pleasure is the highest or only good**. **Ethical hedonism** (prescriptive): people **ought** to seek pleasure. **Psychological hedonism** (descriptive): people **in fact** seek pleasure |

<SortingGame title="Which ethical theory is speaking?">

```sort
@buckets Deontology | Utilitarianism | Egoism | Relativism | Virtue ethics | Hedonism
"Lying is wrong in itself, even if a lie would produce good results." => Deontology :: Right and wrong depend on duty, regardless of consequences.
"Choose the action that brings the greatest happiness to the greatest number." => Utilitarianism :: The principle of utility.
"Everyone ought to pursue their own interest." => Egoism :: Ethical egoism — a normative claim.
"What is right in Lagos in 2025 may not be right elsewhere or at another time." => Relativism :: Moral principles are valid relative to place and time.
"Ask not 'what should I do?' but 'what kind of person should I be?'" => Virtue ethics :: It focuses on the character of the actor.
"Pleasure is the only thing good in itself." => Hedonism :: Pleasure as the highest good.
```

</SortingGame>

**Conclusion:** these problems are **"insurmountable" because they are controversial** — no definite answer has been given. That is exactly why they train our reasoning.

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. What is skepticism? Distinguish its four forms and explain the difference between knowing and feeling certain.">
Skepticism is the claim that knowledge is impossible — we are never justified in claiming certain knowledge. It arises from the belief that knowledge requires certainty, and from the possibility of illusion and hallucination. Philosophical skepticism: we cannot know the external world. Methodological skepticism (Descartes): doubt everything until you reach what cannot be doubted. Global skepticism: nothing can be known. Local skepticism: knowledge is unavailable in a particular area. Feeling certain only measures the strength of a belief and may be mistaken; knowledge is a relevantly justified true belief with conclusive evidence.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Explain the problem of universals, contrasting realism and nominalism.">
The problem is whether all objects are concrete particulars or whether some are universals — abstract, timeless entities that can be wholly present in many places at once (e.g. blueness, the number five, justice). Particulars cannot be in two places at once and exist "in themselves". Realists say universals exist objectively: the five blue balls share the single entity "blueness". Nominalists say only particulars exist and universals are mere names: the balls simply resemble one another, and resemblance is basic.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Discuss the mind–body problem and four theories that address it.">
The problem is how an immaterial mind relates to a material body. Dualism posits two entities; monism only one. Interactionism (Descartes): mind and body influence each other through the pineal gland — but how can the un-extended affect the extended? Property dualism: one brain process has physical and mental properties. Occasionalism (Malebranche): God produces the correlation on each occasion. Parallelism (Leibniz): two synchronised clocks. These invent "nomological danglers" (Feigl). Materialism (Crick): the mind is the brain. Functionalism: mental states are defined by their causal roles. Epiphenomenalism: the mind is caused by the brain but causes nothing — implausible given salivating and anger.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Outline the major views on the existence of God.">
Polytheism (many gods); theological dualism (two opposed gods, good and evil); henotheism (many gods, allegiance to one); monotheism or theism (one personal, supreme creator); deism (one God who does not intervene); pantheism (God is identical with nature); panentheism and absolute monism (the universe in God, God more than the universe; a less-than-real plurality); agnosticism (the truth is unknown — suspend judgement); atheism (denies God); naturalism (no supernatural realm; entails atheism).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="5. Why should one be moral, and how do deontology and utilitarianism judge actions?">
Reasons: sociological (social life is impossible without morality), psychological (reputation or conscience), theological (God's command, the afterlife), the "law of nemesis" (the Golden Rule), self-respect (necessary for happiness), and recognising true values. Deontology judges acts right or wrong in themselves by duty, regardless of consequences — rule deontology (conformity to rules) versus act deontology (intuiting each act). Utilitarianism (consequentialist) judges by consequences — the greatest happiness for the greatest number — either act by act or by rules.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- Philosophical problems are **perennial**: they arise from **conflicting beliefs** and have **no generally accepted solutions**.
- **Epistemology**: empirical vs a priori; **skepticism** comes from the belief that **knowledge requires certainty**. Four types: **philosophical, methodological (Descartes), global, local**. **Feeling certain ≠ knowing**; knowledge = **relevantly justified true belief** (Lund).
- **Metaphysics**: **nihilism**, **materialism**, **idealism** (eliminative, reductive; Berkeley). **Universals**: **realists** vs **nominalists**. **Mind–body**: dualism vs monism — interactionism (Descartes, pineal gland), property dualism, occasionalism (Malebranche), parallelism (Leibniz), materialism (Crick), functionalism, epiphenomenalism. **God**: polytheism, henotheism, theism, deism, pantheism, agnosticism, atheism, naturalism.
- **Ethics**: normative, descriptive, meta-ethics, applied; **value subjectivism vs objectivism**; why be moral (sociological, psychological, theological, nemesis, self-respect); theories: **deontology** (act/rule), **utilitarianism** (act/rule), **egoism** (ethical/psychological), **relativism**, situation ethics, **virtue ethics** (Aristotle), **hedonism** (ethical/psychological).
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Paired terms are MCQ favourites: **realist/nominalist**, **dualism/monism**, **ethical vs psychological** egoism and hedonism (**prescriptive vs descriptive**), **act vs rule**.
- Match **names to theories**: Descartes — interactionism and methodological skepticism; **Malebranche** — occasionalism; **Leibniz** — parallelism; **Crick** — the astonishing hypothesis; **Feigl** — "nomological danglers".
- Know the difference between **deism** (God does not intervene) and **pantheism** (God = nature).
- For "why be moral?" essays, cover at least **four reasons** with a short argument each.
