## You Are Already a Philosopher

The chapter's author, **Adeshina Afolayan**, sets out to show two things:

1. most people do not realise that **in denying philosophy, they deny their own intrinsic nature**;
2. **philosophy plays a significant role in moulding a meaningful existence**, for individuals and for society.

---

## Why Is Philosophy Inevitable?

### Reason 1: We are born curious

Children **pester** their parents with questions:

- Where do babies come from?
- Who created God?
- What is this, and what is that?

Busy parents snap: *"Can't you see I'm busy?"* This shows two things: **children are curious beings driven to ask unlimited questions**, and **adults are too busy to accommodate that curiosity**.

| Thinker | Point |
|---|---|
| **Plato** | **Philosophy developed out of wonder and curiosity** |
| **Aristotle** (Plato's disciple) | **Curiosity pushes human beings to want to know what they do not yet know** |
| **Gareth Matthews (1976)** | What philosophers do "in rather disciplined and sustained ways" is closer than we think to what **children do naturally** — "to have philosophical thoughts is for them as natural as making music or playing games, and quite as much a part of **being human**" |
| **Jostein Gaarder (1996)** | "If a newborn baby could talk, it would probably say something about **what an extraordinary world** it had come into" |
| **Bertrand Russell (1959)** | Philosophy "has at least the power of **asking questions which increase the interest of the world**, and show the **strangeness and wonder** lying just below the surface even in the commonest things of life" |

Unlike animals, which care for little beyond what their biology demands (food, shelter, even love), **humans are the only species conscious of having experiences**. You are not only reading this page; **you are aware that you are reading it**. So we constantly try to **make sense of our environment and our place in it**:

- whether it will rain;
- whether government removes the fuel subsidy;
- whether to marry or take a bribe;
- whether being honest pays;
- whether life is meaningful, and whether heaven and hell exist.

**Then why don't adults philosophise?** As we grow up, **we take the universe for granted** — it becomes a **habit**. We rush past flowers, sunsets, rainbows, the Milky Way, and even a rare eclipse (*"an average Nigerian will likely give up a rare eclipse of the moon for a good movie on Africa Magic!"*). Society weighs us down with busyness. **We were all created as philosophers, but we become "philosophers who do not philosophise."**

### Reason 2: Ideas shape the quality of our lives

**Philosophy fundamentally determines the quality and essence of our lives**, because most of our actions and judgements rest on **ideas and beliefs about the world and our place in it**:

- If you are **religious**, you have judged that **there is more to reality than what we see**.
- If you live by the **Golden Rule** (*"Do unto others as you want them to do unto you"*), you believe that **actions can be categorised as good or bad**.

We rarely invent these ideas. We absorb them from the **beer parlour, parents, the newspaper stand, friends, the barber's shop, the mechanic's workshop, school, church and mosque**. They are at work whenever we express our **hopes, fears, values, ideals, ambitions and attitudes**.

### Philosophy hiding in everyday life

| Everyday situation | The philosophical problem it raises |
|---|---|
| Watching robots in a factory, you wonder whether **you too are a programmed robot** | **Free will** — are we actually free to do what we do? |
| Fuel prices rise again; you wonder whether you have **any duty to obey the government** or pay tax | **Political obligation** — when is obeying the government justified? |
| You tell your children "always tell the truth"; one asks *"How many truths do we have?"*, another *"Are all lies bad?"* | **The nature of truth and lies** |
| The president pardons sixteen prisoners after ten years; a friend says they should have been killed because prison hardens them | **Philosophy of law** — does punishment **reform or deter**? |
| Your spouse wakes terrified from a dream: *"It was so real"*; you reply *"Dreams are not real"* | **Reality versus appearance** |
| You want a law stopping churches and mosques using outdoor loudspeakers; a friend says it violates freedom of expression | **How best to govern a good society** and regulate its members' freedom |

**Why we still don't philosophise** — we use these ideas but **take them at face value**:

1. we have **no clear picture** of the ideas and beliefs we claim as ours — we hold them **arbitrarily**;
2. we never **examine** their **strength, weakness and reasonableness**.

The parent says *"Just do as I say and stop asking stupid questions!"*; the husband insists dreams are unreal and goes back to sleep.

<SortingGame title="Which philosophical problem is this?">

```sort
@buckets Free will | Political obligation | Nature of truth | Purpose of punishment | Reality vs appearance | Governing freedom
"If robots only do what they are programmed to do, maybe I am the same." => Free will :: Are we really free to do what we do?
"Why should I pay tax to a government that does nothing for me?" => Political obligation :: Is obedience to government justified?
"Daddy, are all lies bad?" => Nature of truth :: The nature of truth and lies.
"Ten years in prison cannot change a criminal — it only hardens him." => Purpose of punishment :: Does punishment reform or deter?
"The dream felt so real — how do I know I am not dreaming now?" => Reality vs appearance :: What is real and what only appears real?
"Banning outdoor loudspeakers would violate worshippers' freedom of expression." => Governing freedom :: How to govern a good society and regulate freedom.
```

</SortingGame>

## What Then Is Philosophy?

We use the word loosely every day:

- *"He took the calamity with philosophic calm."*
- *"My philosophy of business: never trust a person who comes for credit!"*
- *"Our corporate philosophy: reliable products with good service."*
- *"My sport philosophy: winning isn't the most important thing — it's the only thing!"*
- *"My marital philosophy: my wife or nobody else!"*

Here "philosophy" means **a general outlook, policy or principle that guides what we do or who we are**. That sense is not wrong, but philosophers have offered many fuller definitions:

- the attempt to **understand the universe as a whole**;
- an examination of **man's moral responsibilities and social obligations**;
- an effort to **fathom divine intentions** and humanity's place in them;
- an effort to **ground natural science**;
- a rigorous examination of the **origin, extent and validity of human ideas**;
- an exploration of **will or consciousness** in the universe; an examination of the values of **truth, goodness and beauty**.

**Start from the Greek:** ***philosophia*** — ***philos*** (love) + ***sophia*** (wisdom) — **"the love of wisdom."**

<Analogy>
**Why "love" of wisdom?** Think of a relationship (Lawhead 2002). Two people who fall in love face endless questions they must keep working at. **The moment they relax and think the relationship is "figured out", it starts to collapse.** A philosopher is a lover of wisdom in an **ongoing quest** — exploring new ideas, re-examining old ones, and confronting undiscovered dimensions of experience. Philosophy is never "finished".
</Analogy>

**A working definition:** philosophy is **a reflective or meditative human attempt to systematically and critically study human experience**.

- It is a ***human*** attempt — we cannot escape it.
- The ideas by which we make sense of existence always need **modification, justification, evaluation and improvement**.

**The wisdom philosophy seeks** is **a comprehensive set of beliefs and ideas that acts as a general vision** of:

- the universe;
- a person's place in it;
- the goals a person may productively pursue.

Philosophy promotes **human understanding** — not for its own sake, but to guide **action**. It helps us **cope with the uncertainties of the universe and live a meaningful life**. So **human survival is tied to human understanding**.

The "love of wisdom" gives two conceptions of philosophy:

| Philosophy as **worldview** | Philosophy as **critical thinking** |
|---|---|
| The **broad or popular** conception | The **narrow, technical or academic** conception |
| **Wisdom for living** — a guide to life and action | **Examining** that guide — "an unexamined life is not worth living" |

## Wisdom for Living: Philosophy as Worldview

Philosophers have long been preoccupied with **constructing worldviews** — "philosophies of life". **"Worldview" and "philosophy" are interchangeable**: a worldview is **the highest manifestation of philosophy**, and **philosophy's function is to guide life and action**. *(The German term is **Weltanschauung** — beyond the textbook.)*

**Definitions of a worldview:**

- *"A point of view on the world, a perspective on things, a way of looking at the cosmos from a particular vantage point"* (Wolters 1983).
- *"A set of beliefs about the important issues in life"*; *"a general view of the world"*.
- *"Visions of the world or aspects of it, whose primary function is to provide a broad or general guide for **perceiving, feeling and transforming reality**"* (Oladipo 2008).

**In summary**, a worldview is **a comprehensive set of ideas** that explains:

1. **the basic nature of the world** you live in;
2. **your own basic nature**;
3. **the achievable goals** you can pursue, and **how you should act** to reach them.

### The seven questions behind every worldview

| | Question |
|---|---|
| (a) | What is **the nature of the world**? How is it structured and how does it function? |
| (b) | **Why** is the world the way it is, and not different? **Where did the universe come from?** |
| (c) | **Where are we going** in the universe? What is the fate of human and non-human life? |
| (d) | What **future** do we anticipate as a species, and how do we choose a suitable one? |
| (e) | How are we to **act, create and transform** the universe? What principles should guide our actions? **What determines good and evil? What gives meaning to my life?** |
| (f) | How do we **construct a picture of the world** that genuinely answers (a), (b) and (c)? |
| (g) | **Where do we begin** to get these answers? |

Answers come **gradually, in bits and pieces**, from personal experience and the conditions of living, until one achieves **a picture of the universe and one's place in it**.

### Why must you build a worldview?

A life is a **composite of ideas and beliefs** behind decisions, actions and judgements. That set of ideas is your worldview — **a conceptual compass** for navigating a universe you have never inhabited before. Whether we know it or not, we all have one. It covers ideas about:

- money, children, clothes, food and education;
- God, the soul, destiny and reincarnation;
- success, death, work and government;
- freedom, justice, equality, reality, good and bad.

**H. S. Staniland (1979)** calls these the **"ideas we live by"**, or **"vitally-important ideas"**. The bigger the idea (*"What is a human being?"*, *"What is political equality?"*), the more decisions it affects. Philosophy links **belief and action**: **if you believe anything, you must be prepared to act on it.**

There are **three reasons** to build a worldview:

1. **Self-insight** — it tells you **who you are**: what you value, your needs, purposes, strengths and weaknesses. You have **no intellectual maturity** until you have formulated some view of the world, however naive.
2. **Survival** — we need a **practical knowledge of the physical world** to act reasonably. Humans are few and weak compared with ants and termites (numerous) or sharks and elephants (strong), so a worldview is a **practical survival strategy**.
3. **Psychological confidence** — understanding your place in the universe, and where everything will end, gives **confidence, satisfaction and happiness**, and the courage to face life's troubles. **Religion or science** usually serves this purpose.

**Worldviews are not fixed.** They reflect a **dynamic world**, **dynamic human beings** and changing cultures. They are also **partial and incomplete**, so they need **constant reflection and revision**. Because the world has become a habit, we hold ideas **habitually**, with true and false beliefs mixed together. An **unstable worldview** hinders action and leaves us **confused**.

## "An Unexamined Life Is Not Worth Living": Philosophy as Critical Thinking

Since worldviews give only **partial knowledge** of a vast universe, the best we can do is to **examine and re-examine our ideas and beliefs critically**, in the light of new knowledge, information and experience. Hence **Socrates**:

> **"An unexamined life is not worth living."**

Most of us take our worldview for granted, just as we take the world for granted. We never sift our beliefs for **contradictions, inconsistencies and illogicalities** — which is why we are **pulled in many directions**.

<Analogy>
Living with an unexamined worldview is like **looking at the world through broken spectacles**, or **driving in heavy rain without working windscreen wipers**. You cannot see clearly until you change the lenses or fix the wipers. **Critical thinking is the new lens and the working wiper.**
</Analogy>

For Socrates, a life devoted only to buying, selling, amassing material things, marrying and having children — **without asking why** — is worthless. The real questions are:

- **Why** do you do these things?
- **What reasons** do you have?
- **Are they reasonable?**

Follow such questions to their logical conclusions, and **you have stepped into the realm of philosophising**.

**H. S. Staniland** defines philosophy as **"the criticism of the ideas we live by"** (1979). Critical thinking shows **how all our ideas hang together in one unified, coherent form**, and confronts the **assumptions** behind our beliefs.

### Three tests for a worldview

| Test | Question to ask | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Factual adequacy** | Does the belief **agree with relevant known facts and experience**? | If you believe the world will end one day, does that belief take account of the **scientific facts** about the origin and end of the world? |
| **Logical coherence** | Do your beliefs **hang together**, or **contradict** one another? If they contradict, at least one is false | Believing both that *"beauty is in the eye of the beholder"* and that *"some works of art are superior to others"*; or that *"we are all responsible for our actions"* and that *"punishing criminals is inhuman"* |
| **Explanatory capacity** | Can the worldview **show how the entities of the universe relate and interact**? | This capacity lets us understand and interact smoothly with the physical world, and **shape and transform** it to suit our goals |

### Two further dimensions of critical thinking

- **Conceptual analysis:** giving **precise, definite meaning** to the words you use about your beliefs, and establishing **logical relationships between concepts**. What, for example, is the relationship between **freedom** and **punishment**? Get this right and you avoid **saying one thing and meaning another**.
- **Reconstruction of ideas:** after scrutiny, you may **abandon or reject** some beliefs, and then **rebuild** your worldview with ideas that fit better.
  - *Example:* a Christian who believes human life is futile and vain might **reject** the Nigerian habit of **extravagant burials**.
  - In its place, they might **adopt** the Islamic practice of burying the dead **on the day of death, without excessive display**.

<FlowDiagram title="From unexamined belief to a better worldview">
<FlowStep title="1. Wonder">
Reactivate curiosity: stop taking the world — and your beliefs — for granted.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="2. Surface the belief">
Name an idea you live by, e.g. "Honesty never pays in Nigeria."
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="3. Conceptual analysis">
What exactly do "honesty" and "pays" mean? Money only, or trust, reputation and peace of mind?
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="4. Test it">
**Factual adequacy** (do the facts support it?), **logical coherence** (does it contradict your other beliefs, e.g. that you want honest friends?), **explanatory capacity** (does it explain how people and institutions actually behave?).
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="5. Reconstruct">
Abandon or refine the belief and adopt one that fits better, then **act on it** — philosophy links belief and action.
</FlowStep>
</FlowDiagram>

<SortingGame title="Which test of a worldview is being applied?">

```sort
@buckets Factual adequacy | Logical coherence | Explanatory capacity
"Does my belief that the earth is flat agree with satellite photographs?" => Factual adequacy :: Checking the belief against known facts.
"I say beauty is in the eye of the beholder, yet I insist some songs are objectively better — these clash." => Logical coherence :: Two beliefs that contradict each other.
"Does my view help me understand how rainfall, farming and prices affect one another?" => Explanatory capacity :: Showing how things in the universe relate and interact.
"I hold people responsible for their actions but think all punishment is inhuman — can both be right?" => Logical coherence :: The textbook's own example of an incoherent pair.
"Is my belief that the world will end next year consistent with what science knows?" => Factual adequacy :: Agreement with relevant known facts.
```

</SortingGame>

## Conclusion

Doing philosophy is **an essential part of human nature** — a **humanistic quest to understand an uncertain, largely unknown world** we are fated to live in for a time.

- Philosophers seek, in a **ceaseless manner**, the guides and ideas by which people can live happily. These are **worldviews**.
- Through **critical thinking**, worldviews are **constantly revised**.
- The goal of philosophising is **the promotion of human well-being — the good life**.

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. Give two reasons why philosophy is inescapable for human beings.">
First, humans are constituted as curious beings. Plato said philosophy began in wonder, Aristotle that curiosity drives us to know, and Matthews that children philosophise naturally. Unlike animals, we are conscious of our experience and must make sense of our surroundings and our place in them. Second, philosophy determines the quality and essence of our lives: our actions and judgements rest on ideas and beliefs about the world (religious belief, the Golden Rule), which we absorb from family, friends, church, mosque and the beer parlour. Adults become "philosophers who do not philosophise" because the world becomes a habit and they take their ideas at face value.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. What does 'philosophy' mean, and why is the lover of wisdom compared to a lover in a relationship?">
Philosophy comes from the Greek philosophia — philos (love) + sophia (wisdom). It is a reflective, meditative human attempt to systematically and critically study human experience. The wisdom it seeks is a comprehensive set of beliefs acting as a general vision of the universe, one's place in it and worthwhile goals — understanding brought to bear on action. Like lovers who must keep working at a relationship, or it collapses once they think it is "figured out", the philosopher is in an ongoing quest to explore new ideas, re-examine old ones and confront new dimensions of experience.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. What is a worldview? State the questions it answers and why we need one.">
A worldview is a comprehensive set of ideas explaining the basic nature of the world, one's own nature, and the achievable goals one can pursue and how to act to reach them (Wolters 1983; Oladipo 2008). It answers seven questions: the nature of the world; why it is so and where it came from; where we are going; what future to choose; how to act (good, evil, meaning); how to construct a true picture of the world; and where to begin. We need it for self-insight and intellectual maturity, as a practical survival strategy, and for psychological confidence, satisfaction and happiness. Worldviews are dynamic, partial and need revision.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Explain philosophy as critical thinking and the three tests of a worldview.">
Because worldviews are partial, Socrates held that "an unexamined life is not worth living", and Staniland defined philosophy as "the criticism of the ideas we live by". Critical thinking examines beliefs for contradictions and asks why we act as we do. The three tests: factual adequacy (agreement with known facts), logical coherence (beliefs hang together without contradiction) and explanatory capacity (showing how things in the universe relate). It also involves conceptual analysis (precise meanings and relations of concepts) and the reconstruction of ideas (replacing rejected beliefs with better ones).
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Philosophy is inevitable**: (1) humans are **curious** (Plato: wonder; Aristotle: curiosity; Matthews: children philosophise naturally); (2) our **ideas and beliefs shape how we live**. Adults become "philosophers who do not philosophise".
- Everyday life raises philosophical problems: free will, political obligation, truth, punishment, reality vs appearance, freedom and good government.
- **Philosophia = love of wisdom** — an ongoing quest, like a relationship; philosophy is a reflective human attempt to systematically and critically study human experience.
- **Philosophy as worldview** (broad or popular): a comprehensive set of ideas about the world, oneself and one's goals; **seven questions**; needed for self-insight, survival and confidence; always revisable.
- **Philosophy as critical thinking** (narrow or technical): **Socrates** — "an unexamined life is not worth living"; **Staniland** — "the criticism of the ideas we live by"; tests: **factual adequacy, logical coherence, explanatory capacity**; plus **conceptual analysis** and **reconstruction of ideas**.
- The goal: **human well-being — the good life**.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Memorise the **attributions**: Plato — wonder; Aristotle — curiosity; Socrates — unexamined life; **Staniland** — "criticism of the ideas we live by", "ideas we live by"; Russell — questions that increase the interest of the world.
- Know the **three tests** of a worldview and the **two further dimensions** (conceptual analysis, reconstruction).
- Be ready to name the **philosophical problem** behind an everyday scenario — a common MCQ style.
- In essays on "Why study philosophy?", present it as a **human necessity**, not just a subject.
