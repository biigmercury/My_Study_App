## What Is a Concept?

The *Oxford Advanced Learner's Dictionary* defines a **concept** as **an idea, a notion or an understanding of something**.

- Concepts are **basically abstract** — they are ideas.
- But **not all concepts are about abstract things**: some have a **concrete or physical aspect**. The concept "car" is an abstract description that comes to mind when the word is mentioned, yet it refers to a physical object.

The chapter's author, **Dipo Irele**, identifies **four pairs** of concepts.

| Pair | First member | Second member |
|---|---|---|
| **Concrete / abstract** | **Concrete:** refer to an **entire object**, material or ideal — *house, car, witness, "Jaja of Opobo", earthquake, orphan* | **Abstract:** refer to **a feature taken in isolation** from the whole object — *whiteness, justice, honesty, beauty*. We meet white clothes and honest people, but **whiteness and honesty do not exist by themselves** and cannot be perceived directly. They can also express **relations** — *inequality, similarity, identity, resemblance* |
| **Relative / non-relative** | **Relative:** the existence of one object **presupposes the existence of another** — *children/parents, husband/wife, pupil/teacher, high/low* | **Non-relative:** exist and make sense **independently** — *table, man, village, car* |
| **Positive / negative** | **Positive:** affirm the **presence** of a quality or relation — *literate person, brilliance, backward pupil, exploiter*. They are positive **even if the quality is unpleasant**: the **logical** nature of a concept need not match our **value** judgement | **Negative:** indicate the **absence** of a quality — *irrational person, illiterate person, nonentity* |
| **Collective / non-collective** | **Collective:** a group of similar objects **as a single whole** — *team, regiment, flock, congregation, herd*. The content **cannot be attributed to each member**: it is ridiculous to say *"a tree is a forest"*, *"a player is a team"*, *"a ship is a fleet"* | **Non-collective:** the content applies to **each object in the class** — *river, plant, house* |

<SortingGame title="Which kind of concept?">

```sort
@buckets Abstract | Relative | Negative | Collective
Justice => Abstract :: A feature considered apart from any particular just act.
Whiteness => Abstract :: Exists only as a quality of white things.
Husband => Relative :: A husband presupposes a wife.
Teacher => Relative :: A teacher presupposes a pupil.
Illiterate => Negative :: It indicates the absence of literacy.
Nonentity => Negative :: It signifies the absence of significance.
Flock => Collective :: A group of sheep taken as one whole — one sheep is not a flock.

```

## Basic Concepts in Philosophy

These concepts are called **basic** because they are **central to the core branches of philosophy** (epistemology, metaphysics, ethics) **and** part of **everyday experience**.

### Concepts from epistemology

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Sense perception** | The **process by which we acquire information about the world through our five senses** — an **active, selective and interpretative** process of becoming conscious of the external world. It is central to epistemology and the **key concept of empiricism**: all knowledge is grounded in how we **see, hear, touch, smell and taste** |
| **Rationality** | The quality of being **based on or agreeable to reason** — **conformity of one's beliefs with one's reasons to believe, and of one's actions with one's reasons to act**. It is the **key concept of rationalism** |
| **Scepticism (doubt)** | The view that **we fail to know anything** — more generally, a **family of views** denying that our beliefs are knowledge, reasonable or justified. It may be **restricted** to beliefs from **memory**, **induction** or any reasoning, or to beliefs about the **external world**, **other minds** or **values** |

**Three strands of rationality:**

1. **Instrumental rationality** — adopting **the best actions to achieve one's goals**.
2. **Axiomatic** — being **logically consistent** within one's preferences and beliefs.
3. **Accuracy** — **accurate beliefs** that make **full use of available information**.

**A rational person's mental state** shows:

- **(i)** **coherent**, non-contradictory beliefs, compatible with experience;
- **(ii)** **purposeful** behaviour guided by **means–ends** analysis;
- **(iii)** **cost–benefit** (pain versus gain) decision-making;
- **(iv)** **optimisation** — maximising gains and minimising losses.

**The sceptic's "brain in a vat":** we cannot rule out that we are **brains in a vat**, artificially stimulated to have exactly the experiences we now have. If that were so, **our experience would not change at all**. So, says the sceptic, **we cannot know anything about the world around us**.

**Solipsism** — the view that **all that exists is the self and its states** — is a form of scepticism: there is no convincing argument for anything beyond the self.

### Concepts from ethics

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Morality** | From the Latin ***moralis*** — **"customs" or "manners"**. Some philosophers distinguish morality from ethics; others use the terms **interchangeably** (*Cambridge Dictionary of Philosophy*). Either way, **morality is discussed within ethics**. "Moral" or "ethical" means **good or right**; "immoral" or "unethical" means **bad or wrong**. Morality concerns **how humans treat other beings**, human and non-human, to promote **mutual welfare, growth, creativity and meaning** |
| **Utility** | Something (a belief, action or decision) is **useful because it brings the right outcome for the greater number** — the basis of **utilitarianism**, a **consequentialist** theory. Actions are right if they **promote the greatest well-being**, judged **neutrally**, by their effects on everyone affected. Utility has been identified with **pleasure, happiness, knowledge, self-realisation, beauty**. Its roots lie in **Epicurus, Plato, Aristotle, the Stoics and Hume**; its **major proponents are Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill** |
| **Duty** | **"That which one must do, or that which can be required of one"** — what one **owes others, and even oneself**. **Universal duties** are owed to others as human beings; **special duties** arise from **special relationships**. Duty is the **primary concept of deontological ethics**, and its modern development is credited to **Immanuel Kant**: **perfect duties** must be performed **whatever the circumstances**; **imperfect duties** may give way to more stringent ones |
| **Virtue** | **A trait of character to be admired** because it makes its possessor **better** — morally, intellectually or in the conduct of affairs. What makes a person good is not just acting rightly but **having the right character** — **virtuous rather than vicious**. Virtue is the **primary concept of virtue ethics** (and of virtue epistemology). In **Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics***, virtues are **acquired by repeatedly performing the act**: we become generous by **repeatedly acting generously** |

<Analogy>
**Duty versus virtue.** A deontologist asks: *"What must I do? What rule applies?"* — like checking the exam regulations. A virtue ethicist asks: *"What kind of person am I becoming?"* — like an athlete who trains daily until good form is second nature. Aristotle says honesty is built the same way: tell the truth again and again until it becomes your character.
</Analogy>

### Concepts from metaphysics

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Substance** | **"That which stands beneath"** — what **exists in itself**, not predicated of or existing in anything else. Everything else is predicated **of** it or exists **in** it. A properly **basic entity existing *per se*** (though depending on an external cause for its existence). It **cannot stop being the substance it is without losing its identity** |
| **Accident** | What **accrues to a substance and modifies it**. Accidents **cannot exist in themselves**, only **in some substance**, and they **can come and go without the thing losing its identity**. E.g. **brown** is always "x is brown" — never found by itself. Properties are always **exemplified** by some substance; substance itself is **un-exemplifiable** |
| **Matter and materialism** | **Matter** is that which **occupies space**, with **size, shape, mass, movability and solidity**. For the ancient Greeks it accounted for things being **visible**. **Materialism:** **reality is matter and matter is reality**. Anything real, even **mind or consciousness**, must be **reducible to and explainable in material terms**. Tables, trees and stones are **more real** than non-material things |
| **Ideas and idealism** | "Idea" comes from the Greek ***idein*** — **"to see"**. **Idealism:** reality is **fundamentally mental, mentally constructed, or immaterial**. When reduced to its lowest terms, reality is **ideas**, so it is **mind-dependent**. The idealist **does not deny tables and trees**, only that their reality is **independent of mind**. **George Berkeley:** **"To be is to be perceived."** Other idealists include **Plato and Hegel** |

The history of Western philosophy has largely been a **struggle between materialism and idealism** over *"What is reality?"*

<SortingGame title="Substance or accident?">

```sort
@buckets Substance | Accident
A mango => Substance :: It exists in itself; qualities are predicated of it.
The mango's yellowness => Accident :: Yellowness exists only in the mango and can change as it ripens.
Ade, the student => Substance :: Ade remains Ade whatever his qualities.
Ade's height of 1.8 m => Accident :: A property that could change without Ade ceasing to be Ade.
A wooden table => Substance :: A basic entity that bears properties.
A scratch on the table => Accident :: It modifies the table without destroying its identity.
```

</SortingGame>

## Concepts from Political Philosophy

### The state and government

**Woodrow Wilson:** the state is **"a people organised for law within a definite territory."** Its characteristics are:

- a **definite territory** where law and order are maintained by a **supreme authority**;
- a **government** organised to achieve the state's purposes;
- a **system of laws binding** on all individuals and associations;
- a **monopoly of coercive power**;
- **sovereignty** — freedom to conduct its own affairs.

**The state is larger than the government**, but it acts **through** government and its three arms — **executive, legislature and judiciary**. The state:

- **enhances citizens' rights and liberties**;
- **protects the territory** from external and internal aggression;
- provides the atmosphere for citizens to **actualise their potential and attain a happy life**.

**Olusegun Oladipo:** government's **primary function** is maintaining **peace, order and harmony** through effective coordination of public affairs. This is **not an end in itself** but a means to **enhance individuals' capacity to survive and flourish**.

### Democracy

The word comes from the Greek ***demos*** (**people**) + ***kratia*** (**rule**) — **rule of the people**. **Abraham Lincoln** (Gettysburg Address): **"government of the people, by the people and for the people."** It is now the **most popular and most legitimate** form of government; non-democracies are **ostracised** by the comity of nations.

**Features of democracy:**

1. A **common will or interest** guiding government.
2. Citizens treated as **rational, active beings** — with **freedom** in social and political life.
3. The **consent of the ruled** as the basis of authority.
4. **Decision by majority vote**.
5. The **rule of law** — impartial application of law.
6. **Equal and political rights**.
7. **Tolerance of dissent**.
8. **Respect for minority and opposing views**.

**History:** democracy began in **ancient Greek city-states such as Athens** — but only **free citizens** took part. **Women, children, foreigners and slaves** were excluded. Contributors to its evolution include:

- **Aristotle** — "polity", or constitutional government;
- **John Locke**;
- **Rousseau** — the **"general will"**;
- **Edmund Burke** and **Montesquieu**.

Today democratic governments are formed through **political parties**.

| Direct democracy | Indirect (representative) democracy |
|---|---|
| **All free citizens take part directly** in day-to-day decisions — the **archetype**, also called **popular democracy**, worked out in **Athens** (Thucydides, Pericles) | Citizens **elect representatives** to act for them — necessary because of **population growth** and the **complexity of industrial societies** |

### Power and authority

**Power** is **the ability to get your own way even when others oppose you** — the "force or ability" to **make things happen**. It includes the ability to **punish or reward**, and to **manipulate** (in contrast to **influence**, which uses **rational persuasion**). Authority is described in terms of **legitimacy and rightness**; power in terms of the **causal factors** by which some determine the actions of others.

| Authority | Coercion |
|---|---|
| Power citizens **accept because they believe it is legitimate and justified** | Power citizens accept **only because of the threat or use of force** — the sense often associated with **military rulers** |

The two often **overlap**. Some say authority and power are the same; others say authority's mark is that it is **voluntarily accepted**.

| De jure authority | De facto authority |
|---|---|
| Authority **by right under rules or laws** — its extent depends on **what the rules permit**, not on the ability to secure obedience | The **actual ability** to induce people to do or believe what one says, because **they are convinced they should obey** |

**Uduigwomen:** **de facto authority often arises from de jure authority**. If people respect the principles that legitimise a ruler, the ruler will enjoy de facto authority.

<SortingGame title="Authority, coercion, de jure or de facto?">

```sort
@buckets Authority | Coercion | De jure | De facto
Citizens pay tax because they accept the tax law as legitimate => Authority :: Power accepted as justified.
Villagers obey armed bandits out of fear for their lives => Coercion :: Compliance only through threat of force.
A newly sworn-in governor can do only what the constitution permits => De jure :: Authority by right within the rules.
A respected elder with no official post is obeyed by the whole community => De facto :: People are convinced they should follow him.
A military junta keeps order with soldiers on every street => Coercion :: Power without accepted legitimacy.
```

</SortingGame>

### Rights and duties

Under **social contract theory**, people **surrender their sovereignty** to a central body that legislates and judges for them. In return they are guaranteed **rights**, and they owe **duties** to the state for its healthy survival.

**Rights** are **entitlements** to perform or not perform certain actions, or to be or not be in certain states — or entitlements that **others** act or refrain from acting. Rights **determine which actions are permissible**, **which institutions are just**, and the form of government, law and morality. There are three sources of rights:

| Kind of right | Source |
|---|---|
| **Natural rights** | The **nature of the holder** — human rights, animal rights, even "plant rights" |
| **Moral rights** | **Moral reasons** — these seem **innate to humans alone** |
| **Legal rights** | **The law** that guides people, institutions and society |

### Sovereignty

From the Latin ***superanus*** — **"supremacy"**. **Appadorai:** sovereignty is **the power of the state to make law, with all the coercive power it cares to employ**. The state **cannot be legally bound except by its own will**, and is **subject to no higher human authority**.

| Thinker | View of sovereignty |
|---|---|
| **Jean Bodin** (French) | **Introduced** the concept into political theory. In every independent community there must be a **supreme source of law**, which is **above the law but not above duty and moral responsibility** |
| **Hugo Grotius** | Stressed **external sovereignty** — **freedom from external control** |
| **Thomas Hobbes** | Sovereignty is **absolute**, located in **the ruler** — the ***Leviathan*** created by the social contract |
| **John Locke** | **Supreme power lies with the people** |
| **Jean-Jacques Rousseau** | Sovereignty belongs to **the people** and is exercised in an **assembly of the whole people** through the **general will**; government is only its **executive agent**. Popular sovereignty here is **as unlimited as Hobbes's** |
| **Jeremy Bentham** | Sovereignty is **unlimited by law but not morally unlimited** — it is limited by the **possibility of justified resistance**. The sovereign must justify power by **useful legislation** promoting **the greatest happiness of the greatest number** |
| **John Austin** (the textbook writes "J. L. Austin") | In every state there must be a sovereign whose power is **unlimited and indivisible** and **clearly located**, and **the sovereign's commands are laws** — commands backed by **penalties** |

**Where sovereignty is located depends on the system:**

- **Britain** — in **the Crown in Parliament** (the textbook, written before 2022, says "the Queen in parliament");
- **the USA** — **less determinate**, divided between central and state governments by the constitution;
- **many democracies** — **the people (the electorate)**, who transfer their sovereignty to government.

Wherever it is located, that body is **supreme and legitimate**.

<SortingGame title="Who said it about sovereignty?">

```sort
@buckets Bodin | Grotius | Hobbes | Locke | Rousseau | Bentham | Austin
"The sovereign is above the law, but not above duty and moral responsibility." => Bodin :: Bodin introduced sovereignty into political theory.
"A state is sovereign when it is free from external control." => Grotius :: The emphasis on external sovereignty.
"The ruler — the Leviathan — holds absolute power and must not be resisted." => Hobbes :: Absolute sovereignty located in the ruler.
"Supreme power in the state lies with the people." => Locke :: Popular supremacy.
"Sovereignty is the general will of the whole people assembled; government merely executes it." => Rousseau :: The general will.
"The sovereign is unlimited by law but limited morally by the people's right to resist; he must promote the greatest happiness." => Bentham :: A moral limit on sovereignty.
"Law is the command of a determinate sovereign, backed by a penalty." => Austin :: The command theory of law.
```

</SortingGame>

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. Define 'concept' and explain the four pairs of concepts with examples.">
A concept is an idea, a notion or an understanding of something — basically abstract, though some refer to concrete objects. Concrete concepts refer to whole objects (house, Jaja of Opobo); abstract concepts refer to features or relations in isolation (whiteness, justice, similarity). Relative concepts presuppose another object (husband and wife); non-relative concepts stand alone (table). Positive concepts affirm a quality (literate, exploiter), even an unpleasant one; negative concepts indicate its absence (illiterate, nonentity). Collective concepts treat a group as one whole (team, flock) — not attributable to members; non-collective concepts apply to each member (river, house).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Explain rationality and scepticism as basic concepts.">
Rationality is being based on or agreeable to reason — conformity of beliefs with reasons to believe and of actions with reasons to act; it is the key concept of rationalism. Its strands are instrumental (the best means to goals), axiomatic (logical consistency) and accuracy (full use of information). A rational mind is coherent, purposeful, weighs costs and benefits, and optimises. Scepticism is the family of views that we fail to know — restricted by source (memory, induction) or by subject (the external world, other minds, values). The brain-in-a-vat argument says we cannot rule out being stimulated brains, so we cannot know the world. Solipsism holds that only the self exists.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Distinguish duty and virtue, and utility, as ethical concepts.">
Duty is what one must do or can be required to do — universal duties are owed to all, special duties arise from relationships. It is the primary concept of deontology; Kant distinguished perfect duties (always binding) from imperfect duties (which may yield). Virtue is an admirable character trait that makes its possessor better — the primary concept of virtue ethics; for Aristotle it is acquired by repeated acts. Utility says an act is right if it produces the best outcome for the greatest number — the basis of consequentialist utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Explain substance and accident, and contrast materialism with idealism.">
Substance is "that which stands beneath" — it exists in itself, bears properties, and cannot change what it is without losing its identity. Accidents (e.g. brown) exist only in substances and can come and go without the thing losing its identity. Materialism holds that reality is matter (that which occupies space, with size, shape, mass and solidity), so even mind must be explained materially. Idealism (from idein, "to see") holds that reality is fundamentally mental and mind-dependent — Berkeley's "to be is to be perceived", also Plato and Hegel.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="5. Discuss power and authority, including de jure and de facto authority.">
Power is the ability to get one's way even against opposition — to make things happen, reward, punish or manipulate. Authority is power accepted as legitimate; coercion is power accepted only through the threat or use of force (e.g. military rule). De jure authority is authority by right under rules, limited to what the rules permit; de facto authority is the actual ability to secure obedience because people are convinced they should obey. De facto authority often arises from de jure authority when people respect the legitimating principles (Uduigwomen).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="6. Compare the views of Bodin, Hobbes, Rousseau and Bentham on sovereignty.">
Bodin introduced sovereignty as the supreme source of law — above the law but not above duty and moral responsibility. Hobbes made it absolute and located it in the ruler (the Leviathan) created by the social contract. Rousseau placed it in the people's general will, exercised in an assembly of all, with government as mere executive — and as unlimited as Hobbes's. Bentham held it unlimited by law but morally limited by the possibility of justified resistance, so the sovereign must legislate usefully for the greatest happiness of the greatest number. (Also: Grotius — external sovereignty; Locke — the people; Austin — the commands of a determinate sovereign are law.)
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Concept** = an idea or notion; four pairs: **concrete/abstract**, **relative/non-relative**, **positive/negative**, **collective/non-collective**.
- **Epistemic concepts**: **sense perception** (empiricism), **rationality** (rationalism; instrumental, axiomatic, accuracy), **scepticism** (brain in a vat; solipsism).
- **Ethical concepts**: **morality** (*moralis* = customs), **utility** (Bentham, Mill), **duty** (Kant: perfect and imperfect), **virtue** (Aristotle: acquired by repetition).
- **Metaphysical concepts**: **substance** (exists in itself) vs **accident** (exists in a substance); **materialism** vs **idealism** (*idein*; Berkeley: to be is to be perceived).
- **Political concepts**: **state** (Wilson; larger than government); **democracy** (demos + kratia; Lincoln; eight features; direct vs indirect); **power** (authority vs coercion; **de jure vs de facto**); **rights** (natural, moral, legal) and duties; **sovereignty** (*superanus*): Bodin, Grotius, **Hobbes (absolute)**, Locke, **Rousseau (general will)**, **Bentham (greatest happiness, right of resistance)**, Austin (command).
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- **Hobbes vs Rousseau vs Bentham on sovereignty** is a favourite comparison — know one phrase for each: *Leviathan / general will / greatest happiness and resistance*.
- Know the **four pairs of concepts** with two examples each.
- Remember the **etymologies**: *moralis* (customs), *idein* (to see), *superanus* (supremacy), *demos + kratia*.
- Watch **de jure vs de facto** — a practical scenario question is likely.
