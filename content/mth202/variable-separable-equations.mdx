# Variable Separable Equations

The method of separation of variables is the first and most intuitive technique for solving first-order ODEs. The idea is beautifully simple: move everything involving $y$ to one side and everything involving $x$ to the other, then integrate both sides independently.

## The Setup

A first-order ODE of the form

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = F(x, y)$$

is called **variable separable** when $F(x, y)$ factors into a product of a function of $x$ alone and a function of $y$ alone:

$$F(x, y) = f(x) \cdot g(y)$$

so the equation becomes:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = f(x)\, g(y) $$

**Real-world analogy:** Think of $x$ and $y$ as two strangers who can be "separated" — one moves to the left side of an equation, the other to the right. Once separated, each can be integrated independently on their own side.

---

## Method

**Step 1.** Rewrite the equation so all $y$-terms (including $dy$) are on the left, and all $x$-terms (including $dx$) are on the right:

$$\frac{dy}{g(y)} = f(x)\, dx $$

**Step 2.** Integrate both sides:

$$\int \frac{dy}{g(y)} = \int f(x)\, dx + A$$

where $A$ is the single arbitrary constant of integration.

**Step 3.** If possible, solve for $y$ explicitly.

---

## Example 3.1 — Basic Separation

Solve:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} \cdot y = (x + 1)^2$$

**Step 1.** Separate variables — move $y$ to the left, $dx$ to the right:

$$y\, dy = (x+1)^2\, dx$$

**Step 2.** Integrate both sides:

$$\int y\, dy = \int (x+1)^2\, dx$$

$$\frac{y^2}{2} = \frac{(x+1)^3}{3} + A$$

where $A$ is an arbitrary constant.

**General solution:**

$$y^2 = \frac{2(x+1)^3}{3} + C \qquad (C = 2A)$$

---

## Example 3.2 — Implicit Solution

Solve:

$$x(y^2 - 1)\, dx - y(x^2 - 1)\, dy = 0$$

**Step 1.** Rearrange to separate:

$$\frac{x\, dx}{x^2 - 1} = \frac{y\, dy}{y^2 - 1}$$

**Step 2.** Note that $\frac{d}{dx}(x^2 - 1) = 2x$, so $x\, dx = \frac{1}{2} d(x^2 - 1)$. Integrating:

$$\frac{1}{2}\ln|x^2 - 1| = \frac{1}{2}\ln|y^2 - 1| + A$$

$$\ln|x^2 - 1| - \ln|y^2 - 1| = \ln B \qquad (A = \ln B/2 \Rightarrow 2A = \ln B)$$

$$\ln\left|\frac{x^2 - 1}{y^2 - 1}\right| = \ln B$$

**General solution:**

$$x^2 - 1 = B(y^2 - 1)$$

where $B$ is an arbitrary constant.

**Note:** Replacing one constant with another ($A \to \ln B$) is a standard trick to simplify the form of the general solution. You will use it often.

---

## Example 3.3 — The Logistic Equation

Solve the logistic equation:

$$\frac{dP}{dt} = P(\beta - \delta P)$$

with initial condition $P(0) = P_0$.

**Step 1.** Separate variables:

$$\frac{dP}{P(\beta - \delta P)} = dt$$

**Step 2.** Use partial fractions. Decompose $\frac{1}{P(\beta - \delta P)}$:

$$\frac{1}{P(\beta - \delta P)} = \frac{1}{\beta P} + \frac{\delta}{\beta(\beta - \delta P)}$$

**Step 3.** Integrate:

$$\frac{1}{\beta}\ln P - \frac{1}{\beta}\ln(\beta - \delta P) = t + A$$

$$\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\left(\frac{P}{\beta - \delta P}\right) = t + A$$

**Step 4.** Exponentiate both sides and let $e^{\beta A} = A$ (renaming the constant):

$$\frac{P}{\beta - \delta P} = Ae^{\beta t}$$

**Step 5.** Apply the initial condition $P(0) = P_0$:

$$A = \frac{P_0}{\beta - \delta P_0}$$

**Step 6.** Cross-multiply and solve for $P(t)$:

$$P(t) = \frac{\beta/\delta}{1 + \left(\frac{\beta}{\delta P_0} - 1\right)e^{-\beta t}}$$

**Behaviour:** As $t \to \infty$, the denominator $\to 1$, so $P \to \beta/\delta$ (the carrying capacity).

---

## Recognising Separable Equations

Not every first-order ODE is separable. The key question is: *can $F(x,y)$ be written as a product $f(x)g(y)$?*

| Equation | Separable? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| $\frac{dy}{dx} = \cot x \cot y$ | Yes | $f(x) = \cot x$, $g(y) = \cot y$ |
| $(x^2+1)dy + (xy - xy^3)dx = 0$ | No | Cannot factor into $f(x)g(y)$ |
| $xy\, dy + \sqrt{1-y^2}\, dx = 0$ | Yes | Rewrite as $\frac{y\, dy}{\sqrt{1-y^2}} = -\frac{dx}{x}$ |
| $\frac{dy}{dx} = x + y$ | No | The $x + y$ cannot be factored |

---

## Key Takeaways

- Separation of variables works when $F(x, y) = f(x) \cdot g(y)$ — the function factors.
- After separation, each side is integrated independently; the result is one arbitrary constant.
- When integrating, you may need partial fractions (logistic equation) or substitution.
- The general solution may be implicit (e.g., $x^2 - 1 = B(y^2-1)$); this is acceptable.
- You can rename arbitrary constants at any step to simplify the expression — just be consistent.
- For an IVP, substitute the initial condition after finding the general solution.

---

## Exam Tips

- Always separate variables completely before integrating — the $dx$ and $dy$ must be on opposite sides.
- When you see $\int \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\, dx$, the result is $\ln|g(x)| + C$.
- In the logistic equation, memorise that partial fractions give $\frac{1}{P(\beta - \delta P)} = \frac{1}{\beta P} + \frac{\delta}{\beta(\beta - \delta P)}$.
- If the ODE has the form $\frac{dN}{dt} = kN$, the solution is always $N = N_0 e^{kt}$ — you can write this directly without full working, but state that it is the separable solution.
- Check for the *trivial solution* $y = 0$ (or $g(y) = 0$): when you divide by $g(y)$, you lose this solution. It may or may not be useful depending on the problem.
