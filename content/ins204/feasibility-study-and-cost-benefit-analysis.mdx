## What Is a Feasibility Study?

> A **feasibility study** is a **formal, structured investigation carried out early in the SDLC to determine whether a proposed system is worth pursuing** before significant resources are committed to detailed design and construction. *(Hoffer, George & Valacich, 2020)*

It answers the fundamental project-selection questions:

| Question | Feasibility type |
|---|---|
| **Can we build it?** | Technical |
| **Can we afford it?** | Economic |
| **Will people use it?** | Operational |
| **Are we legally permitted to build it?** | Legal |
| **Can we build it in time?** | Schedule |

### Objectives of a Feasibility Study

1. Evaluate **alternative solutions** objectively.
2. **Estimate the costs and benefits** of each alternative.
3. **Identify major risks** before they are incurred.
4. Give decision-makers enough evidence to **approve, modify, defer or reject** the project.

Feasibility studies matter because they act as a **formal checkpoint** that stops organisations committing scarce financial, human and technical resources to projects unlikely to succeed.

<Analogy>
Before building a hostel on campus, the university asks: Is the soil suitable and do we have engineers? (**technical**) Will rents cover the construction loan? (**economic**) Will students actually want to live there? (**operational**) Do we have the building permit and land title? (**legal**) Can it be ready before the new session? (**schedule**) A "no" to any one question can sink the project, however good the others look.
</Analogy>

## The Feasibility Study Process

Most studies follow a similar sequence of steps; the depth varies with project size.

<FlowDiagram title="Seven steps of a feasibility study">
<FlowStep title="Define the problem">
Define the **problem and the objectives** of the proposed system.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Identify alternatives">
Identify and describe **alternative solutions** — always including the option of **doing nothing** (keeping the current system).
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Assess TELOS">
Assess each alternative against the **five feasibility dimensions**: technical, economic, operational, legal and schedule.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Cost-benefit analysis">
**Estimate costs and benefits** for each alternative (payback, ROI, NPV).
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Assess risk">
Identify and assess the **risks** of each alternative.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Write the report">
Document findings in a **feasibility report** with a clear **recommendation**.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Go / no-go decision">
**Present** findings to decision-makers (a steering committee or project sponsor) for a **go/no-go decision**.
</FlowStep>
</FlowDiagram>

### Deliverables of a Feasibility Study

- A written **feasibility report** summarising findings for each dimension
- A **cost-benefit analysis** with supporting calculations (e.g., payback period)
- A **risk register** listing major risks and proposed mitigation strategies
- A documented **recommendation**: **proceed**, **proceed with modification**, **defer**, or **abandon**

## The Five Types of Feasibility (TELOS)

Remember them with **TELOS**: **T**echnical, **E**conomic, **L**egal, **O**perational, **S**chedule.

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Technical">

**Technical feasibility** examines whether the system **can actually be built** with currently available (or reasonably obtainable) **hardware, software, network infrastructure and technical skills**, within acceptable technical risk.

**Purpose:** determine whether the organisation has, or can acquire, the technology and expertise to deliver the solution, and surface technical risks such as:
- **unreliable power supply**,
- **bandwidth limitations**,
- **unavailable integration interfaces** with legacy systems.

*Question:* Can we build it, and can we support it afterwards?

</Tab>
<Tab label="Economic">

**Economic feasibility** — also called **cost-benefit analysis at the project-selection level** — determines whether the **projected financial benefits justify the expected costs** over a defined time horizon.

It gives decision-makers an **objective financial basis** — **payback period, ROI or NPV** — for funding the project relative to other uses of the same money. In short: **do lifetime benefits exceed lifetime costs?**

</Tab>
<Tab label="Operational">

**Operational feasibility** assesses whether the system will **work within the organisation's day-to-day environment** — whether staff and users are **willing and able to adopt it**, and whether it fits existing **workflows, culture and management support**.

Even a technically excellent system **fails if people resist it or cannot use it**. Typical questions: Will users like it? Will they use it? Will it change their work environment?

</Tab>
<Tab label="Legal">

**Legal feasibility** examines whether the system **complies with laws, regulations, contracts and industry standards** — including **data protection**, **intellectual property**, labour law and sector-specific regulation.

It keeps the organisation from **legal liability, regulatory sanction or contractual breach**, and identifies compliance requirements that must be **designed in from the start** rather than retrofitted. Examples: **software licensing**, IP ownership, obligations to third-party vendors. (In Nigeria, handling personal data brings in the **Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023**.)

</Tab>
<Tab label="Schedule">

**Schedule feasibility** evaluates whether the system can **realistically be developed and deployed within the time frame** the business demands, given the resources, scope and dependencies.

It checks whether deadlines are realistic, identifies scheduling risks and **critical dependencies** early, and lets decision-makers **adjust scope, resources or the deadline** before the project starts. (A registration portal delivered after registration week is worthless that session.)

</Tab>
</Tabs>

<ExamTip>
Lecture 2 introduced **three** feasibility aspects (technical, economic, operational). Lecture 3 expands this to **five** (adding **legal** and **schedule**). If a question says "types of feasibility", give all five — TELOS — and define each with an example.
</ExamTip>

## Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA)

**Cost-benefit analysis** is the **quantitative technique underlying economic feasibility**. The analyst identifies and, where possible, **puts a naira value on** every cost and benefit of the proposed system, then compares them using a recognised financial metric. CBA is performed **throughout the SDLC** to check economic feasibility and to compare alternatives.

### Categories of Costs and Benefits

| Category | Definition | Example (course registration system) |
|---|---|---|
| **Tangible costs** | Can be precisely measured in money | Server hardware, software licences, developer salaries |
| **Intangible costs** | Real but hard to quantify | Staff morale disruption during transition; temporary productivity dip |
| **Direct costs** | Attributable specifically to the project | Payment to the software development contractor |
| **Indirect costs** | Shared overheads allocated to the project | A share of the ICT Directorate's electricity and facility costs |
| **Tangible benefits** | Can be precisely measured in money | Reduced overtime pay; reduced printing costs |
| **Intangible benefits** | Real but hard to quantify | Improved student satisfaction; enhanced institutional reputation |

### The Three Common CBA Methods

Your notes name the three most common methods: **payback analysis, return on investment (ROI) and present value analysis**. Each treats the figures differently, but the objective is the same — **reliable information for decision-making**.

**1. Payback period** — how long it takes for **cumulative net benefits to recover the initial investment**. Shorter is better.

$$
\text{Payback period} = \text{years before recovery} + \frac{\text{unrecovered cost at start of that year}}{\text{net benefit during that year}}
$$

When net benefit is the same every year, this simplifies to initial cost ÷ yearly net benefit.

**2. Return on investment (ROI)** — the **percentage return** over the system's life.

$$
\text{ROI} = \frac{\text{total benefits} - \text{total costs}}{\text{total costs}} \times 100\%
$$

**3. Present value / Net present value (NPV)** — money received in the future is worth **less** than money today (it could have earned interest). Each future amount is **discounted** to today's value:

$$
PV = \frac{FV}{(1+r)^{t}} \qquad \text{NPV} = -C_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{B_t - C_t}{(1+r)^{t}}
$$

where $r$ is the discount rate, $t$ the year, $C_0$ the initial cost, and $B_t - C_t$ the net benefit in year $t$. **If NPV is greater than zero, the project is economically feasible.**

## Worked Example: Online Course Registration System

A university plans an online registration system.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Development cost (year 0) | ₦12,000,000 |
| Yearly benefits (reduced overtime, printing and manual processing) | ₦6,000,000 |
| Yearly running cost (hosting, support, maintenance) | ₦1,500,000 |
| Evaluation period | 5 years |
| Discount rate | 10% |

**Yearly net benefit** = ₦6,000,000 − ₦1,500,000 = **₦4,500,000**

**Step 1 — Payback period.**

| Year | Net benefit | Cumulative position |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | −₦12,000,000 | −₦12,000,000 |
| 1 | ₦4,500,000 | −₦7,500,000 |
| 2 | ₦4,500,000 | −₦3,000,000 |
| 3 | ₦4,500,000 | +₦1,500,000 |

The cost is recovered during year 3. Payback = 2 + (3,000,000 ÷ 4,500,000) = **2.67 years (about 2 years 8 months).**

**Step 2 — ROI over 5 years.**
- Total benefits = 5 × ₦6,000,000 = ₦30,000,000
- Total costs = ₦12,000,000 + 5 × ₦1,500,000 = ₦19,500,000
- ROI = (30,000,000 − 19,500,000) ÷ 19,500,000 × 100 = **53.8%**

**Step 3 — NPV at 10%.**

| Year | Net benefit | Discount factor $1/(1.1)^t$ | Present value |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ₦4,500,000 | 0.9091 | ₦4,090,909 |
| 2 | ₦4,500,000 | 0.8264 | ₦3,719,008 |
| 3 | ₦4,500,000 | 0.7513 | ₦3,380,917 |
| 4 | ₦4,500,000 | 0.6830 | ₦3,073,561 |
| 5 | ₦4,500,000 | 0.6209 | ₦2,794,146 |
| | | **Total PV** | **₦17,058,540** |

NPV = ₦17,058,540 − ₦12,000,000 = **₦5,058,540**. Since NPV > 0, the project is **economically feasible**.

## Risk Assessment

A feasibility study is, at its core, **a structured exercise in risk reduction**: by examining all five dimensions before committing resources, the organisation turns **unknown risks into known, manageable ones**.

| Risk category | Description | Mitigated primarily by |
|---|---|---|
| **Technical** | Chosen technology cannot deliver required functionality, performance or integration | Technical feasibility assessment |
| **Organisational** | Structure, politics or culture obstruct the project | Operational feasibility assessment |
| **Human** | Insufficient skills, staff turnover or user resistance | Operational feasibility; training planning |
| **Financial** | Costs exceed budget or benefits fail to materialise | Economic feasibility (CBA) |
| **Legal / compliance** | Regulatory breach, litigation or contractual default | Legal feasibility assessment |
| **Schedule** | Project misses critical deadlines | Schedule feasibility assessment |

The **risk register** (a deliverable of the study) records each risk, its likelihood and impact, and the mitigation planned.

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="List the five types of feasibility and the question each answers.">
**Technical** — can we build it? **Economic** — can we afford it (do benefits exceed costs)? **Operational** — will people use it? **Legal** — are we permitted to build/operate it? **Schedule** — can we build it in time?
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="A system costs ₦8,000,000 to develop and yields net benefits of ₦2,000,000, ₦3,000,000, ₦3,000,000 and ₦4,000,000 in years 1–4. Find the payback period.">
Cumulative: year 1 → −₦6,000,000; year 2 → −₦3,000,000; year 3 → ₦0. The cost is exactly recovered at the end of year 3, so **payback = 3 years**.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="Find the present value of ₦5,000,000 received in 2 years at a 10% discount rate.">
PV = 5,000,000 ÷ (1.1)² = 5,000,000 ÷ 1.21 = **₦4,132,231** (to the nearest naira).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="Distinguish tangible from intangible benefits with one example each for a hospital records system.">
**Tangible benefits** can be measured precisely in money — e.g., reduced cost of paper folders and filing staff. **Intangible benefits** are real but hard to quantify — e.g., improved patient satisfaction or fewer medical errors from misplaced files.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="State the four possible recommendations of a feasibility report.">
**Proceed**, **proceed with modification**, **defer**, or **abandon**.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
A feasibility study is a formal early checkpoint that decides whether a system **should** be built. It follows seven steps — define problem, identify alternatives (including doing nothing), assess **TELOS**, cost-benefit analysis, risk assessment, report, go/no-go decision — and produces a report, CBA, risk register and recommendation. Economic feasibility uses **payback period**, **ROI** and **NPV**; a project with **NPV > 0** is economically feasible. Every risk category (technical, organisational, human, financial, legal, schedule) is reduced by the matching feasibility assessment.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

**What lecturers test:**
- Definition, objectives and steps of a feasibility study
- The five types of feasibility (TELOS) with examples
- Tangible vs intangible and direct vs indirect costs/benefits
- **Calculations**: payback period, ROI and present value/NPV
- Risk categories and how feasibility mitigates them

**Common mistakes:**
- Forgetting the **"do nothing"** alternative
- Computing ROI with net benefits in the numerator **and** subtracting costs again — use (total benefits − total costs) ÷ total costs
- Discounting year 0 — the initial cost is already in today's money

**Likely question:**
> "Explain five types of feasibility. A proposed payroll system costs ₦10m to develop and ₦1m a year to run, and saves ₦4m a year. Calculate its payback period and 4-year ROI, and advise management." (20 marks)
