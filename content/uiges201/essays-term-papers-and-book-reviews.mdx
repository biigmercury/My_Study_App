## What Is Effective Writing?

More than 80% of your university examinations may be **written**, and **no student, whatever the course, can avoid written assignments**. Writing can even become a livelihood in journalism, publishing, teaching and research.

**Effective writing clearly and purposefully presents the writer's message.** Before you write, answer three questions:
- **What question am I answering?** Is my purpose to inform, interpret, argue a point or make a submission?
- **Who is my reader?** Classmates, a lecturer, the Head of Department, the Dean? Consider their background, values and religion.
- **In what capacity are they reading?** For an examination, as an official transaction, or informally?

A term paper laden with slang is out of place. **Writing proceeds from thought**, and every piece should have **three qualities**:

| Quality | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Economy** | "A sentence should contain **no unnecessary words**, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts." — **Strunk and White** |
| **Simplicity** | Wordiness is not intelligence; big polysyllabic words may even show **ignorance**. Keep sentences simple — but **vary** them (simple, compound, complex) to avoid monotony. |
| **Clarity** | Say what you mean **with precision and certainty**. Use a thesaurus to avoid repetition. "Readers are **not mind readers**." Keep each paragraph built around **one topic sentence** (unity and coherence). |

The writing process itself — **pre-writing, writing, post-writing** — is covered in the lesson *Outlining, Paragraph and Paragraph Development*.

## The Types of Essay

The coursebook names **four types**: **expository, narrative, argumentative and descriptive**. It then treats the **persuasive** essay as a step beyond the argumentative.

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Expository">

**Exposition explains.** An expository essay answers five basic questions, each with its own method:

| Question | Method |
|---|---|
| **How do you do it?** | **Process analysis** — how a procedure works or a system operates (*how to build a local oven*) |
| **How is it similar to or different from something else?** | **Comparison and contrast** |
| **What sub-divisions does it contain?** | **Classification** |
| **What are its characteristics?** | **Definition** |
| **Why did it happen?** | **Causal analysis** |

- **Definition** may be the dictionary meaning, a **delimited (operationalised)** meaning that fixes the sense you will use, or an **extended definition** with examples, history or variations.
- **Analysis** is either **process analysis** or **item analysis**. Item analysis investigates the **reasons or causes** of a situation, and may discuss its **effects**.
- **Classification** needs **broad, clear categories**. It must be **exhaustive**, with each unit in its proper category.
- **Comparison and contrast** needs subjects with enough in common, or enough differences, to give **sufficient parallel points**. Think of your primary-school teacher's "living things do this, while non-living things cannot".

**How to write it.**
1. **Delimit the topic** into a thesis, and state your point in the introduction.
2. **Gather information.**
3. **Capture the reader's interest from the first sentence** — an expository essay must not be dull.
4. **Cross-check facts** and arrange them logically, using examples.
5. Be **objective**, and move from the **general to the specific**.

It works best when the information is **new, relevant and factual**: the reader should say, "**I see, and I now understand better.**"

*Sample topics:* Democracy; University Examinations; Cancer; Investments; Success; the Freedom of Information Act; Plane Crashes in Nigeria; the June 12 Saga; Living Off-campus.

</Tab>
<Tab label="Narrative">

**Narration tells a story** or recounts an event or experience: a biography, a factual story, a diary entry, a fairy tale. It is about **events and circumstances as they affect people in a place and at a time**.

- **The opening sentence** focuses on the **truth or lesson** the story will establish. Topic sentences do not drive each paragraph as in other essays.
- It thrives on **imagination** and needs **tension**, "so that the reader has something to look forward to; otherwise there is no story".
- **Order:** usually **chronological** (first this, then that), with **flashback** where a past event explains the present.
- **Tools:**
  - a **plot** — a "meaningful and dramatic sequence of actions or events";
  - **characters** made real and distinct;
  - a **conflict** that needs resolution;
  - a **setting** in **space and time** — name the place and the period.

  The reader should keep asking, **"So what then happened?"**
- **Dialogue** *shows* character instead of *telling* it. Don't say "Romeo is a wicked man"; let his words reveal it.
- **Pace** — the speed of narration — is fast in some places and slow in others.
- **Point of view:**
  - **first person** ("I", "we") is **limited** to what one observer sees and hears;
  - **third person** ("he", "she", "they") can be **omniscient**, knowing what every character thinks and does.

</Tab>
<Tab label="Descriptive">

**Description paints pictures with words** — "a written portrait" of a person, a place, an event or a phenomenon. It answers **"What does it look like?"**

- **Spatial arrangement:** move from one point to another so the reader can visualise the scene — top to bottom, inside to outside, far to near, right to left, specific to general, or the reverse.
- **Pre-writing:** visit the place, or examine the object.
- **Sensory impressions:** list dimensions, colours, shape, size, feel, smell, touch and qualities. **Invoke all the senses.**
- **Technical description** of visible, verifiable objects must be **objective, factual and non-personal**.
- Use **specific, exact words**, and **observe**: "a good writer must not be guilty of **looking and not seeing**".
- **Use analogies.** It is not enough to say something is big: **how** big? Enormous? Mammoth? Could it fill the space of a three-storey building? Which **metaphor or simile** will show it?

</Tab>
<Tab label="Argumentative">

The argumentative essay is an attempt to **convince the reader about a belief or position** held by the writer, using **facts and proof based on logical reasoning**. It "turns a question into a problem" and, through well-informed submissions, **sways the reader's opinion**.

- Begin with **tactful, courteous language**, finding **points on which both sides agree**.
- **Acknowledge other viewpoints**, and admit their validity where appropriate. Then show where the opponent's argument **breaks down**.
- **Rebuttal (refutation)** takes two forms:
  1. **list all the opposing points**, then give counter-arguments to each; or
  2. **take the points one at a time** and refute each in turn.
- State facts **objectively and dispassionately**, and make a strong case against the objections.
- **Establish the central point from the beginning.** Give background, define important terms, and lay a basis for your view.

</Tab>
<Tab label="Persuasive">

**Argumentative vs persuasive.** The argumentative essay **stops at stating why** the writer holds a view. The persuasive essay **goes a step further**: it gives a logical basis for the reader to **change their view** and makes a **call for action**.

- **Argumentative:** "I see the reason behind your point of view."
- **Persuasive:** "I see the logic behind your argument **and I agree with you**."

**Six forms of evidence** (for both essays):
1. **facts and statistics**;
2. **appeal to authority**;
3. **appeal to emotion**;
4. **examples**;
5. **anecdotes**;
6. **logical interpretation** (appeal to reason).

**Six steps to effective persuasion:**
1. **define the issue** and clarify terms;
2. present **reasons and supporting evidence**;
3. **connect** with the audience;
4. **avoid errors in logic**;
5. **qualify or specify** your claims;
6. **counter opposing reasons** and audience objections.

</Tab>
</Tabs>

## Reasoning and Fallacies in Argument

**Inductive reasoning** gathers and weighs **a series of facts or evidence**, then draws a **general conclusion**. For an inductive argument to be acceptable:
1. the explanation must **account for all the facts**;
2. the evidence must be **observable and reproducible by others**;
3. the evidence must **strongly support the conclusion**.

**Conclusion signals:** *therefore, in conclusion, hence, consequently, based on the findings from a survey…* **Reason signals:** *first, second, because, for instance, as shown by…*

> *A survey of five hundred babies between ages 0–3 months shows a pattern of wellness among babies who are fed strictly on breast milk. The babies sleep for longer hours at a stretch and are less colicky than babies fed on infant formula.*

That survey supports a **relationship**. It does **not** let the writer say formula-fed babies are **always** colicky. "**Always**" turns evidence into **over-generalisation**, and the logic becomes flawed. **Qualify your claims.**

**Deductive reasoning** begins with a **generalisation**, adds a related statement, and ends with a conclusion **necessarily drawn** from the two. This three-statement form is the **syllogism**:

> **Major premise:** All 100-level students of the University of Ibadan must take GES 101.
> **Minor premise:** Motunrayo is a 100-level student of the University of Ibadan.
> **Conclusion:** Motunrayo must take GES 101.

The **major premise must be true and the argument valid** before the conclusion is justified.

### Eight fallacies (errors in logic) to avoid

| Fallacy | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Non sequitur** | A conclusion that **does not follow** from the premises | *All living things can move. A car can move. Therefore, a car is a living thing.* |
| **Hasty generalisation** | A conclusion from **too small a sample** | *The two Lagos drivers I met were rude, so Lagos drivers are rude.* |
| **Stereotypes** | Arguing from general assumptions or **bias about race, gender, religion, age or ethnic group** | *She is a woman, so she cannot lead the engineering team.* |
| **Unreliable authority** | Citing someone who is **not a qualified expert** on the subject | *A popular musician says the vaccine is unsafe, so it must be.* |
| **Ad hominem** | **Attacking the person**, not the issue | *"The Local Government Chairman is a political nutcase, so one can understand why he will not advocate free education for the girl-child."* (coursebook) |
| **False analogy** | Assuming that **some similarities mean total similarity** | *A university is like a business, so students who fail should be "sacked" like workers.* |
| **Begging the question** | A conclusion **without evidence**, or circular reasoning that **assumes what it should prove** | *Our lecturer is the best because he is better than all the others.* |
| **Bandwagon** | "**Everyone is doing it**", so you should too | *"Everyone knows that African leaders are…"* (coursebook) |

**Two slips in the coursebook's examples.**
- **Hasty generalisation.** Its example — *Bankers dress neatly. Thelma dresses neatly. Therefore, Thelma is a banker* — has **exactly the same structure as its non sequitur** (the car argument). Both treat "A has property X; B has X; therefore B is A". A true hasty generalisation leaps from **a few cases to all cases**, as in the table.
- **False analogy.** Its example claims that negotiating with insurgents would bring war "just like when **Dimka led the first coup d'état**". Nigeria's **first** coup (15 January 1966) was led by **Major Chukwuma Kaduna Nzeogwu** and other majors. **Lt-Col. Buka Suka Dimka** led the **failed** coup of 13 February 1976, in which General Murtala Mohammed was killed.

The fallacies are treated in depth in **GST 212** (*Formal and Informal Fallacies*).

## Forms an Essay Can Take

Whether an essay is narrative, descriptive, argumentative or expository, it takes **a particular form**: a **letter, report, article, review**, and so on. Elements of the four types may **co-occur** in one piece. Letters, memos, e-mails and reports are covered in *Official Correspondences*; this lesson takes the **review** and the **term paper**.

## The Review

A **review** is an **expository essay or critique**. *Critique* does **not** mean "to find fault": its Greek root means **to separate, discern or choose**.
- A review **summarises the original** — its main ideas and major supporting points.
- It gives the **reviewer's reactions and the reasons** for them.
- It **evaluates strengths and weaknesses**.
- It **distinguishes facts** (provable by experiment, measurement or research) **from opinions** (expressed beliefs).

**The introduction** should give:
- the **title** and **author**;
- the **number of pages**;
- the **publisher** and **year of publication**;
- the **genre**;
- the **reviewer's name**;
- other relevant background.

### The three steps

<FlowDiagram title="Writing a review">
<FlowStep title="1. Preview (scan)">
- **What is it?** An essay, a novel, a play, a collection of poems?
- Study the **title**.
- The **genre** decides what you examine. For a literary work, look at **plot, characterisation, setting, point of view and theme**.
- Learn the **author's background** (blurb, biography), **other works**, usual **techniques, style and themes**.
- Note the **preface**, dates of **first and current editions**, price (optional), **binding** (paperback or hardcover) and **cover illustration**.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="2. Read">
**Active, slow reading** — to understand, analyse, assess and scrutinise.
- **Weigh the writer's claims** against reason.
- **Annotate**, and **underline or highlight** words that "leap out of the page".
- Mark **transitions** and useful **quotations**.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="3. Review proper">
Present your analysis and reaction, answering the **5 Ws and H**: *Who? What? When? Where? Why? How?*
- State the work's **purpose or thesis**, and show with brief quotations **how it fulfils it**.
- **Compare it** with other works on the topic.
- Did it **meet your expectation**? What did you **learn**? What is its **contribution to scholarship**?
- Give your **overall opinion**. Would you **recommend** it, and to **which audience**?
</FlowStep>
</FlowDiagram>

<Reveal question="Model: a short review of Buchi Emecheta's Second-Class Citizen (the GES 201 set novel)">
**Second-Class Citizen** by **Buchi Emecheta**. Novel (autobiographical fiction); first published in **1974** (London: Allison & Busby). *Reviewed by A. R. Oluwaseun.*

*Second-Class Citizen* follows **Adah**, a determined Igbo girl from Lagos. She fights her way into education, marries **Francis**, and follows him to London. There she discovers that being black, female and a mother makes her a "second-class citizen" twice over. In Nigeria she is limited by **gender**. In England, Francis warns her, "the day you land in England, you are a second-class citizen": landlords reject the family "as soon as it was known that they had children". At home, Francis treats her as "a second-class human". The novel's thesis is that **education, work and self-belief** can carry a woman beyond both labels. Adah's decision to write her first book, *The Bride Price*, and Francis's burning of the manuscript, form the turning point of her escape.

**Strengths:**
- Emecheta writes in **plain, vivid prose**, with humour that softens painful scenes.
- The **third-person point of view stays close to Adah**, so readers share her hopes and humiliations.
- The novel's account of 1960s London **housing discrimination and childminding** is documentary in its detail.

**Weaknesses:**
- **Francis is drawn almost entirely as a villain**, which flattens the marriage.
- Some episodes **summarise years in a page**.

**Verdict:** a moving, readable and historically valuable novel. Recommended for students of African literature, gender studies and migration, and for any reader who has felt like an outsider.

*(Structure used: bibliographic introduction → summary → thesis → strengths → weaknesses → recommendation. Check the page count and publisher of your own edition.)*
</Reveal>

## The Term Paper (Research Paper)

A **research or term paper** is an **extensive, formal composition** that assembles information **from readings in a number of sources**. It tests **how much you have discovered and internalised** about a subject. Most are **expository**, with some description or persuasion; a paper may define concepts, explain processes, defend a view or uphold an idea.

<OrderPuzzle title="Steps in writing a term paper">

```order
Choose and narrow the subject :: Interesting, topical and relevant to the course; not too wide for the assignment; enough materials available.
Draw up a working bibliography :: Books and journals in the library, electronic journals, interviews or correspondence with authorities.
Read and take notes :: Record the author, title, place, year and page FIRST. Decide what to quote word for word and what to put in your own words.
Arrange the notes under sub-headings :: Discard what doesn't fit.
Draw up an outline :: To ensure proper organisation and thorough treatment of every facet.
Write the first draft :: A rough draft — don't waste time on the perfect opening sentence; just put your thoughts together.
Write the final draft :: Polish style, transitions and layout.
Add footnotes or endnotes and the bibliography :: Follow the style your teacher or department specifies.
```

</OrderPuzzle>

**Choosing a subject.**
- If none is given, choose one that is **interesting, topical and relevant** to the course.
- Make it **narrow enough to treat well**: a narrow subject treated deeply beats a broad one treated superficially.
- Avoid subjects that are **too biased, too current, too specialised or too personal** to research.
- It should **appeal to readers** and **add something to scholarship**.

**Using sources — and plagiarism.**
- A research paper is **not a list of quotations**: **evaluate** the claims of the authors you cite and present facts **in your own words**.
- A **serious and common fault is using another's words or data without acknowledging the source — plagiarism**: presenting identified scholars' "words, data and information … as if they were your own".
- **Always give credit**, especially for word-for-word quotation.
- Put a quotation of **fewer than three lines** in **quotation marks** within the text. Set **longer** quotations off as an **indented extract**.

**Footnotes, endnotes and bibliography.**
- **Footnotes:** a raised number after the quotation or borrowed idea, in sequence, with the note **at the bottom of that page**.
- **Endnotes:** the same notes **collected at the end** of the paper, on a separate page **just before the bibliography**.
- **Bibliography:** the list of **all sources used**. It must be **complete, accurate and consistent**.
  - Arrange it **alphabetically by authors' surnames**.
  - With co-authors, the second and third authors' names are written first name first (*Strunk, W. and E. B. White*).
  - When an entry runs past one line, **indent the second line** (a "hanging indent").
- **Formats:** **APA** (American Psychological Association) and **MLA** (Modern Language Association). The University of Ibadan also has its own format for postgraduate theses and dissertations.
- **Charts, tables and diagrams** must be **relevant and clearly labelled** — don't pad the paper with them.

| *(standard practice)* | **APA (7th edition)** | **MLA (9th edition)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Bibliography entry** | Emecheta, B. (1974). *Second-class citizen*. Allison & Busby. | Emecheta, Buchi. *Second-Class Citizen*. Allison & Busby, 1974. |
| **List is called** | References | Works Cited |
| **In-text citation** | (Emecheta, 1974, p. 45) | (Emecheta 45) |
| **Distinctive features** | **Year comes early**; initials for first names; sentence case for book titles | **Full first name**; **year at the end**; title case |

```sort

```

## Problems Students Face in Writing

| Problem | Remedy (from the coursebook) |
|---|---|
| **Lack of writing skills** | Learn the demands and technicalities of each form; the Use of English course material is a boon. |
| **Misunderstanding the assignment** | Ask: "What is expected of me? Is this argumentative or persuasive? If descriptive, do I know enough to paint a vivid portrait?" |
| **No ideas** | **Brainstorm (ideation):** jot down all you know; research; rub minds with others; make an **outline**. Not every idea belongs in the essay. |
| **Staring at a blank page** | Remember it's a **first draft**: **free-write** and fine-tune later. Organise into **introduction, body and conclusion**, with **one unit of thought per paragraph**. |
| **Not knowing how to re-write** | Revise for **correctness, coherence and clarity**. |
| **Language errors** | Check spelling, diction, tenses and subject–verb agreement; keep a **dictionary and thesaurus** handy. **Never use text-speak:** *be4, evry1, 'cos, nd, u, luv*. |
| **Punctuation** | Know when to use the comma, semicolon, colon, exclamation mark and full stop. **A full stop after a group of words does not make a sentence** — every sentence must make complete sense, without ambiguity. |

<Reveal question="2. Identify the three major steps in writing a review.">

3. The **review proper** — your analysis and reaction: the 5 Ws and H, thesis, strengths and weaknesses, comparison, recommendation.

<Reveal question="3. Distinguish the argumentative essay from the persuasive essay.">
Both use evidence and reasoning to support a view.
- The **argumentative** essay stops at **justifying** the writer's position: the reader says, "I see the reason behind your point of view."
- The **persuasive** essay goes further, giving a logical basis for the reader to **change their view** and making a **call for action**: "I see the logic behind your argument and I agree with you."
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. What 'development tools' does the coursebook say a narrative essay needs?">
- A **plot**: a meaningful, dramatic sequence of events.
- **Conflict** that looms large and needs resolution.
- **Characters** clearly distinguished, real and original.
- A **setting** fixed in **space and time**: name the place and the period.

**Dialogue** and **supporting details** make it dramatic.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Effective writing** suits **purpose and reader**, and has **economy, simplicity and clarity**.
- **Expository** explains: process analysis, comparison and contrast, classification, definition, causal analysis. **Narrative** tells a story: plot, conflict, characters, setting, dialogue, pace, point of view. **Descriptive** paints with words: spatial order, the senses, exact words, analogies.
- **Argumentative** justifies a view: common ground, acknowledge others, rebuttal. **Persuasive** adds a **call for action**, using facts, authority, emotion, examples, anecdotes and reason.
- **Inductive:** evidence leads to a general conclusion (qualify it; avoid "always"). **Deductive:** the syllogism. **Avoid fallacies:** non sequitur, hasty generalisation, stereotypes, unreliable authority, ad hominem, false analogy, begging the question, bandwagon.
- **Review** = critique, not fault-finding. Bibliographic introduction, then **preview → reading → review proper**; evaluate strengths and weaknesses; separate fact from opinion; recommend.
- **Term paper:** subject → working bibliography → notes (publication details first) → outline → first draft → final draft → notes and bibliography. **Acknowledge every source; plagiarism is a serious fault.** APA and MLA.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Read the essay question twice and **identify the type** before planning. A "persuasive" question that gets an argumentative answer **misses the call to action**.
- In fallacy questions, look at **what the argument relies on**: the person (ad hominem), the crowd (bandwagon), a tiny sample (hasty generalisation), or a conclusion that doesn't follow (non sequitur).
- Expect "**state the three steps in writing a review**" and "**what is plagiarism?**" — short, high-yield questions.
