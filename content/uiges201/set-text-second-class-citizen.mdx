## The Author and the Novel

**Buchi Emecheta** (1944–2017) was born in **Lagos** to **Igbo** parents from **Ibusa** (spelt "**Ibuza**" in the novel). She went to the **Methodist Girls' High School, Lagos**, married young, and moved to **London in 1962**. There she raised five children largely alone and studied sociology at the University of London. She became one of Africa's most important women writers. Her works include ***In the Ditch*** (1972), ***Second-Class Citizen*** (1974), ***The Bride Price*** (1976), ***The Slave Girl*** (1977), ***The Joys of Motherhood*** (1979) and the autobiography ***Head Above Water*** (1986).

***Second-Class Citizen*** is **strongly autobiographical**: Adah's story closely follows Emecheta's own early life. The novel is dedicated **"To my dear children, Florence, Sylvester, Jake, Christy and Alice, without whose sweet background noises this book would not have been written."** Its sequel in Adah's story is ***In the Ditch***.

**Setting:**

- **Lagos, Nigeria** (chapters 1–2) — **colonial and newly independent** Nigeria of the 1940s–1950s;
- **London, England** (chapters 3–13) — mainly **Kentish Town**, in the early **1960s**: cramped rented rooms, the Underground, libraries, hospitals, and signs saying "**Sorry, No Coloureds**".

**Narration:** **third-person**, closely following **Adah's** thoughts and feelings, with frequent comments from an older, wiser narrator looking back.

## Characters

| Character | Who they are |
|---|---|
| **Adah Ofili (Adah Obi)** | The **protagonist**. A clever, determined Igbo girl who fights her way to education, a library career and England, and finally to independence as a mother and a writer. Pet names: **"Nne nna"** ("father's mother") and **"Adah Eze"** ("princess") |
| **Francis Obi** | Adah's **husband**, a student of **Cost and Works accountancy**. **Lazy**, controlling and traditional; he lives on Adah's earnings, repeatedly **fails his exams**, believes a wife who refuses him should "have sense beaten into her", and finally **burns her manuscript** |
| **Pa** | Adah's **father**, a railwayman who supports her schooling. He **dies** while she is a child |
| **Ma** | Adah's **mother**, a seamstress who doubts the value of educating a girl. After Pa's death she is **"inherited" by Pa's brother** |
| **Boy** | Adah's **younger brother**; the family's money is spent on **his** education |
| **Mr Cole** | The kind **Sierra Leonean teacher** who welcomes Adah when she **walks into school uninvited** |
| **Cousin Vincent** | Her mother's relative who **canes** her for using the two shillings meant for meat to pay her exam fee |
| **Lawyer Nweze** | Ibuza's **first lawyer** from the UK, welcomed like a "Messiah" — a symbol of the **"United Kingdom" dream** |
| **Francis's parents** | They profit from Adah's salary; Francis's Pa tells him the money will come **into their house** |
| **Titi and Vicky** | Adah's **first two children** — a daughter and a son. **Vicky** nearly dies of **virus meningitis** |
| **Bubu** | Her **third child**, a boy born by **Caesarean section** |
| **Dada** | Her **fourth child**, a daughter born in **May** |
| **Trudy** | The **child-minder** in Malden Road who **neglects** the children and **lies** about it |
| **Miss Stirling** | The **children's officer** in Malden Road |
| **Mrs Konrad** | The friendly **Czech chief librarian** at the North Finchley library |
| **Pa Noble** | An old **Nigerian landlord** in **Willes Road**. He came in the forties to study law, kept failing, and ended up a lift man and a "clown" for his English workmates |
| **Mrs Noble (Sue)** | Pa Noble's **wife**, "a large-boned **Birmingham** woman", with five children |
| **Janet** | A **Cockney** girl; wife of **Mr Babalola**, a Northern Nigerian student who took her in when she was sixteen and pregnant |
| **Mr Okpara** | An **Igbo man** who finds Adah in the park after a fight and persuades her to go home and "beg" Francis |
| **Bill** | A **Canadian** colleague at **Chalk Farm Library** who introduces Adah to **James Baldwin** and tells her that **"black was beautiful"** — "the first real friend" she had in England |
| **Peggy** | An **Irish** colleague at Chalk Farm Library |

## Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

<FlowDiagram title="Second-Class Citizen — Adah's journey">
<FlowStep title="1. Childhood">
Adah is born in Lagos during the **Second World War**. She is a **disappointment because she is a girl**, so nobody records her birth. She dreams of the **"United Kingdom"**, which her father speaks of as if it were heaven, especially after the town welcomes **Lawyer Nweze**. Denied school while her brother **Boy** attends **Ladi-Lak Institute**, she **walks into Mr Cole's class** on her own. Ma is taken to the police station and **forced to drink gari** for "child neglect", and Pa agrees to send Adah to school.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="2. Escape into Elitism">
**Pa dies.** Adah becomes a **servant** in her uncle's house; **Ma is inherited by Pa's brother**; the family money goes on **Boy's** education. Adah keeps her schooling only because educated girls fetch a **bigger bride price**. She uses **two shillings** meant for meat to pay her **entrance-examination** fee, and **Cousin Vincent** canes her. She wins a **full-board scholarship** to the **Methodist Girls' High School**. Needing a home, she **marries Francis Obi**, works at the **American Consulate Library** on Campbell Street (her big salary supports Francis's family), and has **Titi** and **Vicky**. Francis goes to England; Adah follows by ship with the children.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="3. A Cold Welcome">
England in March is **bitterly cold**. Francis has changed: in **one cramped room in Ashdown Street, Kentish Town**, he tells her that whatever she was in Lagos, "**the day you land in England, you are a second-class citizen**". The other Nigerian tenants want the children **fostered**. Adah gets a job at the **North Finchley library**, though she is already pregnant.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="4. The Daily Minders">
Adah loves her job and her kind boss, **Mrs Konrad**. The children go to **Trudy**, a registered child-minder who **neglects** them, leaving them in the back yard in wet nappies.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="5. An Expensive Lesson">
**Vicky falls gravely ill** with **virus meningitis**. Trudy **lies** to the children's officer, **Miss Stirling**, destroying Adah's belief "**that the white man never lied**". Trudy is **struck off** the council list, and the children get **nursery places** — at a terrible price.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="6. 'Sorry, No Coloureds'">
They are given **notice to quit** because of the children. House-hunting brings door after door marked "**Sorry, No Coloureds**", and the few hopeful places reject them "as soon as it was known that they had children". Francis **fails his exams** and blames Adah. She points out that the English **worship Jesus, "a coloured man"**, yet refuse coloured families.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="7. The Ghetto">
The story of the Nigerians who came in the forties to "get a quick degree in Law" and failed. Among them is **Pa Noble**, who bought a run-down house in **Willes Road**. He humiliates himself before his English wife **Sue (Mrs Noble)**, but gives the Obis a room.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="8. Role Acceptance">
In **December**, Adah gives birth to **Bubu** by **Caesarean section** — a big, hungry baby she calls "this Mohammed Ali of a son".
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="9. Learning the Rules">
In the **hospital ward**, Adah feels isolated among chattering English mothers. She learns a **new code**: to **thank people, even for their smiles**.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="10. Applying the Rules">
A harsh winter in **one room** that is kitchen, bedroom and playroom. Vicky is **bitten by bugs** in Pa Noble's house; a doctor refuses to come at **Christmas** until Francis goes to the police.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="11. Population Control">
Adah goes to the **Family Planning Clinic** and learns about the Pill, the jelly and **the cap**. Francis objects, and she has to **forge his signature**. She passes part of her **library examination**; Francis **fails again** and writes to his parents blaming the cap. Adah is **pregnant again**.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="12. The Collapse">
Adah's marriage collapses. **Dada** is born in **May**. While nursing her, Adah buys ***Teach Yourself to Write*** at **Foyle's** and writes a novel, ***The Bride Price***.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="13. The Ditch Pull">
A brief, happy summer as a housewife. **Bill** takes the manuscript seriously — but **Francis burns it**: "my family would never be happy if a wife of mine was permitted to write a book like that". To Adah it is **killing her child**. She takes a job at the **British Museum** and **leaves Francis**. In court, **Francis denies they were ever married** (he has **burnt** the marriage certificate, her passport and the children's birth certificates) and says he does not mind the children being **sent for adoption**. Adah, pregnant with her **fifth** child, answers: "**The children are mine, and that is enough. I shall never let them down as long as I am alive.**" Outside, an old friend calls her by her childhood name, **"Nne nna"**.
</FlowStep>
</FlowDiagram>

## Themes

| Theme | How the novel shows it |
|---|---|
| **Gender oppression** | Adah's birth is unrecorded because she is a girl. **Boys come first** in education. Her schooling is valued only for a bigger **bride price**. Ma is "**inherited**". In marriage, Francis claims her body, earnings and decisions: to him a woman is "a second-class human", to have "sense beaten into her" if she refuses. Even women (Ma, the landlady) uphold the system. |
| **Education as liberation** | From walking into school uninvited to the scholarship, the library job and the library exams, **education is Adah's way out** — the "**Presence**" (her dream) drives her. Francis, by contrast, **wastes** his chances. |
| **Race and "second-class citizenship"** | In England Adah meets **"Sorry, No Coloureds"**, Trudy's lies, and Francis's warning that every black person in England is "second-class". The novel also shows Nigerians **discriminating against each other** and **accepting** inferiority (Pa Noble playing the clown). |
| **Marriage and patriarchy** | Francis lives on Adah's wages ("laying the golden eggs") but controls her — **forbidding contraception**, burning her book, denying the marriage in court. Mr Okpara's "Ibo psychology" assumes that "men never do wrong, only the women". |
| **Motherhood** | Adah's children are the centre of her life: Vicky's illness, refusing to foster them, four children under five. Ending with "**The children are mine**", motherhood becomes **strength**, not a burden. |
| **The immigrant experience** | The cold, **cramped single rooms**, the dream of the UK as heaven against its reality, **culture shock** (child-minders, the Welfare State, hospital manners), and the **failed dreams** of men like Pa Noble. |
| **Self-assertion and independence** | Adah grows from an obedient wife ("anything for a quiet life") to a woman who **forges a signature**, **writes a book**, **leaves**, and claims her children — the beginning of Emecheta's own story as a writer. |

## Symbols and Motifs

| Symbol or motif | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **"The United Kingdom"** | At first **heaven** ("like paying God a visit") — the dream that drives Adah; later a place of cold, rejection and second-class status |
| **The Presence** | Adah's **inner dream or guiding spirit**, which comforts her. It "seemed to have deserted her" in England, then returns as the urge to **write** |
| **The two shillings** | Adah's first **act of self-determination** — and her willingness to pay the price (the caning) |
| **The title "Second-Class Citizen"** | The **inferior status** imposed on black immigrants, and on Adah **as a woman** within her own marriage |
| ***The Bride Price*** manuscript | Adah's **creative self — "her child"**. Its burning is Francis's attempt to **destroy her independence**, and it is the last straw |
| **The grey bird** | Seen singing alone near the public baths — "**happy in its lonely freedom**", a hint of Adah's future |
| **The cap** (contraception) | **Control over her own body**, which Francis tries to deny her |
| **"Nne nna"** | Adah's childhood name, heard again at the end — a return to her **true self** and a fresh start |

## Narrative Technique and Style

- **Third-person narration focused on Adah**, with the voice of an older Adah commenting on her younger self ("Thinking back on it all now that she was grown up…").
- **Autobiographical fiction:** Adah's life mirrors Emecheta's (an Ibuza family, Lagos childhood, Methodist Girls' High School, the American Consulate library, London, four children under five, the burnt manuscript).
- **Irony and humour,** even in pain: Ma forced to drink gari for "child neglect", Pa's horror that Lawyer Nweze "could not swallow pounded yam any more", the "cap for your other end instead of for your head".
- **Social commentary:** essays within the story on the Nigerian "élite" in England, the Welfare State, colonial attitudes.
- **Nigerian (Igbo and Yoruba) words and beliefs:** *Nne nna*, *Iyawo*, *lappa*, *boli*, *gari*, the river goddess **Oboshi**.
- **Simple, direct, conversational English** that makes the novel easy to read.

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. Discuss the theme of gender oppression in Second-Class Citizen.">
Adah's oppression begins at birth: as a girl she is a "disappointment" whose birth goes unrecorded. Boys are preferred for education — the family money goes to Boy — and her schooling is tolerated only because it will raise her bride price. After Pa's death, Ma is "inherited" by his brother and Adah becomes a servant. In marriage Francis controls her: he lives on her wages, forbids contraception (she must forge his signature), blames her for his failures, treats her as "a second-class human" and finally burns her manuscript. Even Mr Okpara assumes the woman is always at fault. Yet Adah resists — through education, work, contraception, writing and finally leaving — so the novel is also a story of women's self-liberation.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Explain the significance of the title.">
Francis tells Adah that whatever her status in Lagos, "the day you land in England, you are a second-class citizen". Black immigrants face "Sorry, No Coloureds" signs, cramped rooms, lying child-minders and humiliations (Pa Noble the "clown"), and many accept the inferior status. The title also applies to Adah as a woman: within her marriage and her culture she is treated as second-class — her wages, body and ambitions belong to her husband and his family. The novel shows her rejecting both kinds of second-class status.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Compare and contrast Adah and Francis.">
Adah is ambitious, hard-working and determined: she forces her way into school, wins a scholarship, earns a high salary at the American Consulate, works in London libraries, passes library exams and writes a novel. She is also loyal and long-suffering ("anything for a quiet life"), until Francis destroys her book. Francis is lazy, traditional and dependent: he lives on her money ("laying the golden eggs"), fails his accountancy exams repeatedly, blames her, sees a wife as "a second-class human", resists contraception, burns her manuscript and finally disowns her and the children in court. Adah grows; Francis does not.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Discuss the immigrant experience as portrayed in the novel.">
The dream of the United Kingdom as heaven meets a cold reality: bitter weather, one cramped room in Kentish Town, pressure from other Nigerians to foster the children, racist "Sorry, No Coloureds" signs, Trudy's neglect and lies, and bug-ridden lodgings. Emecheta also shows the immigrant community's own failings — men like Pa Noble who came to "get a quick degree in Law" and failed, and Nigerians who discriminate against each other. Yet England also offers Adah opportunities denied at home — the Welfare State, nurseries, contraception, libraries and the freedom to write.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Buchi Emecheta** (1944–2017); ***Second-Class Citizen*** (1974) is **autobiographical**; the sequel is *In the Ditch*. Set in **Lagos** (ch. 1–2) and **London's Kentish Town** (ch. 3–13).
- **Adah** (protagonist) vs **Francis** (husband, failing accountancy student). Children: **Titi, Vicky, Bubu, Dada**; a fifth on the way. Also Trudy, Miss Stirling, Mrs Konrad, **Pa and Mrs Noble**, Janet, Mr Okpara, **Bill**, Peggy, Mr Cole, Cousin Vincent, Lawyer Nweze.

</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Past papers give **extracts** and ask **who is speaking, who is addressed, and what is happening**. Know the **key speeches** (Francis's "second-class citizen" warning, the burning of the manuscript, the court scene).
- Learn the **children's names in order** (Titi, Vicky, Bubu, Dada) and the **places** (Ashdown Street, Willes Road, Malden Road, North Finchley and Chalk Farm libraries, the British Museum).
- For essays, discuss **at least two themes** with **specific episodes** as evidence, and explain the **title**.
