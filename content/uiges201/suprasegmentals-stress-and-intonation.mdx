## Segmentals and Suprasegmentals

Spoken English can be studied from two angles:

| **Segmental** aspect | **Suprasegmental** aspect |
|---|---|
| The individual **vowels and consonants** (the previous lesson) | Features that operate **above the segment**: the **syllable**, **stress**, **intonation** and **rhythm** |

The chapter's author, **Adesina B. Sunday**, stresses why this matters in Nigeria: our indigenous languages, which influence our English, are **tone languages**. They use pitch on each syllable to change word meaning, and they pattern prominence differently from English.

---

## The Syllable

> **The syllable is the smallest pronounceable unit of a language.**

It is the **basic domain** in which the other suprasegmental features operate. Don't confuse it with the **morpheme**, the smallest **meaningful** unit:

- **syllable** = the basic unit of **pronunciation**;
- **morpheme** = the basic unit of **meaning**.

Sometimes the two **coincide**: *boy, girl, read, make, buy* are each one syllable **and** one morpheme. In a longer word, a syllable may also be a morpheme — *faith* in *faithfulness*, *see* in *foreseeable*.

| Word type | Syllables | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Monosyllabic** | 1 | boys, house, trains, safe, take, rings, thoughts, realms, types |
| **Disyllabic** | 2 | ru-ler, hun-ter, cap-tive, buil-der, sa-viour, ca-ble, pur-ple, doc-tor, daugh-ter |
| **Trisyllabic** (three-syllable) | 3 | me-lo-dy, du-ra-ble, a-ban-don, a-sso-ciate, a-tten-tion, con-du-cive, cal-cu-late, im-por-tant |

### The structure of a syllable

| Part | What it is | In *spray* /spreɪ/ |
|---|---|---|
| **Onset** | Consonant(s) **before** the vowel | /spr/ |
| **Nucleus (peak)** | The **vowel** — the only **obligatory** part | /eɪ/ |
| **Coda** | Consonant(s) **after** the vowel | — (none) |

- English allows **at most three consonants in the onset** (*spray, scrap*) and **at most four in the coda** (*texts* /teksts/, *tempts* /tempts/).
- **Syllabic consonants:** in restricted cases — only at the **end of a word**, in an **unstressed** syllable — a consonant can be the nucleus. The syllabic consonants are **/m n l/**: rhythm /rɪðm/, Britain /brɪtn/, bottle /bɒtl/, little /lɪtl/, shuttle /ʃʌtl/.

## Stress

> **Stress is the prominence placed on a particular syllable in a word.** In a word of more than one syllable, the syllables do **not** all get the same degree of prominence.

- A **monosyllabic content word** (noun, verb, adjective, adverb) is automatically stressed. **Function words** (prepositions, conjunctions, articles…) are normally **unstressed**, unless emphasis is needed.
- The real challenge is **which syllable** to stress in longer words — **especially for Nigerian speakers**, whose languages use **tone** rather than stress.

**Production and perception.** The speaker uses **more muscular energy** on a stressed syllable, and the hearer perceives it as **more prominent**. Four cues signal prominence:

| Cue | A stressed syllable is… |
|---|---|
| **Loudness** | **louder** |
| **Length** | **longer** |
| **Pitch** | **higher** |
| **Vowel quality** | **stronger** (a full vowel). This is why **/ə/ (schwa) is never found in a stressed syllable** — it is the **weakest** vowel in English. |

**Free versus fixed stress.** Stress is **fixed** in languages where it always falls on a particular syllable — in **French**, the **last** syllable. English stress is **free**: it can fall on **any** syllable. That makes English stress hard to learn. Some scholars propose rules, others reject them because of the exceptions. The practical advice: **learn each word's pronunciation together with its meaning.**

### Degrees of stress

| Degree | Where | How it is marked |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | The **most prominent** syllable | A small stroke **above and before** the syllable: **ˈ**ready; or CAPITALS: READ-y |
| **Secondary** | The **next** most prominent (in longer words) | A small stroke **below and before**: **ˌ**congratuˈlation |
| **Tertiary** | All other syllables | **Not marked** |

### Two-syllable words

The textbook names **four factors** that attract the primary stress to a syllable:

1. it contains a **long vowel or a diphthong**;
2. it does **not** contain a **short vowel or schwa** /ə/;
3. it ends in **more than one consonant**;
4. it does **not** contain **/əʊ/** — the textbook says "in the first syllable", but its own examples *follow* and *borrow* show the point: a **final** /əʊ/ is **not** stressed.

| Word class | Stress on the **first** syllable | Stress on the **second** syllable |
|---|---|---|
| **Verbs** | ˈfavour, ˈenter, ˈmeddle, ˈfollow, ˈborrow | aˈccept, aˈpply, aˈssist, aˈppoint |
| **Adjectives** | ˈready, ˈfaithful, ˈdirty, ˈclever, ˈshallow, ˈconscious, ˈcareful, ˈhonest | — |
| **Nouns** | ˈmercy, ˈmoney, ˈmorning, ˈlorry, ˈdevil, ˈsister, ˈtrader, ˈcolour, ˈtailor, ˈhoney | — |
| **Adverbs** | ˈvery, ˈsurely, ˈmerely, ˈrarely, ˈseldom, ˈoften | — |

**Rule of thumb:** most two-syllable **nouns, adjectives and adverbs** stress the **first** syllable. **Verbs** stress the **second** syllable when it is "strong" (a long vowel, a diphthong or a consonant cluster: acˈcept, apˈply).

### Three-syllable words

- **Verbs:** the **last** syllable is **not** stressed if it has a **short vowel** and ends in **no more than one consonant**. Stress then moves back: enˈcourage, enˈcounter, deˈtermine, inˈterpret. If the last syllable has a **long vowel or a diphthong**, or ends in **two or more consonants**, it **is** stressed: resuˈrrect, enterˈtain.
- **Nouns:** if the last syllable has **/əʊ/ or a short vowel**, it is not stressed. The **penultimate** (second-to-last) syllable takes the stress if it has a **long vowel or a diphthong**: diˈsaster, toˈmato, toˈmorrow, syˈnopsis, poˈtato.
- **Adjectives** often behave like nouns: imˈportant, meˈlodic, sagaˈcious, inˈdustrious, inˈdustrial.

### Suffixes and stress

| Effect | Suffixes | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **The suffix takes the stress** | -ee, -eer, -aire, -ese, -ette, -esque | refuˈgee, appoinˈtee, attenˈdee; auctionˈeer, mountainˈeer; questionˈnaire, millionˈaire; Japaˈnese, Chiˈnese, Lebaˈnese, Senegaˈlese; cigaˈrette, kitcheˈnette; pictuˈresque |
| **No change to the stress** | -ing, -ish, -able, -ful, -age, -al, -en, -ment, -ance | ˈwriting, ˈchallenging; ˈchildish; comˈmendable, ˈmanageable; ˈcareful, ˈtruthful; ˈstorage, ˈbreakage; reˈfusal, acˈquittal; ˈwiden, ˈfrighten; acˈknowledgement, comˈmitment; aˈssurance, enˈdurance |
| **The suffix shifts the stress** (to the syllable **just before** the suffix) | -graphy, -ion, -eous, -ity, -ic, -ious, -ive, -ial, -ian | phoˈtography, radiˈography; educaˈtion, satisˈfaction, motiˈvation; couˈrageous, advanˈtageous; suitaˈbility, responsiˈbility, gulliˈbility; cliˈmatic, teˈrrific, hoˈrrific, symˈbolic, aristoˈcratic, hyperˈbolic, hisˈtoric; inˈjurious; reˈflexive; proˈverbial, inˈdustrial, comˈmercial; coˈmedian, hisˈtorian, graˈmmarian |

Compare **ˈphotograph → phoˈtography → photoˈgraphic**: each stress-shifting suffix pulls the stress to the syllable just before it.

### Word-class pairs

Some words are **spelt alike** but change word class by **moving the stress**: **nouns stress the first** syllable, **verbs the second**.

| Noun | Verb |
|---|---|
| ˈexport | exˈport |
| ˈimport | imˈport |
| ˈrebel | reˈbel |
| ˈrecord | reˈcord |
| ˈinsult | inˈsult |
| ˈconduct | conˈduct |
| ˈobject | obˈject |
| ˈescort | esˈcort |
| ˈcontract | conˈtract |

**But not always:** *ˈgraduate, ˈcomment, ˈcontact* keep the **first-syllable** stress as both noun and verb. *(Beyond the textbook: the verb "graduate" also changes its last vowel — the verb ends in /eɪt/, the noun in /ət/.)*

### Compound words

| Type of compound | Stress | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Noun + noun** | On the **first** element | ˈclassroom, ˈgatekeeper, ˈsunrise, ˈteacup, ˈgoalkeeper, ˈnetwork, ˈheadache, ˈbusinessman, ˈtypewriter |
| **Compound adverbs** | On the **second** element | downˈstream, southˈwest, upˈhill, downˈhill |
| **First element is a number** | Usually on the **final** element | first-ˈclass, three-ˈcornered, first-ˈbase, first-ˈlady |
| **Compound verbs whose first element is an adverb** | On the **final** element | back-ˈpedal, upˈgrade, overˈlook, ill-ˈtreat, downˈgrade |

## Intonation

When we speak, the voice does not stay level — it **fluctuates**. The range of the voice is called **pitch**, and when pitch varies we have **intonation**. Each person has their **own pitch range**: one person's highest pitch may be another's lowest.

### The four tunes

| Tune | Symbol | On "Yes" / "No" |
|---|---|---|
| **Rise** | ↗ | ↗Yes? ↗No? |
| **Fall** | ↘ | ↘Yes. ↘No. |
| **Fall-rise** | ↘↗ (V) | ↘↗Yes… ↘↗No… |
| **Rise-fall** | ↗↘ (Λ) | ↗↘Yes! ↗↘No! |

### The tone-unit

The **tone-unit** (also called the **tone-group** or **sense-unit**) is the **domain of intonation**. Phonological units form a hierarchy:

**phoneme → syllable → foot → tone-unit → utterance**

- **phoneme:** a single sound (a vowel or a consonant);
- **syllable:** the smallest pronounceable unit;
- **foot:** a strong (stressed) syllable plus the weak syllables that follow it;
- **tone-unit:** the domain of intonation;
- **utterance:** any stretch of speech with clear pauses before and after it.

A tone-unit, and even an utterance, can be **a single syllable**: *Go! Stay! Bend!*

**Structure of the tone-unit:** **(pre-head) (head) TONIC SYLLABLE (tail)** — only the tonic syllable is **obligatory**.

| Part | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Tonic syllable (TS)** | The syllable that **carries the tone**, where the **pitch change begins**. It is the **most prominent** syllable and carries the **tonic (nuclear) stress**, usually on the **last lexical word** (noun, verb, adjective, adverb) in the unit. |
| **Pre-head (PH)** | All **unstressed** syllables **before the first stressed** syllable |
| **Head (H)** | From the **first stressed** syllable up to the tonic syllable |
| **Tail (T)** | Any syllables **after** the tonic syllable |

| Utterance | Analysis |
|---|---|
| Our governor is **wick**ed. | PH *Our* · H *governor is* · TS *wick* · T *ed* |
| See me in my **car**. | H *See me in my* · TS *car* |
| In this **place** | PH *In this* · TS *place* |
| David is one of the **teach**ers. | H *David is one of the* · TS *teach* · T *ers* |
| Happy **home** | H *Happy* · TS *home* |
| **Stay** there. | TS *Stay* · T *there* |
| Doing that is **dan**gerous. | H *Doing that is* · TS *dan* · T *gerous* |
| In a **jif**fy | PH *In a* · TS *jif* · T *fy* |
| Our **man** | PH *Our* · TS *man* — the textbook labels *man* as a tail (T), but every tone-unit **must** have a tonic syllable |
| **Save** us. | TS *Save* · T *us* — the textbook labels *us* "H", but syllables **after** the tonic form the **tail** |

### The four functions of intonation

**1. Grammatical function** — showing the structure of an utterance:

- **Boundaries** between phrases, clauses and sentences. A **rise** is typical at internal boundaries and a **fall** at the end: *The boy ↗, the teacher ↗ and the children are ↘hungry.* / *If you are ↗ready, call ↘me.*
- **Sentence type.** **Yes/no (polar) questions** usually **rise**: *Are you ↗ready?* *Can I ↗enter?* **Wh-questions** usually **fall**: *Where did you ↘keep it?* *Who is leading the ↘group?* *How is she ↘now?*
- **Disambiguation** by moving the tone-unit boundary:
  - *Those who passed **|** immediately gained admission.* Everyone who passed was admitted, straight away.
  - *Those who passed immediately **|** gained admission.* Only those who passed at the first attempt were admitted.

**2. Attitudinal function** — showing emotions and feelings:

| Tune | Attitude or use | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Rise** | General question | *Is this the way?* *Are we free?* |
| | More is to come | *Do it well (and you will be honoured).* |
| | Encouraging | *You are on the right track.* *It isn't deep.* |
| | Listing (rise on each item, **fall on the last**) | *Guava ↗, cashew ↗, mango ↗ and apple ↘.* |
| **Fall** | Finality | *Move now.* *That's my conclusion.* *I rest my case.* |
| **Rise-fall** | Surprise, being impressed | *You made it!* *You again!* *This car!* *Only me!* |
| **Fall-rise** | Uncertainty | *You are capable…* *She is qualified…* |
| | Polite request | *Should we proceed?* *May I come in?* |

Attitude is also shown **non-linguistically**: facial expression, body movement and gesture.

**3. Accentual (contrastive) function** — placing the tonic stress on a word, even one not normally stressed, to **contrast** it with something else:

- *She bought a **red** car* (not a blue one).
- *We were **quite** impressed* (not just somewhat impressed).
- *James **should** always exhibit caution* (not *may*).
- *I **am** your leader* (not *was*).

**4. Discourse function** — marking **new** versus **given** information:

- *The **bird** stays in this nest* — "bird" is new information.
- *The bird stays in this **cage*** — "cage" is new information.

**Given** information is already known or assumed; **new** information is fresh in the context. To learn English intonation, **listen regularly to colloquial English**.

## Rhythm

**Rhythm** is a key feature that distinguishes **native** from **non-native** varieties of English. It means that phonetic events **recur at roughly equal (isochronous) intervals**. Fluent, uninterrupted speech has rhythm.

| **Syllable-timed** languages | **Stress-timed** languages |
|---|---|
| **Every syllable** comes at roughly regular intervals | **Stressed syllables** come at roughly regular intervals; the unstressed syllables between them are **squeezed** |
| **Igbo, Yoruba** and most **Nigerian languages** | **English** |

In stress-timing, the unit of rhythm is the **foot**: a **stressed syllable plus all the unstressed syllables after it**, up to the next stress. Feet are only **approximately** equal, and English avoids **stress clash** (stresses too close together). A foot with many syllables is **compressed** to keep the beat.

| Utterance | Syllables | Feet |
|---|---|---|
| The ˈman has ˈgone | **4** — the · man · has · gone | **2** — ˈman has · ˈgone |
| Our ˈclass ˈchallenges him ˈalways | **8** | **3** — ˈclass · ˈchallenges him · ˈalways |
| The coˈmedian is ˈmaking ˈfrantic ˈefforts to ˈtake his ˈcomedy to ˈCanada | **21** | **7** — ˈmedian is · ˈmaking · ˈfrantic · ˈefforts to · ˈtake his · ˈcomedy to · ˈCanada |

*The textbook's counts differ: it gives 5 syllables for the first example and 6 feet for the third. Its own diagram marks only four syllables for "The man has gone" and seven stressed syllables in the third example, so 4 syllables and 7 feet are correct.*

<Analogy>
Clap on each **stressed** syllable: **ˈclass** … **ˈchal**lenges him … **ˈal**ways. The claps come at a steady beat, so "challenges him" (four syllables) must be said **faster** than "class" (one syllable) to fit the same time. Yoruba and Igbo give **every** syllable its own clap, which is why Nigerian English often sounds "syllable-timed".
</Analogy>

### Weak forms

To keep the rhythm, words in **unstressed positions** take their **weak forms**:

| Word | Strong form | Weak form(s) |
|---|---|---|
| to | /tuː/ | /tə/, /tu/ |
| from | /frɒm/ | /frəm/ |
| have | /hæv/ | /həv/, /əv/, /v/ |
| is | /ɪz/ | /z/, /s/ |
| some | /sʌm/ | /səm/ |
| an | /æn/ | /ən/ |
| a | /eɪ/ | /ə/ |
| the | /ðiː/ | /ðə/, /ðɪ/ |
| could | /kʊd/ | /kəd/ |
| can | /kæn/ | /kən/ |
| must | /mʌst/ | /məst/ |

*I can come* → /aɪ kən ˈkʌm/, not /aɪ ˈkæn ˈkʌm/. The strong form appears when the word is **stressed** for emphasis or contrast: "Yes, I **can**!"

## Conclusion

The chapter examined the suprasegmentals of English — **stress, intonation and rhythm** — and their domain, the **syllable**, along with the role of **pitch**. Second-language users must make a **conscious effort** to master them: importing "equivalents" from indigenous languages does not work, because there is **no one-to-one correspondence**. **Constant exposure to colloquial English** and a **good dictionary** will help.

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. What are the suprasegmental features of English? Define the syllable and describe its structure.">
Suprasegmentals are features above the individual segment: the syllable, stress, intonation and rhythm. The syllable is the smallest pronounceable unit (unlike the morpheme, the smallest meaningful unit). It has an optional onset (consonants before the vowel), an obligatory nucleus or peak (usually a vowel) and an optional coda (consonants after it). English allows up to three onset consonants and four coda consonants; /m n l/ can be syllabic at the end of a word in an unstressed syllable (bottle, Britain, rhythm).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. What is stress? Explain the four cues of prominence and the degrees of stress.">
Stress is the prominence given to one syllable of a word. A stressed syllable is louder, longer, higher in pitch and has a stronger (full) vowel — schwa never occurs in a stressed syllable. The speaker uses more muscular energy; the hearer perceives more prominence. Primary stress (the most prominent, marked ˈ above and before) is followed by secondary stress (ˌ below and before) and tertiary stress (unmarked). English stress is free, not fixed as in French.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Explain, with examples, how suffixes affect stress placement.">
Some suffixes take the stress (-ee refuˈgee, -eer mountainˈeer, -aire millionˈaire, -ese Chiˈnese, -ette cigaˈrette, -esque pictuˈresque). Some leave it unchanged (-ing ˈwriting, -ish ˈchildish, -able comˈmendable, -ful ˈcareful, -age ˈstorage, -al reˈfusal, -en ˈwiden, -ment comˈmitment, -ance enˈdurance). Others shift it to the syllable before the suffix (-graphy phoˈtography, -ion educaˈtion, -eous couˈrageous, -ity responsiˈbility, -ic symˈbolic, -ial inˈdustrial, -ian hisˈtorian).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Describe the structure of the tone-unit and the four functions of intonation.">
Tone-unit: (pre-head) (head) tonic syllable (tail). Only the tonic syllable is obligatory; it carries the tone and the nuclear stress, usually on the last lexical word. Functions: grammatical (marking boundaries, sentence type — rise for yes/no questions, fall for wh-questions — and disambiguation by moving the boundary); attitudinal (rise for questions, lists, encouragement; fall for finality; rise-fall for surprise; fall-rise for uncertainty and polite requests); accentual or contrastive (tonic stress on the word being contrasted); discourse (marking new versus given information).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="5. Explain rhythm, distinguishing stress-timed from syllable-timed languages, and the role of weak forms.">
Rhythm is the recurrence of phonetic events at roughly equal intervals. In syllable-timed languages (Yoruba, Igbo) every syllable takes roughly equal time; in stress-timed languages (English) the stressed syllables recur at roughly equal intervals and the unstressed syllables between them are compressed. The unit is the foot (a stressed syllable plus the following unstressed ones). To keep the rhythm, function words in unstressed positions take weak forms: to /tə/, have /əv/, can /kən/, the /ðə/.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Suprasegmentals** = syllable, stress, intonation, rhythm (above the segment).
- **Syllable** = the smallest pronounceable unit: onset + **nucleus** (obligatory) + coda. Syllabic /m n l/.
- **Stress** cues: loudness, length, pitch, vowel quality — **schwa is never stressed**. English stress is **free**. Degrees: primary ˈ, secondary ˌ, tertiary.
- **Suffixes:** take the stress (-ee, -eer, -aire, -ese, -ette, -esque); leave it (-ing, -ish, -able, -ful, -age, -ment); shift it (-graphy, -ion, -ic, -ity, -ian, -ial).
- **Word-class pairs:** ˈnoun / verˈb (ˈrecord / reˈcord). **Compounds:** ˈnoun-noun; adverbs downˈstream; number-first first-ˈclass; adverb-verb overˈlook.
- **Intonation:** rise, fall, rise-fall, fall-rise. **Tone-unit:** (PH)(H) **TS** (T). **Functions:** grammatical, attitudinal, accentual, discourse.
- **Rhythm:** English is **stress-timed** (feet); Yoruba and Igbo are **syllable-timed**. Unstressed function words take **weak forms**.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Past papers ask you to **mark the stressed syllable** (in-SAN-i-ty, cel-e-BRA-tion, LIT-e-ra-ture, con-TEMPT-i-ble, TES-ti-mo-ny, de-PART-ment, po-ly-syl-LAB-ic). Practise the **suffix rules** — they solve most of these.
- Learn the **word-class pairs** and the **four tunes with one use each**.
- Know the **tone-unit parts** and which one is **obligatory**.
- Explain **why Nigerian English** often sounds different: tone languages, syllable-timing, and the absence of weak forms.
