## Writing Is Learned

Writing is an **expressive (productive) communication skill**: we share experiences, feelings and thoughts through **mutually understandable printed codes**. Unlike speaking, which we acquire naturally, writing — like reading — must be **deliberately learned**.

> "A sure way to wreck your chances of learning how to write competently is to believe that writing is a **'natural gift' rather than a learned skill**." — Langan (2003)

So develop a **positive attitude**, believe you can write well, and **practise constantly**.

---

## Writing Is a Process

Writing grows out of our **thoughts**, which come from what we **hear, read, observe and share**. It is **not linear**: Soles (2010) calls it a **recursive** process that "is not neat and well-organised" — you move back and forth. There are **three stages**:

<FlowDiagram title="The writing process">
<FlowStep title="1. Pre-writing (planning)">
Determine the **subject and purpose** → analyse the **audience** → gather **materials** → prepare an **outline**.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="2. Writing (drafting)">
Write the **introduction**, the **body** (paragraphs) and the **conclusion**, following the conventions of the genre.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="3. Post-writing (revising)">
**Read over** the draft as a reader, **revise**, then **proofread** — without over-editing.
</FlowStep>
</FlowDiagram>

## Pre-writing Tasks

### 1. Determine the subject and purpose

Without an agenda **you will ramble**. In exams you will meet two kinds of questions:

| **Guided question** | **Unguided question** |
|---|---|
| The examiner **names the subject in specific terms** — an easier task | The examiner gives only a **broad scope**, so **you** must narrow it — "perhaps the most terrifying problem in writing" |

**Narrowing a broad topic.** "**Education in Nigeria**" is too broad. Narrow it to, for example: *problems confronting education in Nigeria*; *the structure of the Nigerian education system*; *university versus polytechnic education*; *problems confronting university education in Nigeria*.

**When you choose your own topic, consider:**

1. **the occasion** — what brought about the need to write?
2. **the audience** — who will read it?
3. **topicality** — how current is the issue?
4. **the time available** — can you do it justice, and research it?
5. **the required length** — is the topic too wide or too narrow for the space?

Test the subject: **Is it significant? Is it interesting? Is it manageable?**

**Purpose.** Don't write merely to "satisfy the examiner". Decide what the writing must **do**: **explain (expose), compare or contrast, persuade, narrate or describe**. Use the journalist's **5Ws + H** — **who, what, where, when, why, how**. Try **freewriting**: jot down every idea in rough form, then organise later.

### 2. Analyse the audience

Writing without an audience in mind is "like **a hunter pulling the trigger without sighting the target**". Analyse your readers':

- **demographic** traits — age, education, religion, occupation, economic status, sex, socio-cultural group;
- **psychographic** traits — knowledge, expectations, prejudices, emotions, assumptions, values, attitudes.

**In an exam**, imagine a group of readers and **read your work as one of them**: if it appeals to you, it will appeal to them. The examiner is **not** your only audience.

### 3. Gather materials

| **Primary sources** | **Secondary sources** |
|---|---|
| **Personal experience, direct observation, interviews, questionnaires, personal files and diaries** | **Library materials** — books, magazines, journals, newspapers, **online resources**, public documents |

The categories are: what the writer **knew** (memory), **saw** (observation) and **learned from others** (research). In exams you rely on **past experience** — so **read widely**: "there is a strong correlation between **passionate reading** and effective writing."

### 4. Outline

Your materials are "a jumble of meaningless information" until you **sort and structure** them. This is **outlining**: using **logical symbols** to show the **importance of ideas and the relationships among them** (Panes 1972). An outline is the **bare bones** you will cover with flesh. It is **tentative**, so adjust it as you write.

| Symbol | Rank | What it marks |
|---|---|---|
| **I, II, III** (Roman numerals) | 1st | **Main ideas / main topics** |
| **A, B, C** (capital letters) | 2nd | **Sub-topics / major details** |
| **1, 2, 3** (Arabic numerals) | 3rd | **Sub-subtopics / minor details** |
| **a, b, c** (small letters) | 4th | Further details |
| **1), 2)** and **a), b)** | 5th and 6th | Finer details |

**The coursebook's example** — an expository essay on *Causes and Impact of Incessant Industrial Disputes in Nigerian Universities*:

> **I. Sheer neglect**<br />
> &nbsp;&nbsp;**A.** Education is not seen as a priority sector by government<br />
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**1.** More resources go to political beneficiaries<br />
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**2.** Budgetary allocation to education is far below the UNESCO recommendation<br />
> &nbsp;&nbsp;**B.** Critical facilities are not provided<br />
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**1.** Inadequate research grants<br />
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**2.** Inadequate and obsolete teaching and laboratory facilities<br />
> **II. Insincerity**<br />
> &nbsp;&nbsp;**A.** Government enters into unrealistic agreements<br />
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**1.** Much of the agreements are political deception<br />
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**2.** Negotiations drag on (delay tactics)<br />
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**a.** Irrelevant committees are set up<br />
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**b.** Committee members are frequently replaced<br />
> &nbsp;&nbsp;**B.** Government frequently dishonours agreements it freely entered into

You may choose other symbols, but **be consistent**.

```sort
@buckets I (main idea) | A (major detail) | 1 (minor detail)
Causes of corruption in Nigeria => I (main idea) :: A main division of the essay.
Poor remuneration of public servants => A (major detail) :: A specific cause under the main idea.
Many civil servants earn below a living wage => 1 (minor detail) :: Evidence supporting "poor remuneration".
Solutions to corruption in Nigeria => I (main idea) :: The second main division.
Strengthen anti-corruption agencies => A (major detail) :: A specific solution.

Weak enforcement of laws => A (major detail) :: Another cause under "Causes".
Few high-profile offenders are ever convicted => 1 (minor detail) :: Evidence for weak enforcement.
```

</SortingGame>

## Drafting: Introduction, Body and Conclusion

### The introduction

The introduction is **the smallest but a very important part**. It is the **signpost** that gives direction and forms the reader's **first impression**, "like a drop of bile in several pounds of flesh". The coursebook describes five approaches:

| Approach | How it works | Opening on *Security in Nigeria* |
|---|---|---|
| **Question** | Key points become **rhetorical questions**; the body answers them. Good for **expository** essays | "…Why is the country no longer secure? Who are the people behind the security problem? How can the conflict be managed?" |
| **Statement** | The theses are **directly asserted** | "…Efforts have been made to identify the reasons behind the insecurity… strategies have been devised…" |
| **Definition** | Starts from the **definition of a key concept** — useful when it is technical or has several meanings | "Insecurity may be defined as…" |
| **Quotation** | Opens with an **authoritative quotation**; it lends **credibility** | "In the words of Chief Obafemi Awolowo, 'the greatest glory is not in never falling, but in rising each time you fall'." |
| **Anecdotal** | Tells a **short, relevant story** that illustrates the point | A brief account of a traveller's ordeal on an unsafe highway |

### The body

The **body** — the paragraphs between introduction and conclusion — has **no fixed number of paragraphs**; the **scope** decides. If asked for **three causes and three solutions** of corruption, write **at least six** body paragraphs. If asked for five needs of your community, write **at least five**.

### The conclusion

The conclusion is "**as important as the introduction**" — many readers judge an essay by it. It may offer **suggestions, solutions or recommendations**, **review the main points** (without repeating them word for word) or end with an **anecdote**.

<ExamTip>
**Never end with "Conclusively".** It does **not** mean "in conclusion"; it means **decisively** or **beyond doubt**. Use **in conclusion, to conclude, in summary, to summarise, all in all, all things considered, by and large, in the final analysis, in the long run, briefly, therefore, thus, as a result, consequently**.
</ExamTip>

## Post-writing Tasks

1. **Read over the draft as a reader**, not as its writer — otherwise you read what is **in your mind**, not what is on the page.
2. **Proofread** — "checking the final, edited draft… for typos and other careless errors" (Langan). **Read backwards, from the last sentence to the first**, so the flow does not carry you past small errors. Check **grammar, mechanics, omissions, irrelevant facts, wordiness, non-parallel sentences, and dangling or misplaced modifiers**.
3. **Don't over-edit:** "sometimes additional revision actually destroys good writing" (Trimmer and McCrimmon).

### Writing skills you need

- **Linguistic skills:**
  - **vocabulary**, including the **registers** of different fields;
  - **sentence fluency and variety** — simple, compound, complex and compound-complex sentences, and active and passive voice;
  - **punctuation and spelling** — keep a dictionary close;
  - the right **style**: **informal** (contractions, abbreviations) for personal letters; **formal** for official and technical writing.
- **Rhetorical skills:**
  - the correct **format (layout)** for each form — letter, essay, report, minutes, memo, circular, news story;
  - clear, **logical organisation**, which depends on good **paragraphs**.

## The Paragraph

> A **paragraph** is **a collection of related sentences that discusses one main idea**.

The **topic (main) sentence** states the main idea; the **supporting sentences** explain, illustrate and prove it. A good paragraph has three qualities:

| Quality | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Unity** | It discusses **only one main idea**. Every sentence serves it; two main ideas = a poor paragraph |
| **Coherence** | Sentences are arranged **logically**, so the relationships between them are clear |
| **Completeness** | The main idea is **fully discussed** within the paragraph, not carried into the next |

**Signal (transition) words** show the relationships between sentences and between paragraphs.

<Reveal question="Unity check: which sentence breaks the unity of this paragraph? 'Regular exercise improves health in several ways. It strengthens the heart and lungs. It helps control body weight. My cousin plays football for a club in Lagos. Exercise also reduces stress and improves sleep.'">
**"My cousin plays football for a club in Lagos."** It is about the writer's cousin, not about how exercise improves health. Remove it to restore **unity**.
</Reveal>

### Seven paragraph types

| Group | Type | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Paragraphs that organise the writing** | **Introductory** | **Previews** the whole composition and contains the **thesis statement**; it may open with a question, definition, controversial statement or anecdote |
| | **Transitional** | In the **middle**: discusses the points previewed, and **reviews and previews**. Uses transitions (*first, second, furthermore, additionally, in addition*). It can also close one section and announce the next ("In this section we have examined… we shall now examine…") |
| | **Concluding** | The **final** paragraph — **restates or reviews** the points discussed and impresses the key issues on the reader |
| **Paragraphs that expand the discourse** | **Defining** | Gives the **meaning** (connotation and denotation) of a word or concept, by direct definition, naming or description |
| | **Expository (explanatory)** | **Informs, explains or educates** about a person, subject, phenomenon or issue |
| | **Narrative** | Tells events in **chronological order**, usually in the **past tense**, with **characters, actions, setting and time** |
| | **Descriptive** | **Paints a picture** in words that appeal to the **senses** — of a person, place, object or process (e.g. Achebe's description of **Unoka** in *Things Fall Apart*) |

<SortingGame title="Which paragraph type?">

```sort
@buckets Introductory | Transitional | Concluding | Defining | Expository | Narrative | Descriptive
"This chapter will focus on development journalism and make only passing references to development as a concept." => Introductory :: It previews the discourse and states the thesis.
"In this section we have examined verbal and non-verbal communication; we shall now examine language." => Transitional :: It closes one section and introduces the next.
"In conclusion, scholarly writing in the social sciences is both a technique and an art…" => Concluding :: It sums up the discussion.
"Reading is the process of communication through which most formal learning takes place…" => Defining :: It gives the meaning of "reading".
"The advertising market in Africa faces a number of challenges, the most significant being the lack of research data…" => Expository :: It explains an issue to increase the reader's knowledge.
"I was among those chosen to represent the Rivers people… We meandered through the heavily-guarded gates into the lounge." => Narrative :: Past events in order, with people, place and time.
"He was tall but very thin and had a slight stoop. He wore a haggard and mournful look…" => Descriptive :: A word-picture of Unoka.
```

</SortingGame>

## Thought-Flow (Paragraph) Patterns

**Thought-flow patterns** are ways of **organising and developing ideas within paragraphs**. A paragraph may combine patterns, but one usually **predominates**. The choice depends on the paragraph's **purpose** and the **subject**.

| Pattern | What it does | Common signal words |
|---|---|---|
| **Whole-part** (listing, **enumeration**) | States a **general topic or whole**, then lists its **parts or supporting points** — e.g. the amoeba and its features | *first, second, also, another, in addition, finally, the following* |
| **Cause-effect** | Shows **causality**: how one thing causes or leads to another ("why", "what", "how") | *because, since, due to, as a result of, therefore, hence, consequently, subsequently, thus, if… then* |
| **Problem-solution** | States a **problem** (sometimes as a question) and gives a **solution**, stated or implied. Common in **scientific, instructional and argumentative** writing — e.g. why electrocution is fatal and how to avoid it | *analyse, examine, explore, outline, describe, demonstrate, evaluate, the problem is, the solution* |
| **Comparison-contrast** | Presents **similarities** (comparison) and **differences** (contrast) | **Comparison:** *likewise, similarly, in the same way, as well as, both*. **Contrast:** *however, whereas, while, on the other hand, in contrast, conversely, unlike, nevertheless, notwithstanding, despite* |
| **Sequence** (**time order**) | Presents events or steps **in order** — narratives, history, experiments, manuals, recipes | *first, next, then, later, after that, before, earlier, meanwhile, at last, finally, thereafter, simultaneously* |
| **Generalisation** | States a **principle, theory or law**, then supports it with examples or explanation | *in general, generally, as a rule, for example, for instance, thus* |
| **Analogy** | Explains something **by likening it** to something more familiar, often **metaphorically**. It looks like comparison-contrast but aims to **illuminate or persuade** | *like, just as, similarly, in the same way* |

**Four ways to organise comparison-contrast** (Summerset 2010):

1. **compare, then contrast** — this ends with the differences, which stay in the reader's mind;
2. **describe one subject, then the other**;
3. **discuss specific elements one at a time** across both subjects;
4. **only compare, or only contrast**, depending on the purpose.

*(A correction to the coursebook's analogy example. The passage — "our temple is built, not upon shifting sands or quagmires, but upon the rock… Courts and magistrates may be set up but they cannot function without sheriffs and constables" — is from **Winston Churchill's 1946 "Sinews of Peace" speech**. The "temple" is the **United Nations' structure for world peace**, not "the American nation", as the coursebook says.)*

<SortingGame title="Which thought-flow pattern?">

```sort

The amoeba is the simplest animal. It has the following features: it is sensitive to stimuli, it moves by pseudopodia, it reproduces by binary fission… => Whole-part :: A whole, then its parts or features listed.
Because machines replaced production workers, middle-level jobs disappeared and wages fell. => Cause-effect :: Causes and their consequences.
Why is electrocution so often fatal? Current flows through the body to earth; to stay safe, keep electrical switches away from wet hands. => Problem-solution :: A problem posed as a question, then a solution.
A free morpheme can stand on its own; in contrast, a bound morpheme cannot. => Comparison-contrast :: The differences between two concepts.
First wash the beans; next soak them for an hour; then grind them and fry the paste in hot oil. => Sequence :: Steps in time order — a recipe.
All living things need energy. For example, plants convert sunlight into chemical energy, and animals obtain energy from food. => Generalisation :: A principle stated, then supported by examples.
A university is like a living tree: its roots are the students, its trunk the staff, and its fruits the graduates it gives to society. => Analogy :: Explains one thing by likening it to another.
The drums reached fervent heat; then the guns fired the last salute; moments later a cry of agony rose from the crowd. => Sequence :: Events in time order (from Things Fall Apart).
```

</SortingGame>

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. Describe the pre-writing tasks a writer should perform.">
Determine the subject and purpose: narrow broad or unguided topics (e.g. "Education in Nigeria" → "Problems confronting university education in Nigeria"); consider the occasion, audience, topicality, time and length; test whether the subject is significant, interesting and manageable; fix the purpose (explain, compare, persuade, narrate, describe) using the 5Ws + H; freewrite. Analyse the audience's demographic and psychographic traits (in exams, imagine readers). Gather materials from primary sources (experience, observation, interviews) and secondary sources (books, journals, newspapers, online). Then outline — rank the ideas with Roman numerals, capital letters, Arabic numerals and small letters to form a tentative guide.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Explain five approaches to writing an introduction.">
Question approach — key points as rhetorical questions that the body answers. Statement approach — the theses asserted directly. Definition approach — begins by defining a key or technical concept. Quotation approach — opens with an authoritative quotation (e.g. Awolowo on rising after falling), which adds credibility. Anecdotal approach — a short, relevant story illustrating the point. Choose the one that suits your purpose and topic.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. What is a paragraph? Explain unity, coherence and completeness.">
A paragraph is a group of related sentences discussing one main idea, stated in the topic sentence and developed by supporting sentences. Unity: only one main idea, with every sentence serving it. Coherence: sentences arranged in a logical order with clear relationships, often signalled by transition words. Completeness: the main idea is fully discussed in that paragraph and not carried over into another.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Discuss four thought-flow patterns with their signal words.">
Whole-part (enumeration): a whole followed by its parts — first, also, in addition, finally. Cause-effect: why and how one thing leads to another — because, due to, as a result, therefore, hence. Problem-solution: a problem, often as a question, then a solution — the problem is, examine, evaluate. Comparison-contrast: similarities and differences — likewise, similarly / however, whereas, on the other hand. Sequence: events or steps in time order — first, next, then, after that, finally. Generalisation: a principle and supporting examples — in general, for example. Analogy: explanation by likeness — like, just as.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- Writing is a **learned** skill and a **recursive process**: **pre-writing → writing → post-writing**.
- **Pre-writing:** subject and purpose (**narrow** unguided topics; significant, interesting, manageable; **5Ws + H**; freewriting); **audience** (demographic and psychographic); **materials** (primary and secondary); **outline** (**I → A → 1 → a**).
- **Introductions:** **question, statement, definition, quotation, anecdote**. The **body** has as many paragraphs as the scope needs. **Conclusions:** suggestions, review, anecdote — **never "conclusively"**.
- **Post-writing:** read as a reader; **proofread backwards**; don't over-edit.
- **Paragraph** = one main idea: topic sentence + supporting sentences; **unity, coherence, completeness**.
- **Types:** introductory, transitional, concluding (organise); defining, expository, narrative, descriptive (expand).
- **Thought-flow patterns:** whole-part, cause-effect, problem-solution, comparison-contrast, sequence, generalisation, analogy — each with its **signal words**.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Expect questions asking you to **outline an essay** on a given topic — use the **I / A / 1** symbols **consistently**.
- Be ready to **name the paragraph type or thought-flow pattern** of a given passage, and to **list signal words** for a pattern.
- In every essay, **narrow the topic**, state your **thesis** in the introduction, give **one idea per paragraph**, and **proofread**.
