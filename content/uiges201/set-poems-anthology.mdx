## The Eight Set Poems

The **2025/2026 GES 201 reading list** contains eight poems. Read each one in your **course pack** before using this lesson. Poems still in copyright are quoted here only briefly.

| # | Poem | Poet | Origin | Form | Central concern |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "I am a Feminist, Not Mere Ash" | **Letlhogonolo Swaratlhe** | Contemporary African | Free verse with **dialogue** | A woman refuses to shed her **feminism** |
| 2 | "Servant-kings" | **R. E. G. Armattoe** | Ghanaian (1913–1953) | Short free-verse lyric with **refrain** | African leaders who **serve foreigners** and fight their own people |
| 3 | "If We Must Die" | **Claude McKay** | Jamaican-American (1889–1948) | **Shakespearean sonnet** | **Resistance** to oppression — die fighting, with honour |

### "I am a Feminist, Not Mere Ash" — Letlhogonolo Swaratlhe

**Subject matter.** The poem opens with an **epigraph**: "…you had the kind of hands that moulds women into sad plots…". The persona says some households ask you to **leave your shoes at the front door** — and **her friend's cousin asked her to leave her feminism** there too. She refuses: her knees "no longer knew how to bow down" to **the myth that women are born "mere ash" and men "the relentless raging fires"**. A sharp **dialogue** follows. He says she is "the kind of woman that men run away from"; she replies she will **pray for men**; he insults her ("Bitch"); she answers "**Luke 23:34**" — Jesus's prayer on the cross, "**Father, forgive them for they do not know what they are doing.**" The poem ends with two prayers in the same pattern: forgive them "for they do not see that they have made **warm homes out of prisons**" and "that it is the **invisible shackles** that are the deadliest".

**Themes.**

- **Patriarchy and its myths** — the belief that women are passive "ash" and men powerful "fire".
- **Female wholeness and self-respect** — "I am whole… so I won't be leaving pieces of myself out on any doorstep."
- **Invisible oppression** — custom and tradition ("warm homes") can be prisons; the **"invisible shackles"** of belief are the deadliest.
- **Forgiveness as strength** — she answers insult with a prayer, claiming the moral high ground.

**Devices.**

| Device | Example |
|---|---|
| **Metaphor** | women as "**mere ash**", men as "**relentless raging fires**"; leaving feminism like **shoes at the door**; "**warm homes out of prisons**"; "**invisible shackles**" |
| **Allusion** (biblical) | "**Luke 23:34**" — Christ's words of forgiveness at the crucifixion |
| **Dialogue / drama** | The exchange of quoted lines makes the poem a small **scene** |
| **Anaphora** | "**Father, forgive them for they do not…**" repeated in the last two lines |
| **Irony** | He calls her a name; she prays for him. The "warm homes" are **prisons** |
| **Epigraph** | The opening quotation sets the theme of women being "moulded" |
| **Tone** | **Defiant, confident**, with **sarcastic** wit, ending in **calm moral strength** |

</Tab>
<Tab label="2. Servant-kings">

### "Servant-kings" — R. E. G. Armattoe

**The poet.** **Raphael Ernest Grail Armattoe** (1913–1953) was a **Ghanaian physician, scientist and poet**, an Ewe from the Gold Coast–Togoland region.

**Subject matter.** In short, clipped lines the persona says "**Leave them alone**" — let these **"Servant kinglets"** remain "**Men lost to shame, / To honour lost!**". They are **"Riding to war / Against their own"**, while their **foes** watch, "**urge them on, / And laugh at them!**".

**Interpretation.** The **"servant-kings"** are African rulers who appear powerful but are really **servants of foreign masters** — the colonial strategy of **divide and rule**. They wage war on **their own people** while the outsiders who benefit **encourage and mock** them. The poem applies just as well to **post-independence leaders** who serve foreign interests and turn on their citizens.

**Themes.** **Betrayal** of one's people; **loss of honour and shame**; **neo-colonial manipulation**; **self-destruction** of Africans by Africans.

**Devices.**

| Device | Example |
|---|---|
| **Oxymoron / paradox** | The title "**Servant-kings**" (and "**Servant kinglets**") — rulers who are servants |
| **Diminutive (contempt)** | "**kinglets**" — little, petty kings |
| **Refrain / repetition** | "**Leave them alone**" opens and nearly closes the poem; "**Men lost to shame, / To honour lost**" returns at the end |
| **Chiasmus** | "Men **lost** to **shame**, / To **honour lost**" — the order is reversed |
| **Irony** | They "ride to war" bravely — against **their own** — while their foes laugh |
| **Short lines** | Two to four words each: terse, scornful, like a **dismissive verdict** |
| **Tone** | **Contempt, disgust and sorrow** |

</Tab>
<Tab label="3. If We Must Die">

### "If We Must Die" — Claude McKay

**The poet and context.** **Claude McKay** (1889–1948), born in **Jamaica**, was a central figure of the **Harlem Renaissance**. Then called the "**New Negro Movement**", it was a rebirth of African-American music, dance, art, literature, theatre and politics. He wrote the poem in **1919**, during the "**Red Summer**" of violent white attacks on black communities in the United States. It became a rallying cry against **anti-black racism**. The GES 201 slides read it as a **Marxist** call to the oppressed to **resist the oppressors**.

> If we must die — let it not be like hogs<br />
> Hunted and penned in an inglorious spot,<br />
> While round us bark the mad and hungry dogs,<br />
> Making their mock at our accursèd lot.<br />
> If we must die — oh, let us nobly die,<br />
> So that our precious blood may not be shed<br />
> In vain; then even the monsters we defy<br />
> Shall be constrained to honor us though dead!<br />
> Oh, Kinsmen! We must meet the common foe!<br />
> Though far outnumbered let us show us brave,<br />
> And for their thousand blows deal one death-blow!<br />
> What though before us lies the open grave?<br />
> Like men we'll face the murderous, cowardly pack,<br />
> Pressed to the wall, dying, but fighting back!

*(The course pack misprints line 7 as "the monsters **thee** defy", drops "**deal one**" from line 11, and writes "Like **the** men" in line 13.)*

**Themes** (from the GES 201 slides).

- **Fear is useless.** Death is inevitable, so there is no point postponing bravery; **honour** matters most — even the "monsters" will be "constrained to honour" those who die bravely.
- **Strength in unity.** The oppressed are strong but disunited: "**Oh, Kinsmen! We must meet the common foe!**"
- **Humans versus animals.** The oppressors are "**mad and hungry dogs**", a "**murderous, cowardly pack**". The oppressed, whom racists call sub-human, are the truly **human** ones ("**Like men** we'll face…").
- **Freedom in resistance.** Words like "**penned**", "**pressed**" and "**constrained**" show submission. The longer the oppressed endure in fear, the longer they stay enslaved, so resist even if freedom is not guaranteed.

**Symbols.** **Hogs** — helpless victims, penned in dirt and slaughtered for nothing. **Dogs** — the **oppressors and mob violence**: feral as a pack, but **cowardly** when resisted.

**Style.** A **Shakespearean sonnet**: three quatrains and a couplet, rhymed **ababcdcdefefgg**, in **iambic pentameter** ("if **WE** must **DIE**, let **IT** not **BE** like **HOGS**").

| Device | Example |
|---|---|
| **Simile** | "let it not be **like hogs**"; "**Like men** we'll face…" |
| **Metaphor** | "the mad and hungry **dogs**"; "the murderous, cowardly **pack**" |
| **Anaphora** | "**If we must die**…" (lines 1 and 5) |
| **Apostrophe** | "**Oh, Kinsmen!**" |
| **Rhetorical question** | "What though before us lies the **open grave**?" |
| **Caesura** | "If we must die **—** let it not be like hogs" |
| **Antithesis / hyperbole** | "for their **thousand** blows deal **one** death-blow" |
| **Oxymoron** | "**murderous, cowardly** pack" |
| **Tone** | **Defiant, urgent, heroic** |

</Tab>
<Tab label="4. Africa">

### "Africa" — David Diop

**The poet.** **David Diop** (1927–1960) was born in **Bordeaux, France**, to a Senegalese father and a Cameroonian mother. He was a poet of **Négritude**, the movement celebrating black African identity against colonialism. He **died in a plane crash** in 1960. "Africa" (*Afrique*) comes from his collection *Coups de pilon* (1956) and is read here **in English translation**.

**Subject matter.** The persona, who has **never seen Africa** ("I have never seen you"), knows it through his **grandmother's songs** of "proud warriors in the ancestral savannahs". His "gaze is full of your blood" — the **blood, sweat, toil and slavery** of Africa's children. He asks: is Africa "**the back that bends**", lying down "under the weight of humbleness", the "**trembling back striped red**" that "**says yes to the sjambok**" (a whip)? **"Solemnly a voice answers"**: Africa is "**that young and sturdy tree**", growing "splendidly alone among **white and faded flowers**". With **patience and stubbornness** it puts forth **new shoots**, and its fruit slowly gains "**the bitter taste of liberty**".

**Themes.**

- **Pride in Africa's past** — the "proud warriors" of the "ancestral savannahs".
- **Exploitation and slavery** — colonial forced labour and the whip.
- **Resilience and hope** — the young tree of **independent Africa**, growing slowly but stubbornly.
- **The cost of freedom** — liberty is precious but "**bitter**", won through suffering.
- **Identity in exile** — a diaspora African who knows Africa through memory and song.

**Devices.**

| Device | Example |
|---|---|
| **Apostrophe** | "**Africa my Africa**", "**Africa, tell me Africa**" |
| **Anadiplosis** (chain repetition) | "The blood of your **sweat** / The **sweat** of your **toil** / The **toil** of **slavery** / The **slavery** of your children" |
| **Rhetorical question** | "Are you the back that bends…?" |
| **Metaphor / symbol** | Africa as "**the back that bends**" and as "**that young and sturdy tree**"; "**white and faded flowers**" = Europe and the colonisers; "**new shoots**" = new nations |
| **Imagery** | Visual and colour images: "**black blood** spilt over the fields", the back "**striped red**" |
| **Paradox / oxymoron** | "**the bitter taste of liberty**" |
| **Repetition** | "puts forth new shoots" (twice) |
| **Structure and tone** | Moves from **nostalgic pride** → **anguish** (questions) → **hope** (the answering voice) |

</Tab>
<Tab label="5. Second Coming">

### "The Second Coming" — W. B. Yeats

**The poet and context.** **William Butler Yeats** (1865–1939), Irish poet and Nobel laureate (1923), wrote the poem in **1919** amid the aftermath of **the First World War**, the **Russian Revolution** and unrest in **Ireland**. Yeats believed history moves in **2,000-year cycles** (he pictured them as **gyres**, widening spirals). The Christian era was ending and a new, violent era was about to begin. **Chinua Achebe** took the title ***Things Fall Apart*** from line 3.

> Turning and turning in the widening gyre<br />
> The falcon cannot hear the falconer;<br />
> Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold;<br />
> Mere anarchy is loosed upon the world,<br />
> The blood-dimmed tide is loosed, and everywhere<br />
> The ceremony of innocence is drowned;<br />
> The best lack all conviction, while the worst<br />
> Are full of passionate intensity.<br />
> <br />
> Surely some revelation is at hand;<br />
> Surely the Second Coming is at hand.<br />
> The Second Coming! Hardly are those words out<br />
> When a vast image out of *Spiritus Mundi*<br />
> Troubles my sight: somewhere in sands of the desert<br />
> A shape with lion body and the head of a man,<br />
> A gaze blank and pitiless as the sun,<br />
> Is moving its slow thighs, while all about it<br />
> Reel shadows of the indignant desert birds.<br />
> The darkness drops again; but now I know<br />
> That twenty centuries of stony sleep<br />
> Were vexed to nightmare by a rocking cradle,<br />
> And what rough beast, its hour come round at last,<br />
> Slouches towards Bethlehem to be born?

*(The course pack misprints "gyre" as "re", "falconer" as "falooner" and *Spiritus Mundi* as "Spiritus Miundi".)*

**Subject matter.** **Stanza 1:** the world is falling into chaos — like a falcon flying so wide it can no longer hear its master. Anarchy and bloodshed are let loose, innocence is drowned, and **good people lack conviction while the wicked are passionate**. **Stanza 2:** the speaker expects a "**revelation**" — the **Second Coming**. Instead he sees a vision from ***Spiritus Mundi*** (the "spirit of the world", a shared store of images): a **sphinx-like beast** with a lion's body and a man's head, moving in the desert. He realises that 2,000 years of Christian history ("**twenty centuries of stony sleep**", disturbed by "**a rocking cradle**" — Christ's birth) have produced a **monstrous new era**: "**what rough beast… / Slouches towards Bethlehem to be born?**"

**Themes.** **Disintegration of order** (political, moral, spiritual); **historical cycles**; **violence and loss of innocence**; **moral inversion** ("the best… the worst"); a **dark prophecy** — not the Christ of hope but a "rough beast".

**Devices.**

| Device | Example |
|---|---|
| **Symbol / metaphor** | The **falcon and falconer** (humanity losing touch with its centre or God); the **gyre** (historical cycle); the **rough beast** (the coming age of violence) |
| **Allusion** | The **Second Coming** of Christ; **Bethlehem**; the **sphinx**; *Spiritus Mundi* |
| **Imagery** | "the **blood-dimmed tide**", "a gaze **blank and pitiless as the sun**" (simile), "**indignant desert birds**" |
| **Antithesis** | "**The best** lack all conviction, while **the worst** / Are full of passionate intensity" |
| **Repetition** | "**Turning and turning**", "**Surely**… **Surely**", "**is loosed**" |
| **Irony** | The expected Second Coming of Christ becomes the birth of a monster **at Bethlehem** |
| **Rhetorical question** | The final line |
| **Form** | Loose **iambic pentameter**, mostly **unrhymed** (blank verse), in **two stanzas** |
| **Tone** | **Apocalyptic, ominous, prophetic** |

</Tab>
<Tab label="6. My Heart Leaps Up">

### "My Heart Leaps Up" — William Wordsworth

**The poet.** **William Wordsworth** (1770–1850) was a leading English **Romantic** poet, co-author with Coleridge of ***Lyrical Ballads*** (1798), and Poet Laureate from 1843. The Romantics prized **nature, emotion, imagination and childhood**. He wrote this poem in **1802**.

> My heart leaps up when I behold<br />
> A rainbow in the sky:<br />
> So was it when my life began;<br />
> So is it now I am a man;<br />
> So be it when I shall grow old,<br />
> Or let me die!<br />
> The Child is father of the Man;<br />
> And I could wish my days to be<br />
> Bound each to each by natural piety.

**Subject matter.** The sight of a **rainbow** still fills the speaker with joy, as it did when he was a **child** and as it does **now he is a man**. He prays it will be so in **old age** — **or let him die**, since life without that wonder is not worth living. He concludes that **"The Child is father of the Man"**: our childhood self shapes the adult we become. He wishes his days to be linked "**each to each by natural piety**" — a **reverence for nature** carried through every stage of life.

**Themes.** **Love of nature**; the **continuity** of the self from childhood to old age; the **value of childhood** wonder; **natural piety** (spiritual reverence for nature).

**Devices.**

| Device | Example |
|---|---|
| **Metaphor / imagery** | "My heart **leaps up**" (joy as a physical leap); the **rainbow** (visual image and a symbol of hope and God's promise) |
| **Paradox** | "**The Child is father of the Man**" |
| **Anaphora** | "**So** was it… / **So** is it… / **So** be it…" — past, present, future |
| **Hyperbole** | "**Or let me die!**" |
| **Rhyme scheme** | **a b c c a b c d d** (behold/sky/began/man/old/die/Man/be/piety) |
| **Metre** | Mostly **iambic**, with line lengths varying from **dimeter** ("Or let me die!") to **pentameter** (the last line) |
| **Tone** | **Joyful, reflective, reverent** |

</Tab>
<Tab label="7. Conclave of Hyenas">

### "The Conclave of Hyenas" — Tayo Olafioye

**Context.** A **satire** on the **looting of Nigeria's national wealth**. The poem names "**Abacha**" — the **military regime of General Sani Abacha (1993–1998)** — and those who served it. A **conclave** is a secret meeting; **hyenas** are scavengers. The poet's own **footnotes** decode the symbols: "**Elephant** — Nigerian national treasury or resources", "**Lagido** — money", "**Ikoko** — wolf", "**Ikeregbe** — the goat".

**Subject matter.** "**They shared the elephant**" — limbs, flesh, carcass and tusks, its heart "gulped in a flash". The plunderers are **foxes** that over-gorge, **dogs** that "barked backwards / Not seeing their fronts", posing as **lions** but really "**wildebeests without tails**". These "**tribal hyenas**" (nicknamed Lagido, Ikoko, Ikeregbe) "**laid the royal bed / That cushioned Ogidan's bones**" (*ogidan* is Yoruba for a **leopard**: the ruler). They were "**chambermaids of horror / In the hierarchy of poli-prostitution**". **Stanza 2** contrasts the past and present: "**Once aliens devastated our shores**" (colonialists). "**Now, the internal antagonists / Are the locusts that ruin the crops**", yet "**They too will die from the heat of the earth.**" **Stanza 3** catalogues the fraud: front companies, cheques "posted mystically", contracts "approved without process", "signed with their toes". It warns, in proverbs, against greed. **Stanza 4:** "these idiots" tested whether the stone "bled water" — "**Now they know / Niger stones shed tears and blood**". The people, made "**comatose**" by "**Abacha's vitriol**", "eat **stone breads** for dinner", while **vultures** ("Voltore" in the course pack) sit on their rooftops. **Close:** "**For as long as we have memories / Yesterday shall remain unforgotten.**"

**Themes.**

- **Corruption and looting of public wealth.**
- **Military dictatorship and complicity** — the courtiers who "cushioned" the tyrant.
- **Internal colonialism** — Nigerians plunder Nigeria as foreigners once did.
- **The people's suffering** — "**stone breads for dinner**", "**tears and blood**".
- **Retribution and memory** — "They too will die"; "Yesterday shall remain unforgotten".

**Devices.**

| Device | Example |
|---|---|
| **Extended animal metaphor** (fable-like) | **Hyenas, foxes, dogs, lions, wildebeests, skunk, locusts, vultures, cats** |
| **Symbol** | the **elephant** = the national treasury |
| **Code-mixing** (Yoruba words) | **Lagido, Ikoko, Ikeregbe, Ogidan** |
| **Neologism / pun** | "**poli-prostitution**" (politics + prostitution); "Voltore" (vulture) |
| **Hyperbole / irony** | "Where possible, they **signed with their toes**"; "**Non-intended** corporations" |
| **Simile** | "**Like dogs** they barked backwards"; "daily **like cats**" |
| **Proverb** | "One should not acquire more / When sharing ill-gotten inheritance"; the cat that climbs may "fall into a ditch" |
| **Oxymoron** | "a **festival of smut**" |
| **Contrast** | "**Once** aliens…" vs "**Now**, the internal antagonists…" |
| **Tone** | **Bitter, scornful, satirical**, ending in **solemn warning** |

</Tab>
<Tab label="8. Strainer">

### "When a Strainer Takes in Water" — trans. A. Olabimtan

**What it is.** A **Yoruba incantation** (*ọfọ̀*) translated by **Afolabi Olabimtan**. Incantations are oral poems recited to make something happen by the **power of the spoken word**. They work through **analogy**: if something is true in nature, let it also be true for me. This one is a charm to **appease anger** and **neutralise enemies**.

**Subject matter and structure.**

1. **Analogies:** water poured into a **strainer** or a **wicker fish-trap** "immediately goes out of it" — so anger should not stay. The **drake cannot crow**, even in anger; **Ọ̀wàwà** (the tree hyrax, known for crying at night) "cannot… **howl from dusk to dawn**". When the **brown monkey meets the Alápinni** (a title in the Egúngún masquerade cult), the monkey "**forgets all its anger**".
2. **Command:** "**Oriji leaf commands you to forgive me**" — in Yoruba, the plant's name echoes *jì*, "to forgive", a typical **pun** of incantations. "A **blacksmith never knows what to forge with iron-dross**" — just as dross is useless, so is their anger.
3. **The wish:** "**Let all those who are annoyed with me… Let them have no power over me. / Let their anger subside.**"

**Themes.** **Appeasement and reconciliation**; **protection** against enemies; the **power of the word** (*àṣẹ*); **nature as moral teacher**; Yoruba **cosmology and oral tradition**.

**Devices.**

| Device | Example |
|---|---|
| **Parallelism / repetition** | "When a strainer takes in water, / All the water immediately goes out of it. / When a wicker fish-trap takes in water, / All the water immediately goes out of it." |
| **Analogy** | Natural facts used as proof of the desired result |
| **Anaphora** | "**Let all those who**…", "**Let** them…", "**Let** their anger subside" |
| **Personification** | The **Oriji leaf commands**; the monkey **forgets its anger** |
| **Pun** (in the Yoruba original) | **Oriji / jì** ("forgive") |
| **Local colour** | Ọ̀wàwà, Alápinni, Oriji, the blacksmith, the fish-trap |
| **Oral features** | Rhythm, repetition and a **commanding, ritual tone** meant to be **chanted** |

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. Discuss the theme of resistance to oppression in 'If We Must Die' and 'Africa'.">
McKay's persona urges his "kinsmen" to resist violent oppressors: if death is certain, die "nobly", fighting back, so that even the "monsters" must honour them; unity and courage turn hunted "hogs" into "men". Diop moves from the image of Africa as a bent back "striped red" by the sjambok, saying yes to oppression, to the answering voice that presents Africa as a "young and sturdy tree" growing with "patience and stubbornness" towards "the bitter taste of liberty". McKay's resistance is immediate and physical; Diop's is slow, patient and assured.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Examine the use of animal imagery in 'The Conclave of Hyenas' and 'If We Must Die'.">
Olafioye builds an extended animal satire: corrupt officials are hyenas, foxes, dogs, wildebeests and locusts devouring the "elephant" (the national treasury). The regime's servants "cushioned Ogidan's" (the leopard's) bones, and vultures sit on the people's roofs. The imagery degrades the looters and exposes their greed. McKay uses animals to invert racist rhetoric: the oppressed refuse to die "like hogs", while the oppressors are "mad and hungry dogs", a "murderous, cowardly pack" — the aggressors are the beasts, the victims "men".
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Comment on 'The Second Coming' as a prophetic poem.">
Yeats depicts a world in collapse — the falcon lost from the falconer, anarchy, the "blood-dimmed tide", the drowning of innocence, and a moral inversion in which "the best lack all conviction, while the worst / Are full of passionate intensity". Expecting the Second Coming, the speaker instead sees from Spiritus Mundi a pitiless sphinx-like "rough beast" that "slouches towards Bethlehem to be born" — a new age of violence ending 2,000 years of Christianity in Yeats's cyclical view of history. The ironic allusion to Christ's birthplace, the stark imagery and the closing rhetorical question give it an apocalyptic, ominous tone.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. How do the African poems in the anthology use oral and local features?">
"When a Strainer Takes in Water" is a translated Yoruba incantation built on parallelism, analogies from nature (strainer, fish-trap, drake, Ọ̀wàwà), local references (Alápinni, Oriji leaf, the blacksmith) and commanding repetition ("Let… let…") meant to be chanted. "The Conclave of Hyenas" mixes in Yoruba words (Lagido, Ikoko, Ikeregbe, Ogidan), proverbs and fable-like animals. "Servant-kings" uses refrain and terse, song-like lines. Diop's "Africa" draws on the grandmother's songs, apostrophe and anadiplosis.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Feminist (Swaratlhe):** patriarchal myths (women as **ash**, men as **fire**); refusal to leave feminism "at the door"; **Luke 23:34**; "**invisible shackles**".
- **Servant-kings (Armattoe):** rulers who serve foreigners and fight their own; oxymoron in the **title**; refrain "**Leave them alone**"; "men lost to shame, to honour lost".
- **If We Must Die (McKay, 1919, Harlem Renaissance):** **Shakespearean sonnet**; hogs vs dogs; unity, honour and resistance; "**fighting back!**"
- **Africa (Diop, Négritude):** apostrophe, **anadiplosis** (blood → sweat → toil → slavery); the **young tree**; "**the bitter taste of liberty**".
- **The Second Coming (Yeats, 1919):** falcon and falconer, **gyre**, "**Things fall apart**", *Spiritus Mundi*, the **rough beast** slouching to Bethlehem.
- **My Heart Leaps Up (Wordsworth, 1802):** the rainbow, "**The Child is father of the Man**", **natural piety**, rhyme scheme abccabcdd.
- **Conclave of Hyenas (Olafioye):** satire on looting under **Abacha**; the **elephant** = national treasury; animal metaphors, Yoruba words, "**Yesterday shall remain unforgotten**".
- **Strainer (trans. Olabimtan):** a Yoruba **incantation** of appeasement; analogy, parallelism, the **Oriji** pun, "**Let their anger subside**".
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Past poetry questions give an **extract** and ask for the **dominant figure of speech, the mood of the persona, the type of imagery and the subject matter**. Practise on each set poem's key lines.
- Learn **one or two short quotations per poem** — they make essays convincing.
- Know which poems are **sonnets** (McKay), **incantations** (Strainer) and **satire** (Conclave), and which are **translations** (Diop, Strainer).
