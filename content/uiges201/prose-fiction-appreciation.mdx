## What Is Prose Fiction?

**Prose fiction is the most popular genre of literature today**, because it captures **human experience in narrative form**. This chapter is by **Charles Tolulope Akinsete**.

- **Prose** comes from the Latin ***prosa***, "straightforward". Prose is **simply narrative**: knowledge passed across **directly, in a denotative form**. Drama and poetry date back to classical times; the shift from **verse to prose** came in the **late Medieval period**.
- **Fiction** loosely means an **untrue statement**. In literature it is an **imaginative work of art**.

> **Prose fiction** is the **story or tale that describes imaginary characters and events** — a literary work of imagination born of the author's creativity.

**Is fiction then a lie?** Its characters may never have existed, but fiction **resembles reality closely** because it draws on **authentic human experience**. **C. J. Hallet (1996):** fiction writers create **"acceptable untruths"** to give meaningful insight into the human condition. So fiction is:

> **"untrue" in the absolute sense, but true in the universal sense.**

### Characteristics and origins

- Its principal feature is **storytelling**. Drama and poetry thrive on **connotative** interpretation; prose is **narrative**, though it can carry **symbolic** meanings. That is why "prose" is often replaced by **"tale", "story" or "novel"**.
- **Two theories of origin:**

| Theory | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **From narrative poetry** | **Epic poems** tell stories around a central **hero or heroine** with unusual traits — ***Sunjata*** (old Mali; transcribed and translated by **D. T. Niane**, 1965) and the ***Mwindo Epic*** of the Congo (edited by **Daniel Biebuyck**, 1977). |
| **From oral tales (folklore)** | **"Folk"** = people sharing the same values; **"lore"** = their knowledge and traditions, **handed down orally**. Forms: **myths, legends, folktales, fairy tales**. The most popular is the **folktale** — **tortoise, spider and hare** stories across West Africa. Novels such as Amos Tutuola's ***The Palm-Wine Drinkard*** and Ben Okri's ***The Famished Road*** still draw on folklore. |

### Functions of prose fiction

1. **Entertainment.** This largely explains its popularity, especially **popular fiction** — mystery, horror, crime, detective and romance. Writers include **Arthur Conan Doyle** (Sherlock Holmes), **James Hadley Chase, John Grisham, Agatha Christie, Nora Roberts, Edgar Allan Poe** and **Robert Ludlum**. In Nigeria, the **Pacesetters Series** included Agbo Areo's *Director!*, Buchi Emecheta's *Naira Power*, David Maillu's *The Equatorial Assignment* and Valentine Alily's *Mark of the Cobra*.
2. **Moral values and the preservation of culture.** Achebe's ***Things Fall Apart*** remains a classic because it exposes readers to **African traditions and values**. Its **proverbs and riddles** show the richness of the **Igbo** language.
3. **Social criticism.** **Literary novels** carry strong **symbolic** meaning and address problems that affect the wider community, sometimes re-presenting historical events:
   - Alan Paton's ***Cry, the Beloved Country*** confronts the **racial structures of South Africa** that hardened into **apartheid**. *(The textbook dates it 1848. It was published in **1948**.)*
   - Ousmane Sembène's ***God's Bits of Wood*** (1960) is about the **rail workers' strike in colonial Senegal** in the 1940s.

## Fiction and Non-fiction

| | **Fiction (prose fiction)** | **Non-fiction** |
|---|---|---|
| **Nature** | Characteristically **imaginative** — settings and characters, even real ones, are **recreated** by the writer | Factual and **biographical** writing about **real life** |
| **But…** | Fiction still **reflects reality**. Emecheta's ***The Joys of Motherhood*** and Adichie's ***Purple Hibiscus*** are fiction, yet **Nnu Ego** and **Kambili** truly represent the pains of many African women whose voices are rarely heard | Non-fiction still uses **imagination**. The author must **improvise** (no one recalls every detail) and may **withhold real names** for ethical reasons |

*(The textbook calls Adichie's novel "The Purple Hibiscus (2006)". The title is ***Purple Hibiscus***, published in **2003**.)*

### Four forms of fiction (by length)

| Form | Length | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Flash fiction** | **Shortest** — about **6 to 700 words** | **Brevity**; a very **simple plot**; one or two (rarely up to five) characters; can grow into a longer work | Edith Pearlman's "Golden Years" (2010). Writers: **Kafka**, H. P. Lovecraft, Kawabata, Cortázar, **Vonnegut**, Fredric Brown, **Hemingway** (about 18 flash pieces in *In Our Time*, 1925) |
| **Short story** | **2,000–7,500 words**, about **5–25 pages** — read **at one sitting** | A simple plot, but **more** major characters, and more complex ones, than flash fiction | **Jomo Kenyatta's "The Gentlemen of the Jungle"** (a key African position on colonialism); Achebe's *Girls at War and Other Stories* (1972); Adichie's *The Thing Around Your Neck* (2009); Segun Afolabi's *A Life Elsewhere* (2006) |
| **Novella** (long short story) | **17,500–50,000 words**, about **60–170 pages** | A **complex plot** — a main plot and **a few sub-plots** — and more developed characters | **Hemingway's *The Old Man and the Sea*** (1952), Conrad's ***Heart of Darkness*** (1899), Thomas Mann's *Death in Venice* (1912), Steinbeck's ***Of Mice and Men*** (1937 — the textbook prints 1973) |
| **Novel** | **Longest** — **50,000+ words**, **170+ pages** | A main plot and **numerous sub-plots**; **many themes** | *Things Fall Apart* (1958), Flora Nwapa's *Efuru* (1966), Okpewho's *The Last Duty* (1976), Okri's *The Famished Road* (1991), Chika Unigwe's *On Black Sisters' Street* (2009), Sefi Atta's *Everything Good Will Come*, Lola Shoneyin's *The Secret Lives of Baba Segi's Wives* (2010), Teju Cole's *Every Day Is for the Thief* (2007) |

*(Beyond the textbook: works of roughly 7,500–17,500 words, between the short story and the novella, are often called **novelettes** — hence the textbook's heading "Novella/Novelette".)*

### Forms of non-fiction

| Form | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Biography** | Greek *bios* (life) + *graphia* (writing): **a person's life written by someone else** | Martin Meredith's *Mandela: A Biography* (1997); Femi Osofisan's *J. P. Clark: A Voyage* (2011) |
| **Autobiography** | **"Self-writing"**: the **author, narrator and subject are the same person**, giving a full life account (often from birth to the present) | Nelson Mandela's ***Long Walk to Freedom*** (1994); Oba Sikiru Adetona's *Awujale* (2010); Olusegun Obasanjo's *My Watch* (2014) |
| **Memoir** | Autobiographical, but covering a **specific period** and emphasising **external events** the author took part in or witnessed | Wole Soyinka's ***Aké: The Years of Childhood*** (1981), ***Ìbàdàn: The Penkelemes Years*** (1994) and ***The Man Died*** (1972); Toyin Falola's *A Mouth Sweeter Than Salt* (2004) |
| **Diary** | From the Latin *diarium*, from *dies* ("day"): a **regular record of the diarist's actions and thoughts**. It flourished in the late **Renaissance** | — |
| **Letters** | A collection of **letters written and received** by the author. ***Personalis*** = a private letter; ***negotialis*** = a letter of public affairs | — |

## Elements of Prose Fiction

### A. Subject matter and B. theme

- **Subject matter** is the **summary of events** — the broad idea at the bedrock of the writer's focus. **Buchi Emecheta's *Second-Class Citizen*:** the story of **Adah Ofili**, a young woman who struggles against all odds for **education and self-sustenance**, first in Nigeria and then in **England**, where things get worse.
- **Theme** (thematic preoccupation) is **what is said about the subject matter** — the central ideas, or the **lessons learnt**. In Kenyatta's "The Gentlemen of the Jungle", **colonialism** is the subject matter and **social injustice and discrimination** are the themes. In Phil Nwoko's *Dancing with the Ostrich*, the themes are **selflessness and mentorship**, as **Benita** rises "from grass to grace" with the support of **Mazi Daniel** and his son **Nonso**.

### C. Plot

**Plot** is the **arrangement of incidents** in a story, traditionally in **cause-and-effect** order; **structure** is the overall design. There are two structures:

| **Linear (organic, chronological)** | **Episodic (inorganic)** |
|---|---|
| Moves **chronologically** from beginning to end: introduction, then conflict, climax and resolution | Begins ***in medias res*** ("in the middle of things"; the textbook writes "in media res"), goes back through **flashback**, then resolves. The story is **less tightly knit** |
| Example: Phil Nwoko's *Dancing with the Ostrich* | Builds **suspense**, so popular-fiction writers favour it |

<FlowDiagram title="The five stages of plot">
<FlowStep title="1. Exposition">
The **beginning**: setting and characters are introduced.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="2. Rising action">
**Complications** arise from the interaction of characters.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="3. Climax">
The **peak** of complications — often the decisive confrontation between **protagonist (hero)** and **antagonist (villain)**.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="4. Falling action">
Events **after the climax**, leading towards the end.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="5. Denouement">
The **resolution** — a revelation or even a **catastrophe**.
</FlowStep>
</FlowDiagram>

### D. Character and characterisation

**Characters** are fictional entities — humans, animals, ideas or traits — that act in the story. **Characterisation** is how the writer **forms and presents** them. There are three ways:

| Method | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Naming** | The simplest: names reflect a character's **attributes, behaviour, physique or traits** |
| **Showing** (the dramatic technique) | Characters **talk and act**, and the reader **infers** their motives |
| **Telling** | The writer **describes** the character's true disposition directly — even features the character is unaware of |

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Protagonist** | The **central character**; the story revolves round them | **Ihuoma** in Elechi Amadi's ***The Concubine*** |
| **Antagonist** | The character whose actions are **always in conflict** with the protagonist | **Jack** in William Golding's ***Lord of the Flies*** |
| **Hero / heroine** | A protagonist whose actions **affect the larger society**; may pay with their life | **Snowball** in George Orwell's ***Animal Farm*** |
| **Villain** (anti-hero) | Works to **destroy** the hero or heroine | **Napoleon** in *Animal Farm* |
| **Eponymous character** | Their **name is the title** | **Oliver Twist** (Dickens) |
| **Flat character** | **Two-dimensional**; does not change; **predictable** | — |
| **Round character** | **Three-dimensional**, like a real person; **changes** physically, emotionally or psychologically, and may surprise us | — |
| **Foil** | Has qualities **opposite** to another character's, highlighting them | In Adichie's ***Purple Hibiscus***: **Kambili** and her cousin **Amaka**; **Jaja** and **Obiora**; **Aunty Ifeoma** and her brother **Eugene** (Papa) |

### E. Point of view and F. setting

- **Narrative point of view** is the **vantage point** from which events are observed and presented: chiefly the **first-person narrator** and the **third-person omniscient** narrator.
- **Setting** is the **physical environment and time** of the story. It can be **spatial** (a real or imagined place) or **temporal** (a season, a time of day, an era). It is the background against which the characters live. It can also be **symbolic or allegorical**, as in Orwell's ***Animal Farm*** and Golding's ***Lord of the Flies***.

## Types of Novel

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Historical fiction** | Set in the **past**, focused on **historical events** | Dickens's *A Tale of Two Cities* (1859); Toni Morrison's *Beloved* (1987) |
| **Mystery fiction** | Built around mysterious or **crime**-related events, often solved by a **detective** | Agatha Christie's *The Mysterious Affair at Styles* (1920); Umberto Eco's *The Name of the Rose* (1980); Dan Brown's *Angels and Demons* (2000) |
| **Picaresque** | The adventures of a **rogue protagonist** from a **low-class background** | Tobias Smollett's *The Adventures of Peregrine Pickle* (1751); Kipling's *Kim* (1901) |
| **Magical realist** | The real world **breaks open** to admit imaginary beings — a novel of **fantasy** | Tutuola's ***The Palm-Wine Drinkard*** (1952); Okri's ***The Famished Road*** (1991); Rushdie's *The Satanic Verses* (1988) |
| **Allegory** | Elements carry **symbolic, metaphorical** meanings | Bunyan's ***The Pilgrim's Progress*** (1678); Golding's *Lord of the Flies* (1954); Plato's "Allegory of the Cave" |
| **Gothic** | **Mystery and death** — ghosts, old buildings, vampires, the supernatural | Walpole's *The Castle of Otranto* (1764); Charlotte Brontë's *Jane Eyre* (1847); Stoker's ***Dracula*** (1897) |
| **Epistolary** | Told through **letters** and documents | Alice Walker's *The Color Purple* (1982); **Mariama Bâ's *So Long a Letter*** (1979) |

*(Small corrections: the textbook writes "Thomas Smollet" — the author is **Tobias Smollett**. It writes "A Mysterious Affair at Styles" — the title is ***The* Mysterious Affair at Styles**. And it dates Plato's "Allegory of the Cave" 2017 — that is a modern edition; Plato wrote it in the ***Republic***, about **375 BC**.)*

## A Model Appreciation: *The Old Man and the Sea*

**Ernest Hemingway** (1899–1961) was one of the twentieth century's most renowned American writers. He was born on **21 July 1899** (the textbook says 22 July) and died on **2 July 1961**, aged 61. His short stories include "Indian Camp", "Soldier's Home", "The End of Something", "Hills Like White Elephants" and "The Killers". His novels include ***The Sun Also Rises*** (1926) and ***For Whom the Bell Tolls*** (1940). His most widely read work, ***The Old Man and the Sea*** (1952), appeared about **nine years before he took his own life**.

**Subject matter (the story).** *The Old Man and the Sea* is a **novella** about **Santiago**, an old Cuban fisherman who has gone **84 days without a catch**. On the **85th day** he sails far out into the **Gulf of Mexico** and hooks a **giant marlin**. He battles it for **three days** before killing it with his **harpoon**. Tied to his boat, the fish's blood attracts **sharks**. Santiago fights them day and night, but by the time he reaches home **only the skeleton remains**. He drags himself to bed, falls asleep and **dreams of lions on the shores of Africa**.

**Themes.**

- **Success is a process, not a result.** Santiago fails to bring the fish home intact, yet his **integrity, determination and hard work** make him a picture of the **indomitable human spirit**. He doesn't complain about hunger, thirst or his cut hands — he washes the blood off in the salty sea. *The quality of a man lies in his actions.*
- **Don't depend on luck.** Santiago is called ***salao***, the worst form of ill luck, and is avoided by everyone except the boy **Manolin**, who cares for him despite his parents' disapproval. Santiago trusts his **ability**, not chance: *"It is better to be lucky. But I would rather be exact. Then when luck comes you are ready."* And: *"I'll bring the luck with me."*

**Achievement.** The novella won Hemingway the **Pulitzer Prize (1953)** and contributed to his **Nobel Prize in Literature (1954)**.

<Analogy>
Santiago is like a student who studies for months for a JAMB score that, through no fault of their own, ends up being withheld. The result is taken away, but the **discipline, skill and courage** gained along the way remain. Hemingway's point is that *how* you fight matters more than what you bring home.
</Analogy>

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. Critically discuss the definition, origin and functions of prose fiction.">
Prose (Latin prosa, "straightforward") is narrative and denotative; fiction is imaginative work. Prose fiction is the story or tale of imaginary characters and events — "untrue in the absolute sense, but true in the universal sense", built from acceptable untruths (Hallet) drawn from real human experience. Origins: from narrative and epic poetry (Sunjata; the Mwindo Epic) or from oral folklore — myths, legends and folktales such as tortoise, spider and hare stories, which still feed novels like The Palm-Wine Drinkard and The Famished Road. Functions: entertainment (popular fiction, the Pacesetters Series), moral teaching and cultural preservation (Things Fall Apart), and social criticism (Cry, the Beloved Country; God's Bits of Wood).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Discuss the categorisation of prose.">
Prose divides into fiction (imaginative, though reflecting reality — Nnu Ego, Kambili) and non-fiction (factual, though needing some imagination). Fiction by length: flash fiction (about 6–700 words), short story (2,000–7,500, read at one sitting), novella (17,500–50,000; The Old Man and the Sea, Heart of Darkness) and novel (50,000+; Things Fall Apart). Non-fiction: biography (Meredith's Mandela), autobiography (Long Walk to Freedom), memoir (Aké, The Man Died), diary, and letters (personalis and negotialis). Novels by type: historical, mystery, picaresque, magical realist, allegorical, Gothic, epistolary.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. What is characterisation? Briefly discuss six types of character.">
Characterisation is the writer's process of forming and presenting characters, by naming (names that reflect traits), showing (the dramatic technique — characters act and speak and the reader infers) and telling (the author describes the character directly). Types: protagonist (Ihuoma in The Concubine); antagonist (Jack in Lord of the Flies); hero or heroine (Snowball in Animal Farm); villain (Napoleon); eponymous character (Oliver Twist); flat character (two-dimensional, predictable); round character (three-dimensional, changing); foil (opposite traits — Kambili and Amaka in Purple Hibiscus).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Using any prose work, explain the concept of thematic preoccupation.">
A thematic preoccupation is what a work says about its subject matter — the central ideas or lessons. In The Old Man and the Sea, the subject matter is an old fisherman's struggle with a marlin and sharks; the themes are that success is a process of integrity and determination rather than an end result, and that one should rely on ability and preparation rather than luck ("I would rather be exact"). In Second-Class Citizen, the subject matter is Adah's struggle for education and survival; themes include gender discrimination and the strength of a woman's will.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Prose** (Latin *prosa*, "straightforward") + **fiction** (imaginative) = stories of imaginary characters and events — **untrue absolutely, true universally**.
- **Origins:** narrative and epic poetry (*Sunjata*, *Mwindo*) or **oral folklore** (tortoise, spider and hare tales).
- **Functions:** entertainment (popular fiction, the Pacesetters), morals and culture (*Things Fall Apart*), social criticism (*Cry, the Beloved Country*, *God's Bits of Wood*).
- **Fiction by length:** flash fiction → short story (2,000–7,500 words) → novella (17,500–50,000) → novel (50,000+). **Non-fiction:** biography, autobiography, memoir, diary, letters.
- **Elements:** subject matter vs theme; plot (**linear** vs **episodic**, *in medias res*; exposition → rising action → climax → falling action → denouement); characterisation (naming, showing, telling) and types (protagonist, antagonist, hero, villain, eponymous, flat, round, foil); point of view; setting (spatial and temporal, sometimes symbolic).
- **Novel types:** historical, mystery, picaresque, magical realist, allegory, Gothic, epistolary.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Know the **word ranges** that separate flash fiction, short story, novella and novel, and an example of each.
- **Subject matter vs theme** comes up again here: give the subject matter of a set text and two themes.
- Learn the **character types** with a named example each. For the set text *Second-Class Citizen*, identify the **protagonist (Adah)** and the characters who oppose her.
- Expect questions on **linear vs episodic plot** and the meaning of ***in medias res***.
