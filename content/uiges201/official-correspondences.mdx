## What Are Correspondences?

**Letters, memos and e-mails** are all **correspondences**: written messages exchanged to get official business done. They differ in **who reads them** (inside or outside the organisation), **format** and **level of formality**.

Before writing any correspondence, ask yourself:
- What is my **purpose** — to inform, request, complain, apply or direct?
- **Who** is my reader — a lecturer, the Head of Department, the Dean, a company?
- In what **capacity** will they read it — official, examination or informal?

Your **choice of words, examples and format** must reflect the answer. Every piece should have **economy, simplicity and clarity**.

## Letters

### Types and purposes

There are **two major categories** of letters, **formal** and **informal**; some scholars add a third, the **semi-formal** letter. They are distinguished by **format and style**, and **who the letter is going to** decides both.

As a student, you may need to write letters to:
- explain why you **missed a test**, or why you must **travel home** mid-semester;
- protest the **non-release of a result** or a **clash in the examination timetable**;
- complain to the **MIS office** that another student has been given **your matriculation number** — "a discovery that … does not call for panic; rather, all you need is a well-crafted letter of complaint";
- apply for a **scholarship, accommodation or employment**.

Letters are **strictly issue-related**. Even bad news (a letter of dismissal, say) should be worded to avoid confrontation: a letter **can be strongly worded but polite**. The coursebook's test of a good letter: **if you seem to hear the writer's voice as you read, it is a good letter.**

### The parts of a formal letter

The coursebook's sample format (Fig. 1) has **eight parts**:

1. the **writer's address**;
2. the **inside address** (the receiver's address);
3. the **salutation**;
4. the **subject line**;
5. the **body**;
6. the **complimentary close**;
7. the **writer's signature**;
8. the **writer's typed name**.

The **date** sits under the writer's address. Tap through the specimen below.

### Rules for each part

| Part | Rule from the coursebook |
|---|---|
| **Writer's address** | **No name** in the address. A company writing without a letterhead may include its name. |
| **Date** | Written **in full** under the address. The coursebook uses the American order *March 4, 2003*; British and Nigerian usage prefers ***4 March 2003*** (or *4th March, 2003*). Avoid *4-03-2003* and *4/3/03*. |
| **Inside address** | On the **left**, below the date. **Official titles** are preferable to names (*The Chairman*, *Dean of Arts*). |
| **Salutation** | Aligned with the inside address. Title plus surname (*Dear Dr Okafor*). Use only common abbreviations (*Mr., Mrs., Ms., Dr.*). **Ms.** does not mean divorced — it simply **does not show marital status**. Unknown receiver: *Dear Sir* / *Dear Madam*. |
| **Subject line** | Optional in letters but **compulsory in memos**. It should be specific, concise and appropriate, and state your purpose and the response you need. |
| **Close** | *Yours faithfully, Yours sincerely, Yours truly* — or, as some recent scholars suggest, simply *Faithfully, Sincerely, Respectfully*. A **comma** follows, and **only the first word is capitalised**. The writer–reader relationship decides the close. |
| **Signature block** | About **four lines below the close**: your signature in ink just above your typed name. |
| **Notations** | Optional, at the **lower left**: initials of writer and typist (*OAE: rh*), file references, **enclosures**, **copies** (cc). |

**Other rules.**
- **Punctuation is mixed or open.** In **mixed** punctuation a mark follows the salutation and the close; in **open** punctuation neither takes one.
- **Be consistent:** use either **blocked** (flush-left) or **indented** paragraphs throughout, with a **double space between paragraphs**.
- **Avoid clichés:** *"please find enclosed"*, *"yours of 22nd April refers…"*.
- **Let your opening paragraph establish your purpose.**

*A note on the coursebook's conventions:*
- It says mixed punctuation puts a **colon** after the salutation (*Dear Sir:*). That is the **American** convention; British and Nigerian practice uses a **comma** (*Dear Sir,*).
- It also insists on a full stop after every title (*Mr., Dr., Mrs.*). British style **drops the stop when the abbreviation ends with the word's last letter** (*Mr, Dr, Mrs*), but keeps it in *Prof.*
- Either style is acceptable — **be consistent**.

### Matching the salutation and the close *(standard practice)*

| Salutation | Close | When |
|---|---|---|
| *Dear Sir, / Dear Madam, / Dear Sir or Madam,* | **Yours faithfully,** | You **don't know the receiver's name** — most formal letters to offices |
| *Dear Mr Bello, / Dear Prof. Adie,* | **Yours sincerely,** | You **address the receiver by name** — formal or semi-formal |
| *Dear Tunde, / Dear Mum,* | *Yours affectionately, Your friend, Love,* | **Informal** letters to family and friends |

### Formal, semi-formal and informal letters compared *(standard practice)*

| Feature | Formal | Semi-formal | Informal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Written to** | An office or official (Registrar, MIS, a company) | Someone you know, writing in an official or respectful capacity (a lecturer, your pastor, a friend's parent) | Family and friends |
| **Addresses** | Writer's **and** receiver's | Writer's address and date; usually **no** inside address | Writer's address and date only |
| **Salutation** | *Dear Sir/Madam* | *Dear Mr Bello / Dear Dr Okafor* | *Dear Tunde* |
| **Subject line** | Yes | Usually no | No |
| **Style** | Formal, no contractions or slang, straight to the point | Polite, warm but respectful | Conversational: contractions, jokes, personal news |
| **Close** | *Yours faithfully,* + signature + **full name** | *Yours sincerely,* + **full name** | *Yours affectionately / Your friend,* + **first name** |

## Memos

A **memorandum** (plural **memoranda**; short form **memo**) is a **short written communication circulated within an organisation**, exchanged by employees in the daily conduct of their work. Memos carry **routine enquiries, routine responses, policy instructions and directives**.

**Letter vs memo.**
- **Letters** go **within and outside** an organisation; **memos** circulate **only within** it.
- Memos may match letters in length, formality and organisation, but they differ in **format**.

Memo rules:
- Memos **omit the salutation and the complimentary close**.
- They **require a subject line** — the title of the document. It:
  - aids **filing**, making retrieval easy;
  - gives the reader **a reason to read further**;
  - provides a **framework or delimitation (scope)** for the message.

  *(The coursebook announces "four purposes" but lists only these three.)*
- **Headings** inside the body are **optional**. Where sub-headings are used, limit each section to one point and exhaust that point.
- **Never indent paragraphs.** The **reader's name, the writer's name and the subject** are **lined up vertically**.
- Leave a one-inch margin at the top and bottom. Use **single spacing within paragraphs and double spacing between them**.
- Write the date **in words** (*July 1, 2003*).
- Prefer a **one-page memo**. If you need more space, put the key points on page one and attach **appendices** rather than lengthening the memo.
- Pre-printed memo forms already show Date / To / From / Subject.
- **Sign with your initials**. This shows the writer wrote and read the memo, and stops anyone sending a memo in another person's name. Where a full signature is required, it comes after the last line, starting halfway across the page.

## Official E-mail and Other New-Media Forms

The **e-mail format is very similar to the memo format**, and it now carries much organisational communication.

| Advantages of e-mail | Why it may not replace letters and memos |
|---|---|
| It is **cost-effective** | It **lacks confidentiality** and is more open to abuse by **fraudsters** |
| It **reduces paper traffic** | It may be **too informal** |
| It acts as **organisational memory** | **Information overload** — many people are copied on the same mail |
| It **reduces geographical constraints** | **Network or power outages** |
| It **increases the quantity of communication** within an organisation | It **lacks visual or aural cues** |
| It **increases the number of communication relationships** | — |

**Netiquette (e-mail etiquette):**
- **Focus on purpose and context.** E-mail suits **specific circumstances**: you cannot terminate someone's appointment by e-mail.
- **E-mail is not private.** Messages can be intercepted, so keep policy matters and company secrets out of it.
- Anything you send **can be used as evidence in court** — every message is **permanent and retrievable**.
- Use a **subject line** to tell the reader what to expect.
- **Don't type in capitals throughout** — it reads as **shouting**.
- **Log off** when you leave your desk; someone could send an incriminating mail from your open account.
- **Check your mailbox often**, and target your message to its proper audience, not your whole mailing list.
- Use **correct spelling, grammar and familiar abbreviations**.

## Minutes of Meetings *(standard practice)*

**Minutes** are the **official written record of a meeting**: who attended, what was discussed and, above all, **what was decided and who is to act**. The **secretary** takes them. At the next meeting they are read, corrected if necessary, **confirmed (adopted) as a true record** and **signed by the chairman and the secretary**. From then on they are evidence of the body's decisions.

**The three documents of a meeting:**

<FlowDiagram title="Notice → Agenda → Minutes">
<FlowStep title="Notice of meeting">
Sent before the meeting: who is meeting, **date, time and venue**.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Agenda">
The **numbered list of items** to be discussed, in order — usually sent with the notice.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Minutes">
Written after the meeting, **following the agenda's order and numbering**, recording decisions and actions.
</FlowStep>
</FlowDiagram>

**How minutes are written:**
- **Reported speech, past tense, third person:** *The Chairman informed members that…*, not *"I want to inform you…"*.
- **Impersonal and objective:** record the substance and the **decision**, not every word, and no opinions of the secretary.
- **Motions:** record who **moved** and who **seconded** them, and the outcome (*carried*, *unanimously adopted*, *defeated*).
- **Action points:** name **who** will do **what** and **by when** — many minutes add an **Action** column.
- **Types:**
  - **verbatim** minutes record every word (courts, legislatures);
  - **narrative** minutes summarise the discussion;
  - **resolution (action)** minutes record only the decisions.

## Reports

### Definitions

A **report** gives an account of, or presents information about, an **occurrence or phenomenon**. It is marked by **organisation and purpose** and is not always tied to business — it may be academic. It usually **begins with a précis or executive summary**, then **analyses** the information and gives **reactions, opinions or recommendations**.

> "An orderly and objective communication of information that serves a business purpose." — **Lesikar et al.**

> "A document in which a given problem is examined for the purpose of conveying information, reporting findings, putting forward ideas and sometimes making recommendations intended to serve as a basis for action." — **Chappel and Read (1974: 138)**. Key words: *document, problem, examined, information, solutions.*

> "A specialized, professional and academic writing, structured and presented to aid the decision making process of the authorizing body." — **Soola (1998)**

Words that recur in these definitions: **structured, orderly, factual, specialised, objective, professional.**

- Reports are usually **initiated by an authorising body**, which intends to apply the findings to its policy or decisions.
- **Formal reports** require **comprehensive research and analysis**.
- Reports vary in **length**, **formality** and **complexity**.

A report may present:
1. **information only**;
2. **information plus analysis**; or
3. **information, analysis and recommendations**.

### Types of report

Examples include **accident, credit, justification, audit, trip and interim** reports. The coursebook groups reports into **three broad categories**:

| Regular and routine | Production / occasional | Commissioned |
|---|---|---|
| **Periodic** reports — weekly, monthly, quarterly, annual | **Disciplinary** reports | **Policy formulation / change** reports |
| **Progress** reports | **Situation** reports | **Consumer research** reports |
| | | **Investigative** reports |
| | | **Feasibility** reports |
| | | **Proposal** reports |

### Questions the report writer must answer

- **Who are my readers** — who will most likely be interested?
- **What do they know**, or think they know, about the subject? Is it from experience, observation, reading or **rumour**?
- **Why will they read it?** If they know a lot, what do they expect to learn? If little, what more?
- **How can I interest them** — and if they are **hostile**, get their attention and make them consider my view?
- **How can I help them enjoy it?** Which **organisational pattern**, and which **signposts and transitions**, will they need?

**Gathering data.**
- Primary and secondary sources: books, journals, encyclopaedias, statistical sources, the Internet.
- **Surveys** (interview, questionnaire) and **direct observation**.
- For an **administrative or judicial panel**, a **call for memoranda** from the public.
- **Evaluate every source** for reliability.

**Reports are called for when:**
1. the subject is **complex and technical**;
2. **quantitative material** or **comparisons** are presented;
3. the information has **implications** that need explaining;
4. the material will be the **basis for further study or decision-making**;
5. the writer must give **recommendations or a plan of action**, with the reasons for them.

### The structure of a formal report *(standard practice)*

```order
Title page :: Title, who it is for, who wrote it, date.
Executive summary :: A précis of the whole report — the coursebook says reports usually begin with one.
Terms of reference :: Who asked for the report, what it must cover, and the deadline.
Procedure / methodology :: How the information was gathered — interviews, questionnaires, observation, documents.
Findings :: The facts, often with tables and charts.
Conclusions :: What the findings mean.
Recommendations :: What should be done, and by whom.
Signature, name and date :: The writer(s) or panel members.
Appendices :: Questionnaires, photographs, raw data.
```

## The Coursebook's Exercises (answered)

<Reveal question="1. Why are subject lines important?">
They tell the reader at once what the document is about. In the coursebook's words, a subject line:
- **aids filing and makes retrieval easy**;
- **gives the reader a reason to read further**;
- **provides a framework or scope** for the message that follows.

Specific details such as invoice, order or matriculation numbers help the reader act quickly.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Mention three conventions (rules) in letter writing.">
Any three of:
- the writer's address **without the writer's name**;
- the **date written in full**, not in figures;
- **official titles** in the inside address;
- the **salutation flush left**;
- a comma after the close, with **only the first word capitalised**;
- the **signature in ink above the typed name**;
- **consistent** blocked or indented paragraphs;
- **mixed or open punctuation** used consistently;
- **no clichés** such as "please find enclosed";
- an opening paragraph that **states your purpose**.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. List the advantages of e-mail, and say when e-mail is not appropriate.">
**Advantages:**
- cost-effective;
- reduces paper traffic;
- serves as organisational memory;
- reduces geographical constraints;
- increases the amount of communication and the number of communication relationships.

**Not appropriate** for confidential matters (policy issues, company secrets), for sensitive actions such as **terminating an appointment**, or wherever a signed, formal record is needed.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. What are reports? Identify three qualities of a good report.">
A report is an **orderly, objective account** of an occurrence or problem. It conveys information and findings and often makes **recommendations as a basis for action** (Chappel and Read). A good report is **structured/orderly, factual, objective**, specialised, professional, and clear about its purpose and readers.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="5. Outline a report on the condition of the restrooms in your hall of residence and its implications for the occupants' well-being.">
**A model outline.**

**Title:** Report on the Condition of Toilets and Bathrooms in Kuti Hall

- **To:** The Hall Warden. **By:** Welfare Committee. **Date:** 20 March 2026.
- **Terms of reference:** At the Warden's request, to inspect all restrooms, assess their condition and recommend improvements, by 20 March 2026.

  2. 14 doors have no locks.
  3. 6 light fittings are broken.

  5. The hall clinic recorded 11 cases of diarrhoea in February.

- **Recommendations:**
  1. Replace the borehole pump within two weeks.
  2. Supply water by tanker in the meantime.
  3. Fix the locks and lights.
  4. Set a daily cleaning schedule, monitored by the Welfare Committee.
- **Signature** (Welfare Secretary), then **Appendices** (questionnaire, photographs).

<Takeaway>
- **Correspondences** — letters, memos, e-mails — must fit their **purpose, reader and format**, with **economy, simplicity and clarity**.
- **Formal letter (8 parts):**
  - writer's address (**no name**) and date in full;
  - inside address (official titles), salutation, subject line, body;
  - complimentary close (comma; first word capitalised), signature, typed name;
  - optional notations (Encl., cc).
  - *Dear Sir* goes with *Yours faithfully*; *Dear Mr Bello* with *Yours sincerely*.
- **Memo:** internal only. **No salutation or close**; a **subject line is compulsory**; To/From/Date/Subject lined up; unindented paragraphs; one page; **initialled**.
- **E-mail:** memo-like; cheap and fast but not confidential. **Netiquette:** subject line, no all-caps, log off, write correctly.
- **Minutes:** the official record — heading, attendance, previous minutes, matters arising, items, AOB, next meeting, adjournment, signatures. **Reported speech, past tense; record decisions and actions.**
- **Report:** an orderly, objective account that serves decision-making. Categories: **routine** (periodic, progress), **occasional** (disciplinary, situation), **commissioned** (policy, consumer research, investigative, feasibility, proposal). Structure: summary, terms of reference, procedure, findings, conclusions, recommendations.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- In a letter question, **marks go to format** as well as content. Include every part, put the **subject line** in, and **match the close to the salutation**.
- **Memos lose marks for salutations and "Yours faithfully".** Use the memo heading and initial it.
- For minutes, practise turning **direct speech into reported speech**, and always name **who moved and seconded** a motion.
- **Check that the day and the date agree** (*Tuesday, 27 March 2012*) — even the coursebook's sample got this wrong.
