## Why Punctuation Matters

**Punctuation** is the **system of symbols** used to **separate sentences and parts of sentences**, and to make writing **clear, meaningful and interesting**. Each symbol is a **punctuation mark**. Punctuation marks are **"traffic signs" for readers**: they represent the **pauses and gestures** we use in speech and guide readers through the network of words and ideas.

A sign on a door read:

> **No Entrance Alarm Will Sound**

People read it as "there is no entrance alarm" — and kept setting it off. Corrected, it read:

> **No Entrance. Alarm Will Sound.**

Two full stops changed the meaning completely. Writers punctuate for **two reasons**: to give **order and structure** to strings of words, and to add **style**.

## The Full Stop (.)

| Use | Examples |
|---|---|
| **End of declarative and imperative sentences** (not exclamations) | *Life is good.* / *Pass the salt.* / *Oil floats on water.* |
| **Requests, indirect questions, imperatives phrased as questions** | *Would you please turn the television down.* / *He wonders who she is.* |
| **Abbreviations and initials** | *W. F. Kumapayi has a Ph.D. in Statistics.* / *Mr., Mrs., Feb., i.e., a.m., p.m., B.C., A.D.* |
| **Ellipsis (…)** — omitted words | *I would have loved to go swimming but…* Three dots for words missing at the start or middle of a quotation; **four** at the end (the fourth is the full stop) |

**Acronyms and initialisms often drop the dots:** **OPEC, NATO** (acronyms, pronounced as words), and **UN, AU, IBB** (pronounced letter by letter). A sentence ending in an abbreviation needs **no extra full stop**: *The relics date back to 800 B.C.* If it is a question, add the question mark: *Did he earn a B.A.?*

*(The coursebook glosses a.m. and p.m. as "ante meridian" and "past meridian". The Latin is ***ante meridiem*** ("before midday") and ***post meridiem*** ("after midday").)*

## The Comma (,)

The comma is a **mark of separation** and a **brief pause**: "During the day, there are numerous activities."

| # | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | After an **adverbial clause that begins** a sentence. **No comma when it ends the sentence** | *Until we have your final report, we cannot decide.* / *We cannot decide until we have your final report.* |
| 2 | After an introductory **participial phrase or infinitive** | *Seeing her again, I felt my heart go pitter-patter.* / *To win this competition, we must trust each other.* **But not after an infinitive that is the subject:** *To drive a Honda would be exhilarating.* |
| 3 | After an introductory **prepositional phrase** (optional) | *After the concert, we stopped for coffee.* = *After the concert we stopped for coffee.* |
| 4 | Around **conjunctive adverbs** | *Consequently, the ship sank.* / *Bunmi said, however, that she could not…* |
| 5 | After **yes / no** at the start | *Yes, I have set the table.* / *No, I will not buy you a car.* |
| 6 | With nouns of **direct address** | *Kazeem, wash those clothes.* / *I told you, Ms Achebe, that we no longer sell milk.* |
| 7 | After **interjections** | *Oh, I didn't expect to see you here.* / *Alas, the man is gone.* |
| 8 | Around **appositives** (one comma if it ends the sentence) | *Titi, the new girl, has secured a room.* / *I hugged my best friend, Habiba.* |
| 9 | Between items in a **series** of three or more (the last comma before *and* is optional) | *We bought rice, seasonings, beans and spaghetti.* |
| 10 | Between **two adjectives** that each describe the noun (when *and* could join them) | *It was a long, tiring lecture.* **But:** *the brave young man* (no comma — *brave* describes "young man") |
| 11 | Before a **coordinating conjunction joining two main clauses** | *I saw the game, but I don't remember the score.* **Not** between two subordinate clauses: *Because time is short and because the matter is urgent, we must act.* |
| 12 | To mark an **omitted word** | *Agaba did the talking, and Akpana, the smiling.* (= "Akpana did the smiling") |
| 13 | Before a **question tag** | *She has beautiful eyes, doesn't she?* |
| 14 | With **quoted speech** | *Ugar said, "Bring me the flash drive."* / *"I am overjoyed," my mother cried.* / *"What," she asked, "is the point of this?"* |
| 15 | In **dates** | *October 14, 2012* — and after the year in mid-sentence: *On November 4, 1985, a miracle occurred.* |
| 16 | In **addresses and places** | *Akanji visited Ikorodu, Lagos, over the weekend.* |
| 17 | To **prevent misreading** | *Before Biwom, Akande was our accountant.* / *The finer the silk, the higher the price.* |
| 18 | Around **non-restrictive** (extra) information, but **not** restrictive (essential) information | *My only sister, Habiba, is coming.* vs *My brother Timmy graduated top of his class* (I have more than one brother) / *The man who gave the information cannot be trusted.* |

<SortingGame title="Comma needed or not?">

```sort
@buckets Comma needed | No comma
Because we are such good friends I am asking you this favour. => Comma needed :: An introductory adverbial clause: "…friends, I am asking…"
I am asking you this favour because we are such good friends. => No comma :: The adverbial clause ends the sentence.
Kazeem wash those clothes. => Comma needed :: Direct address: "Kazeem, wash those clothes."
To eat is desirable. => No comma :: The infinitive is the subject of the sentence.
My only sister Habiba is coming on a visit. => Comma needed :: Non-restrictive appositive: "My only sister, Habiba, is coming…"
The man who gave the information cannot be trusted. => No comma :: A restrictive clause — it tells us which man.
This is the place isn't it? => Comma needed :: A question tag: "This is the place, isn't it?"
That tall lanky girl is my younger sister. => No comma :: "Tall" describes the whole phrase "lanky girl" (the coursebook's example).
Yes I have set the table. => Comma needed :: An introductory yes or no: "Yes, I have set the table."
```

</SortingGame>

## The Colon (:) and Semicolon (;)

| **Colon** — "as follows"; a strong break | **Semicolon** — stronger than a comma, weaker than a full stop |
|---|---|
| **Before a list** at the end of a sentence: *Bring these items: ID card, pencils and erasers.* | **Joins two closely related main clauses** without a conjunction: *The rains are here; my farmer-dad is happy.* |
| **Before an explanation or emphasis**: *There is only one word for the decision: ridiculous.* / *Honesty: the best policy.* | **Before a conjunctive adverb** joining clauses: *There's so much to do; however, there's never enough time.* |
| **Before a long quotation** | **Separates main clauses that already contain commas**: *Mr Soyinka, president of the corporation, has predicted a good year; but his reasoning is not beyond reproach.* |
| **References, ratios and times**: *John 3:16*, *Fasoyin (2010: 53)*, *a 3:5:1 ratio*, *22:34* | **Separates list items that contain commas**: *The guests are Ipeh, my cousin; Ifeyinwa, my best friend; and Kunle, my classmate.* |
| **Titles and subtitles**: *Things Fall Apart: The Centre Holds* | — |
| **Salutation in a formal letter** (American style): *Dear Prof. Adie:* *(British and Nigerian formal letters normally use a **comma**: "Dear Sir,")* | — |

## The Apostrophe (')

**1. Possession.**

- Singular nouns, and plurals **not** ending in s: add **'s** — *dog's, chef's, William's, men's, geese's*.
- Singular nouns **ending in s**: the general rule is to add **'s** — *boss's, Jones's, bus's, class's*. Traditional exceptions are **Jesus'** and **Moses'**, and classical names such as **Socrates'**.
- Plural nouns **ending in s**: add only **'** — *boys', tigers', militias'*. In the expression ***for … sake***, an s-ending noun takes only the apostrophe: *for goodness' sake*.

**2. Contractions (missing letters):** *it's* (it is), *he's*, *we've*, *he'd*, *don't*, *won't*. Missing digits: *'91*, *'60s*.

**3. Plurals of letters and some abbreviations:** *mind your p's and q's*, *the three R's*, *M.D.'s*. But plain **multiple capitals and numerals take just -s**: *IOUs, ABCs, the 1950s, 747s*.

<ExamTip>
**It's or its?** **It's** = *it is* or *it has* (*It's raining*). **Its** = possessive, **with no apostrophe** (*The dog wagged its tail*). Likewise **whose** (possessive) vs **who's** (who is), and **your** vs **you're**. Never use an apostrophe for an ordinary plural: *mangoes for sale*, not "*mango's for sale*".
</ExamTip>

<SortingGame title="Correct or incorrect apostrophe?">

```sort
@buckets Correct | Incorrect
The rooms' interior décor is ugly. => Correct :: A plural possessive (several rooms) takes only the apostrophe.
There's something Miss Sango's fiancé can do. => Correct :: A contraction (there is) and a possessive.
The dog wagged it's tail. => Incorrect :: The possessive is "its" — "it's" means "it is".
Fresh tomatoe's sold here. => Incorrect :: A plain plural has no apostrophe: "tomatoes".
The children's playground is closed. => Correct :: An irregular plural not ending in s takes 's.
My boss's car is new. => Correct :: Singular ending in s: boss's.
The students's hostel was renovated. => Incorrect :: A plural ending in s takes only the apostrophe: students'.
Mind your p's and q's. => Correct :: Plurals of single letters take 's.
```

</SortingGame>

## Question Mark, Exclamation Mark and Brackets

| Mark | Uses | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Question mark (?)** | **Direct questions**, including quoted ones | *Where did you get the money from?* / *The woman asked, 'When will you buy my wares?'* |
| | **Imperatives written as questions** — a question mark or a full stop | *Will you please stop that noise?* (or *.*) |
| | **Uncertainty**, in brackets | *King Jaja of Opobo ruled from 1869(?) to 1887.* |
| **Exclamation mark (!)** | **Strong emotion, emphasis or surprise**; exclamatory sentences, forceful commands and interjections. **Don't overuse it** | *Oh my goodness!* / *Eureka!* / *What a weekend we had!* / *Watch out for the tree!* / *Ouch! That stings.* |
| **Brackets / parentheses ( ) [ ]** | **Extra information** — the sentence still makes sense without it | *The job at hand (for teachers) requires resilience.* / *The child(ren) will eat bread today.* |
| | **Numbers, letters and symbols**; list dividers | *The price per bag (₦8,000) is outrageous.* / *Their plans are (1) to seize the station, (2) to incite the citizens…* |
| | **Abbreviations to be used later** | *The African Union (AU) has given a ceasefire ultimatum.* |

## Hyphen, Dash and Slash

| Mark | Uses | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Hyphen (-)** — joins | **Compound words** | *holier-than-thou*, *been-tos*, *wannabes* |
| | **Compound adjectives before a noun** | *a two-day seminar*, *sweet-smelling flowers*, *a well-planned meeting*, *a fifth-floor apartment*, *a well-known name* |
| | **Prefixes** — to avoid confusion, or before a **proper noun** | *re-employ*; *re-form* (form again) vs *reform* (change); *pre-independence*, *non-Nigerian*, *anti-American* |
| | **Fractions and compound numbers** | *a two-thirds majority*, *twenty-five thousand naira* |
| | **Fixed compounds** | *in-laws* (sister-in-law), *great-* (great-aunt), *-elect* (president-elect), *self-* (self-employed). **Not** *toothache, textbook* |
| | **Word breaks** at the end of a line, at syllable boundaries | *audi- / torium* |
| **Dash (–, —)** — interrupts | **An abrupt change or interruption**; the slides call it a "double hyphen" | *We gave them many gifts — too many to count.* |
| | **Emphatic parenthesis** (stronger than brackets) | *This boy — I think he is foolish — bought a fairly used shirt for ₦10,000.* |
| | **Afterthought**, or to introduce a list | *We may not see each other for a long while — maybe never again.* |
| **Slash (/)** | **Alternatives**, and **sessions and dates** | *Students must submit school receipts and/or ID cards.* / *The 2019/2020 session…* |

## Quotation Marks, Capitals and Italics

### Quotation marks (" " or ' ')

- They enclose **direct speech or quoted words**: *"I love Jesus," the child cried.*
- They mark **titles of short works** — poems, short stories, articles, songs, chapters: *Have you read "Arrows of Rain"?*
- They draw attention to **words used as words**, nicknames and slang: *The word "grammar" has an intriguing history.*
- Use **single quotation marks inside double** ones (or the reverse): *"Kunle said, 'You haven't heard the last of me!'"*
- **Question marks:** inside if only the quotation is a question (*I heard you ask, "When is the match over?"*); outside if the whole sentence is (*Have you seen "24"?*).
- **Commas and full stops:** the coursebook says they **always** go inside the closing quotation mark. That is the **American** rule — and the coursebook's own example *"God", he acknowledged, "is the source of my strength".* breaks it. **British** usage places them inside **only if they belong to the quotation**. Choose one convention and **be consistent**.

### Capital letters

- The **first word of a sentence** and of a **quoted sentence**: *The preacher lamented: "Hilda's goal is…"*
- **Proper nouns** vs common nouns: *Violet / violet, Jack / jack, Bush / bush*.
- **Proper adjectives**: *a Nigerian novelist, German cars*.
- **Titles**: capitalise the **content words** (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs) and the **first word**, but not small grammatical words: *A Concise Introduction to Political Science*. Capitalise the first word of a **subtitle**.
- **Days, festivals and holidays**: *Democracy Day, Boxing Day, Easter Sunday*.
- **Kinship terms used as titles or names**: *Uncle Bode, Auntie Rosa, Mother*. But not after a possessive: *his sisters, Folake's uncle*.
- The pronoun **I**, always.
- **Pronouns referring to God**: *You are He that saved me.*

### Italics, bold and underlining

In typed work they **foreground** words:

- **titles of long works** — books, newspapers, magazines, films, TV programmes: *Half of a Yellow Sun* is by **Chimamanda Adichie**; *Arrow of God*;
- **foreign words**: *The Yoruba word for a child is* omọ;
- **emphasis**.

In handwriting, **underline** titles instead.

## The Coursebook's Post-test (solved)

<Reveal question="1. Punctuate: We don't know if he took his medicine however we hope he did">
**We don't know if he took his medicine; however, we hope he did.** — a semicolon before the conjunctive adverb and a comma after it.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Punctuate: My sister the medical doctor has travelled overseas">
**My sister, the medical doctor, has travelled overseas.** — a non-restrictive appositive is set off by commas. (Without commas, *my sister the medical doctor* would imply you have more than one sister and are identifying which one.)
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Repunctuate this passage (adapted from Abimbola Adelakun's Under the Brown Rusted Roofs): 'the Olobinrin ile were fetching water for the eight day prayer, when Kudi and her daughter Mulika came Kudi greeted the women; who were arguing that they were senior wives for they were married into the agboole before some others. I don't want to know if you are older than I am! One of the women was shouting all I know is that I was married into this agboole before you'">
The *olobinrin ile* were fetching water for the eight-day prayer when Kudi and her daughter, Mulika, came. Kudi greeted the women, who were arguing that they were senior wives, for they were married into the *agboole* before some others.

"I don't want to know if you are older than I am!" one of the women was shouting. "All I know is that I was married into this *agboole* before you."

**What changed:**
- a capital letter to start;
- the comma removed before "when";
- commas around the appositive "Mulika";
- a full stop after "came";
- a comma (not a semicolon) before the non-restrictive "who";
- a comma before "for";
- quotation marks around the direct speech, with a new paragraph for the speaker;
- "one" in lower case after the quotation;
- italics for the Yoruba words.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Punctuate: Iya she said give your son the water of life to drink">
**"Iya," she said, "give your son the water of life to drink."** — a broken quotation: quotation marks around each part, commas around "she said", and a lower-case "give" because it continues the same sentence.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Punctuation** = traffic signs for readers: it gives **order** and **style**, and can **change meaning** ("No Entrance. Alarm Will Sound.").
- **Full stop:** ends statements and commands, marks abbreviations (*Mr., Ph.D.*); ellipsis (…) shows omission.
- **Comma:** after introductory clauses and phrases; conjunctive adverbs; yes/no; direct address; interjections; appositives; series; coordinate adjectives; before *and/but* joining main clauses; omissions; question tags; quotations; dates; addresses; to prevent misreading; **non-restrictive** information only.
- **Colon:** lists, explanations, long quotes, references and times, subtitles. **Semicolon:** joins related main clauses, goes before a conjunctive adverb, separates list items that contain commas.
- **Apostrophe:** possession (*dog's, boys', boss's*), contraction (*it's*), plurals of letters (*p's and q's*). **Its ≠ it's.**
- **? !** for questions and strong feeling; **( )** for extras; **hyphen** joins (*two-day*), **dash** interrupts, **slash** for alternatives and sessions.
- **Quotation marks** for speech and short titles; **capitals** for sentence starts, proper nouns and adjectives, titles, festivals, "I"; **italics** for long titles and foreign words.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Expect **"punctuate the following"** passages, often with **dialogue**. Practise quotation marks, commas with speech, and capitals after quotes.
- Watch the classic errors: **its / it's**, the **apostrophe in plain plurals**, the **comma splice** (two main clauses joined only by a comma — use a semicolon or a conjunction) and a **missing comma after an introductory clause**.
- Know the **difference between the hyphen and the dash**, and between **restrictive and non-restrictive** clauses.
