## Why Study Literature?

The world knew **illiteracy** before it knew literacy. Today's problem is often **aliteracy**: **the lack of willingness or interest to read, even though one is able to**. Reading creative works helps form a person's **character and intellect**. To get the most from it, you need some knowledge of **literary principles**, and this chapter (by **Solomon O. Olaniyan**) provides that foundation.

---

## What Is Literature?

The word comes from the **Latin *litera*** (properly *littera*, "a letter"), which has to do with **writing**. In the broadest sense, **anything written or printed** is "literature": drug leaflets, gadget manuals, chemistry textbooks. But that definition **excludes oral literature** (folktales, chants, riddles). So the textbook defines literature as:

> **creative or imaginative works (written and spoken) involving human or animal characters, which depict human conditions, expressed through language (verbal and non-verbal), in which the elements of *indirectness* and *literariness* are emphasised.**

| Element | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Indirectness** | Literature **distorts reality**. It is **not history**: it re-presents society **fictionally or "factionally"** (a blend of fact and fiction). What happens in it is **plausible**, but not a one-to-one copy of real life. |
| **Literariness** | **Technique, style and aesthetic use of language**, different from everyday language. |

### Seven functions of literature

| Function | What it means | Example from the textbook |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Entertainment and pleasure** | It **delights** — thrills, relieves tension, prevents boredom. This is why people read literature in the first place. | — |
| **2. Teaching morals** (didactic) | It **educates subtly**, young and old. It often follows **poetic justice**: **good triumphs over evil**. | Ojaide: African literature is "traditionally **didactic**"; the writer is "**the conscience of the society**". In Akinjobi's *Mirage*, the jilting **Wale** has a bad end and the jilted **Remi** a good one, while patient **Dare** succeeds at home. African **folktales** are highly didactic. |
| **3. A repository of history** | It **documents a people's history and culture creatively** — without being history itself. | Chinua Achebe's ***Things Fall Apart*** records **Igbo** history and culture. |
| **4. Language proficiency** | Readers meet **new vocabulary and usages**, improving their communication skills. | — |
| **5. Therapy** | Literature can act like **medicine** against despair: readers live through the **resolution of conflicts**, and writers **clear their minds** of burdens. | — |
| **6. Economic** | It creates **jobs** for writers, critics, reviewers, publishers, distributors, marketers, editors and proof-readers. | — |
| **7. Ideological** | It is used as **propaganda** to orient society politically, morally and culturally. | **Plato's *Republic*** prescribes literature in the education of rulers, to instil **courage and fearlessness**. |

## The Three Genres

**Genre** (from **French**, "kind" or "type") is decided by **how a work is composed, structured and organised**.

| Genre | What defines it | Types |
|---|---|---|
| **Drama** | **Impersonation, action, dialogue, stage, audience, costume, mime and movement** — realised through **performance** | **Tragedy, comedy, tragicomedy**; also ritual drama, liturgical drama, historical drama, **farce** and **melodrama** |
| **Poetry** | Written in **lines, verses, stanzas and cantos**; marked by **rhythm, imagery and its language** | **Ballad, lyric, sonnet, elegy, ode** |
| **Prose** | **Tells a story** organised in **episodes, sections or chapters** | **Fiction** (imaginary): epistolary, picaresque, social and historical novels. **Non-fiction** (true accounts): biography, autobiography, diary, essay, memoir. **By length:** flash fiction → short story → novella → novel |

<SortingGame title="Which genre or sub-form?">

```sort
@buckets Drama | Poetry | Prose fiction | Prose non-fiction
A tragedy performed on stage with costumes and dialogue => Drama :: Impersonation and performance define drama.
A sonnet of fourteen lines => Poetry :: The sonnet is a type of poem.
A novel written entirely as a series of letters => Prose fiction :: An epistolary novel.
The story of a statesman's life, written by a journalist => Prose non-fiction :: A biography — a true account.
An elegy mourning a dead friend => Poetry :: The elegy is a poem of mourning.
A farce full of slapstick and mistaken identities => Drama :: Farce is a type of comedy for the stage.
A novel following a roguish hero through a series of adventures => Prose fiction :: A picaresque novel.
A famous musician's account of her own life => Prose non-fiction :: An autobiography.
```

</SortingGame>

## The Elements of Literature

| Element | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Subject matter** | The **topic** the author writes about — politics, violence, death, marriage, love. Literally, the subject that **matters** to the writer. |
| **Theme** | The **main or central idea**, the **underlying message**. **Subject matter is not theme**: subject matter is the general topic; themes are the **specific messages** drawn from it. Subject matter *politics* → themes: *the struggle for power*, *politics as a dirty game*, *electoral malpractice*. |
| **Point of view** | The **angle from which a story is narrated**. **First-person:** one of the characters narrates ("I…"). **Third-person:** a narrator outside the story — **omniscient** (knows everything about every character, motive and outcome) or **limited** (knows only part, usually one character's mind). |
| **Plot** | The **sequence of events**, with emphasis on **causality** (cause and effect) — this is what distinguishes plot from mere story. Driven by **characters and conflict**; **chronological or episodic**. Five parts: **exposition → complication → climax → falling action → denouement** (or introduction → rising action → climax → falling action → resolution). Plot belongs to **drama and prose**. |
| **Setting** | The **time and place** of the events, plus the historical and social circumstances. **Temporal** (time) and **locale/spatial** (place) settings. Setting contributes to the **themes**. |
| **Character and characterisation** | **Characters** are the fictional persons — humans, animals or personified concepts. They perform the actions that develop the plot. **Characterisation** is how the writer **presents** them. |

**Settings from the textbook:** Shakespeare's *Macbeth* — **medieval Scotland**. Omobowale's *Seasons of Rage* — **contemporary Africa**, full of violence, power struggles, rivalry and betrayal. Soyinka's *The Lion and the Jewel* — **colonial Yorubaland**. Dickens's *Great Expectations* — **19th-century England** (London and Kent).

**Types of character:** **round** (complex, changing), **flat** (simple, unchanging), **foil** (contrasts with another character to highlight their qualities), **protagonist** (the central character — **Okonkwo** in *Things Fall Apart*, **Wale** in *Mirage*) and **antagonist** (the opponent).

**Characterisation techniques:** basically **telling** (the author describes the character) and **showing** (the character is revealed through action). These work through **authorial presentation, dialogue, psychological disposition, setting** and **interior monologue or soliloquy**.

### Hints for literary analysis

1. **Read with questions in mind.**
2. **Bring out the basic ideas** — the characters, locations and events.
3. **Present your own view or reaction** to issues in the text.
4. **Discuss the most essential ideas** in relation to both the **form** and the **content** of the work.
5. **Use the simple present tense:** "Wale **abandons** Remi", not "Wale abandoned Remi".

## Figures of Speech

**Figures of speech** are **literary devices (tropes)** used to **polish or embellish** a work.

### Imagery

**Imagery** is the writer's use of words to **paint a mental picture or create sensations**, making readers see, feel and hear what is not overtly there.

| Type | Sense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Visual** | Sight | *The red sun sank behind the palm trees.* |
| **Auditory** | Hearing — sounds, noises, music | *The talking drum boomed through the night.* |
| **Gustatory** | Taste | *The pepper soup burned sweetly on my tongue.* |
| **Olfactory** | Smell — odours and scents | *The smell of roasting corn drifted from the roadside.* |
| **Tactile** | Touch — including beating or anything that tickles the skin | *The rough cane bit into his back.* |
| **Kinaesthetic** | Movement | *The dancers twisted and leapt around the fire.* |

### Comparison and substitution

| Figure | Definition | Examples (from the textbook) |
|---|---|---|
| **Simile** | Compares two **unlike** things that share a quality, using **"like"** or **"as"** | *Rehoboth is **as gentle as a dove**.* *The armed robbers scurried **like** a frightened rabbit.* *The man is as tall as an iroko tree.* |
| **Metaphor** | Compares **without** "like" or "as" — an **explicit transfer** of qualities. The **commonest** figure, because the language of literature is largely metaphorical | *Our leaders' words are **showers of fire**.* *Satan is **a roaring lion**.* *The word is an egg* (Osundare). |
| **Personification** | Gives **human qualities** to **non-human things or abstract ideas** | *The sky is weeping.* *Sin whispers to the wicked in their ears.* *The earth answers farmers with bountiful harvest.* |
| **Metonymy** | An object **closely associated** with an idea **stands for it** | *There is a relationship between **the gown and the town*** (university and community). *The pen is mightier than the sword.* *The White House will visit Aso Rock.* *The wigs* (lawyers) are discussing the release. |
| **Synecdoche** | A **part stands for the whole** (or the whole for a part) | ***All eyes** are on you.* *We need more **hands**.* *He has no **roof** over his head.* *The palace could not contain all the **feet**.* |
| **Antonomasia** | Calling someone by the **name of a famous figure** whose qualities they show, or replacing a name with an **epithet** | *Many politicians are **Judas Iscariot**.* *Nigerian electorates are **Oliver Twist**.* *Shakespeare, **the Bard of Avon**.* |
| **Apostrophe** | Directly **addressing someone absent, an abstraction or a non-human thing** as if it could respond | ***Death**, why have you taken our boss away?* *Twinkle, twinkle, little **star**.* *Is this a dagger which I see before me?* (Macbeth) |
| **Allusion** | A **reference** to a literary or historical person, place or event, or to another text — **classical, historical, biblical or literary** | *He met his **Waterloo** in the election* (historical). |

**Metonymy vs synecdoche:** both use **representation**. Metonymy uses something **associated** with the idea (the pen for writing; the crown for royalty). Synecdoche uses a **part of it** (hands for workers; roof for a house).

### Contradiction, contrast and exaggeration

| Figure | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Irony** | What is **said contradicts what is meant** — not to deceive, but for rhetorical or aesthetic effect | *Those students were **so punctual** that they came in five minutes to the end of the class.* *Though his mother sells cloth, Kayode still wears rags.* |
| **Hyperbole** | **Extravagant exaggeration** for serious, ironic or comic effect | *All University of Ibadan students attended my birthday.* *That guy can consume **a mortar of pounded yam**.* |
| **Oxymoron** | **Two contradictory words side by side** | *intellectual drop-outs*, *truthful lies*, *pleasurable pain*, *hostile friends*, *an **open secret*** |
| **Paradox** | A statement that **seems absurd** but holds a **deeper truth** | *If you want peace, prepare for war.* *Blessed are the poor in spirit…* *Death shall die.* |
| **Antithesis** | **Opposing ideas balanced** in parallel structure | *Man proposes, God disposes.* *Many are called, but few are chosen.* *Marriage has many pains, but celibacy has no pleasures.* |
| **Euphemism** | Saying something **unpleasant in a mild, pleasant way** | *The king has **joined his ancestors**.* *Abacha **answered the call of his creator**.* *The station **relieved some staff of their jobs**.* *Our community was visited by "street auditors"* (armed robbers). |
| **Chiasmus** | Two parallel phrases with the **order of words reversed** | *Don't **live to eat**; **eat to live**.* *If you **fail to plan**, you **plan to fail**.* |
| **Rhetorical question** (erotema) | A question asked **for emphasis or persuasion**, not for an answer | *Are we not all human beings?* *Is it not evident that death is inevitable?* |

*(The textbook's euphemism example says Abacha died in 1997. General Sani Abacha died in **June 1998**.)*

**Oxymoron vs paradox:** an oxymoron is **two words** ("open secret"); a paradox is a whole **statement** ("If you want peace, prepare for war").

### Sound and repetition

| Figure | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Assonance** | Repetition of **similar vowel sounds** in nearby words — adds **musicality** | *I w**a**ke up to b**a**ke my c**a**ke every day.* *The boys t**oi**led to get **oi**l and c**oi**l.* |
| **Alliteration** | Repetition of the same **consonant sound at the start** of nearby words | *The man could not **l**ive **l**ong because he had **l**ung disease.* *A **p**igmy des**p**ot **p**arades giant **p**adlocks.* |
| **Onomatopoeia** | Words whose **sound imitates** what they mean | *The train **rumbled**.* *The aeroplane **zoomed** off.* *The **booming** of guns.* |
| **Anaphora** | The same word or phrase repeated at the **beginning** of successive lines or sentences | *"**Words which** go to war to find peace / **Words which** go to peace to find war…"* (Osundare, "Words which") |
| **Epistrophe (epiphora)** | Repetition at the **end** of successive lines — the opposite of anaphora | *Are they Hebrews? **So am I**. / Are they Israelites? **So am I**.* (2 Corinthians 11:22) |
| **Anadiplosis** | The word that **ends** one clause **begins** the next | *…support your faith with **goodness**, and **goodness** with **knowledge**, and **knowledge** with self-control…* (2 Peter 1:5–7) |

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. Define literature and briefly explain its basic genres.">
Literature is creative or imaginative work, written or spoken, involving human or animal characters that depict human conditions through language, in which indirectness (a fictional or "factional" distortion of reality) and literariness (technique, style, aesthetic language) are emphasised. Drama is realised through performance — impersonation, action, dialogue, stage, audience, costume — with types such as tragedy, comedy, tragicomedy, farce and melodrama. Poetry is written in lines, stanzas and cantos, marked by rhythm, imagery and special language (ballad, lyric, sonnet, elegy, ode). Prose tells a story in episodes or chapters: fiction (epistolary, picaresque, social, historical novels; by length, from flash fiction to the novel) or non-fiction (biography, autobiography, diary, essay, memoir).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. In what ways is literature relevant to man?">
It entertains and gives pleasure; teaches morals (didactic, often through poetic justice — Ojaide calls the African writer the conscience of society); is a repository of history and culture (Things Fall Apart and the Igbo); improves language proficiency; has a therapeutic function, relieving despair through the resolution of conflicts; contributes to the economy (writers, critics, publishers, editors, marketers); and serves ideological purposes, as Plato's Republic recommends literature in the education of rulers.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Mention and briefly explain the elements of literature.">
Subject matter (the topic); theme (the central idea or message drawn from the subject matter); point of view (first-person, or third-person omniscient or limited); plot (a causal sequence of events — exposition, complication, climax, falling action, denouement — found in drama and prose); setting (temporal and spatial — when and where); character (fictional persons: round, flat, foil, protagonist, antagonist) and characterisation (telling and showing through authorial presentation, dialogue, psychology, setting, interior monologue and soliloquy).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. List ten figures of speech with an example of each.">
Simile (as gentle as a dove); metaphor (Satan is a roaring lion); personification (the sky is weeping); irony (so punctual they came five minutes to the end); hyperbole (he can eat a mortar of pounded yam); oxymoron (open secret); paradox (if you want peace, prepare for war); antithesis (man proposes, God disposes); euphemism (he joined his ancestors); metonymy (the pen is mightier than the sword); synecdoche (more hands); antonomasia (politicians are Judas Iscariot); chiasmus (eat to live); alliteration (a pigmy despot parades giant padlocks); assonance (wake, bake, cake); onomatopoeia (zoomed); anaphora ("Words which…"); apostrophe (Death, why have you taken him?); rhetorical question (are we not all human?).
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Literature** = creative, imaginative work (written or oral) with **indirectness** (fictional or "factional") and **literariness** (style, aesthetic language). From the Latin *litera*, "letter".
- **Seven functions:** entertainment, moral teaching (**poetic justice**), history, language proficiency, therapy, economy, ideology (Plato's *Republic*).
- **Genres:** **drama** (performance; tragedy, comedy, tragicomedy), **poetry** (lines and stanzas; ballad, lyric, sonnet, elegy, ode), **prose** (fiction and non-fiction; flash fiction → novel).
- **Elements:** subject matter ≠ theme; point of view (first-person, third-person omniscient or limited); **plot** = causality (exposition → complication → climax → falling action → denouement); **setting** (time and place); characters (round, flat, foil, protagonist, antagonist) and characterisation (telling and showing).
- **Figures:** imagery (six senses), simile vs metaphor, personification, metonymy vs synecdoche, antonomasia, apostrophe, allusion, irony, hyperbole, oxymoron vs paradox, antithesis, euphemism, chiasmus, rhetorical question, assonance, alliteration, onomatopoeia, anaphora, epistrophe, anadiplosis.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Figures of speech appear in **every** past paper ("identify the figure of speech in the following lines"). Learn the **pairs** that confuse students: **simile/metaphor**, **metonymy/synecdoche**, **oxymoron/paradox**, **anaphora/epistrophe**, **assonance/alliteration**.
- **Subject matter vs theme** is a favourite short-answer question — always give an example of each.
- When writing about a set text, use the **simple present tense** and refer to **characters, setting and events**.
