## Part A — Reading Comprehension

## What Comprehension Means

**Reading** is getting meaning from print: you **decode** the words, then **construct the writer's meaning** from them. **Comprehension** is how well you do the second part. It means **understanding** what is stated, **interpreting** what is implied, and **evaluating** what is said. A student can read every word of a passage aloud and still not comprehend it.

### Four levels of comprehension

| Level | The question it answers | Typical question words |
|---|---|---|
| **Literal** | **What does the text say?** The facts stated directly | *Who, what, when, where; "According to the passage…"* |
| **Inferential** (interpretive) | **What does the text mean?** Reading between the lines: implied ideas, causes, the writer's attitude | *"What does the writer imply…?", "Why do you think…?", "The tone of the passage is…"* |
| **Critical** (evaluative) | **Is it true, fair, well argued?** Judging evidence, bias, fact versus opinion | *"Do you agree…?", "Is the argument convincing?", "Which statement is an opinion?"* |
| **Creative** (applied) | **How can I use it?** Applying the ideas to new situations | *"What would happen if…?", "Suggest another solution…"* |

<SortingGame title="Which level of comprehension does each question test?">

```sort
@buckets Literal | Inferential | Critical | Creative
According to the passage, when does the library fill up? => Literal :: The answer is stated directly.
What does the writer imply by calling the handout "the student's Bible"? => Inferential :: You must read between the lines.

Which of these sentences from the passage is an opinion, not a fact? => Critical :: Distinguishing fact from opinion is evaluation.
```

## Reading Techniques: Match the Method to the Purpose

| Technique | What you do | Use it to… |
|---|---|---|
| **Skimming** | Read **quickly for the gist**: title, headings, first and last paragraphs, topic sentences | Preview a chapter; decide if a book is relevant; get the general idea |
| **Scanning** | Run your eyes over the text **looking for one specific item** — a name, date, figure or keyword | Find your name on a results list, a date in a history text, an entry in an index |
| **Intensive reading** | Read **slowly and closely**, sentence by sentence, often re-reading and annotating | Study a textbook chapter; answer comprehension questions; analyse a poem |
| **Extensive reading** | Read **widely and at length** for general understanding and pleasure | Build vocabulary, fluency and general knowledge — novels, newspapers, magazines |

<SortingGame title="Skimming, scanning, intensive or extensive?">

```sort
@buckets Skimming | Scanning | Intensive | Extensive
Checking the exam timetable for the date of GES 201 => Scanning :: You are hunting for one item.
Glancing through five library books to see which one is relevant to your term paper => Skimming :: You want the general idea quickly.
Studying a chapter on the sound system of English before a test => Intensive :: Slow, close, detailed reading.
Reading three novels during the holiday for enjoyment => Extensive :: Wide reading for pleasure and fluency.
Looking up "plagiarism" in a book's index => Scanning :: A specific word.
Reading the headings and first lines of a newspaper article to see what it is about => Skimming :: Gist only.
Working through a comprehension passage to answer exam questions => Intensive :: Every detail may matter.
```

### SQ3R — a study-reading method

**Francis P. Robinson** (*Effective Study*, 1946) designed **SQ3R** for reading textbooks:

<FlowDiagram title="SQ3R">
<FlowStep title="S — Survey">
**Skim** the chapter first: title, introduction, headings, diagrams, summary and questions at the end.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Q — Question">
Turn each heading into a **question** (*"Types of Essay"* → *"What are the types of essay?"*). Questions give your reading a purpose.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="R1 — Read">
Read **actively**, section by section, looking for the answers to your questions.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="R2 — Recite">
After each section, **look away and say or write the answers** in your own words. If you can't, re-read.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="R3 — Review">
At the end, and again later, **go over all your questions and answers** to fix them in memory.
</FlowStep>
</FlowDiagram>

<OrderPuzzle title="Put the SQ3R steps in order">

```order
Survey :: Skim headings, introduction, summary.
Question :: Turn headings into questions.
Read :: Read actively for the answers.
Recite :: Answer from memory, in your own words.
Review :: Revisit everything later.
```

</OrderPuzzle>

## Bad Reading Habits and Their Cures

Your eyes do not glide smoothly along a line: they **jump and stop**. Each stop is a **fixation**, and comprehension happens during the stops. Efficient readers take in **a phrase per fixation** (a wide **eye span**) and rarely jump back.

| Habit | Why it slows you down | Cure |
|---|---|---|
| **Vocalisation** — reading aloud or moving your lips | You can read only as fast as you can speak | Close your lips; read silently |
| **Sub-vocalisation** — "hearing" every word in your head | The same speech-speed limit | Read in phrases; push your pace slightly |
| **Regression** — eyes jumping back to re-read | Breaks the flow of meaning | Keep moving; trust the context to clarify |
| **Finger-pointing / head movement** | Ties your eyes to one word at a time | Let your eyes, not your finger or head, move |
| **Word-by-word reading** | Meaning lives in phrases, not single words | Practise taking in groups of words: *in the library / before the exam* |
| **Poor concentration** | Re-reading the same paragraph | Set a purpose (SQ3R); remove distractions such as your phone |
| **Limited vocabulary** | Every unknown word is a stop | Read extensively; use context clues; keep a word notebook |

Average adults read ordinary prose silently at roughly **200–300 words per minute**. Study reading is slower — and should be. **Speed is useless without comprehension.**

## Practice: a Timed Passage

<ReadingSpeed title="Timed reading — 'Readers before Thinkers'">

```passage
Every semester, the university library fills up in the last three weeks before examinations. Students who have not borrowed a single book since resumption suddenly arrive with flasks of tea, phone chargers and photocopied handouts, and settle in for marathon sessions that end only when the porters switch off the lights. The scene is so familiar that nobody remarks on it any more. Yet it says something uncomfortable about how many of us read.

Reading, for such students, is an emergency measure rather than a habit. Books are opened to pass examinations, not to satisfy curiosity, and they are closed again the moment the last paper is written. The handout, a few pages summarising a whole course, has become the student's Bible; the textbook, with its footnotes and its demands on patience, gathers dust. Some lecturers blame the smartphone, which offers endless entertainment in bite-sized pieces and trains the mind to expect a reward every few seconds. Others blame the cost of books, which has climbed far beyond the means of most families.

Both explanations contain some truth, but neither is the whole story. A habit is learned, and it is learned early. A child who grows up in a house without books, who never sees an adult reading for pleasure, is unlikely to discover the joy of reading at twenty. The problem, in other words, begins long before the university gate.

The remedy, therefore, cannot be a single campaign or a week of book fairs. Parents must read to their children and in front of them. Schools must give pupils stories they want to read, not only texts they must analyse. And universities must reward wide reading in their assessments, so that the student who has explored beyond the handout is not marked the same as the one who memorised it. A nation that wants thinkers must first produce readers.
```

## Skills for Answering Comprehension Questions

**Find the main idea.** Each paragraph usually has a **topic sentence**, most often the first, sometimes the last. The other sentences are **supporting details**: examples, reasons, facts. The passage's main idea ties all the paragraphs together; titles and conclusions often signal it.

**Separate fact from opinion.**
- A **fact** can be checked: *The library closes at 10 p.m.*
- An **opinion** is a judgement: *The library closes far too early.*

Watch for opinion signals: *should, must, best, worst, beautiful, I believe, clearly*.

<SortingGame title="Fact or opinion?">

```sort
@buckets Fact | Opinion
The University of Ibadan was founded in 1948. => Fact :: Verifiable from records.
Ibadan is the most beautiful city in Nigeria. => Opinion :: "Most beautiful" is a judgement.
GES 201 is a compulsory course for 200-level students. => Fact :: Checkable in the course handbook.
Handouts should be banned in universities. => Opinion :: "Should" signals a recommendation.
The handout summarises the course in a few pages. => Fact :: Observable.
Smartphones have ruined this generation. => Opinion :: A sweeping judgement.
```

</SortingGame>

**Work out meaning from context.** You rarely need a dictionary in an exam. The sentence around a word gives **context clues**:

| Clue | Example |
|---|---|
| **Definition** | *An **anthology** — a collection of poems by different writers — was prescribed.* |
| **Synonym / restatement** | *The speech was **verbose**; in other words, it was far too wordy.* |
| **Antonym / contrast** | *Unlike his **gregarious** brother, Tunde prefers to be alone.* (so gregarious = sociable) |
| **Example** | ***Condiments** such as pepper, salt and curry were on the table.* |
| **General sense** | *After walking for six hours in the sun, the **famished** travellers ate everything.* (very hungry) |

**Replacing a word.** The replacement must be the **same part of speech**, in the same **tense and number**, and fit **this context**. *"The students **devoured** the novel"* — read eagerly, **not** ate.

**Answering technique:**
1. **Skim** the passage for the gist.
2. **Read the questions.**
3. **Read the passage intensively**, marking where each answer lies.
4. Answer **in complete sentences** and **in your own words** unless told to quote.
5. **Don't add** information that is not in the passage — unless the question asks for your opinion.

## Part B — Register

## What Is Register?

A **register** is a **variety of language defined by the situation in which it is used**. The same speaker uses different registers in the lecture hall, the market, a hospital and a WhatsApp chat. Register varies according to the **use**; a **dialect** varies according to the **user** (region or social group).

The linguist **M. A. K. Halliday** described three features of a situation that shape register:

| Variable | Question | Example (a doctor talking) |
|---|---|---|
| **Field** | **What** is going on — the subject and activity? | Diagnosing an illness → *hypertension, prescription, dosage* |
| **Tenor** | **Who** is taking part, and what is their **relationship**? | Doctor to patient (polite, simplified) vs doctor to doctor (technical) |
| **Mode** | **How** is language used — spoken or written, and in what channel? | A consultation (spoken) vs a case note (written, abbreviated) |

### Levels of formality — Joos's "five clocks"

**Martin Joos** (*The Five Clocks*, 1961) described five styles, from most to least formal:

| Style | Where it is used | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Frozen** | Fixed wording that never changes: prayers, oaths, anthems, legal formulas | *"I do solemnly swear…"*; the National Pledge |
| **Formal** | One-way, carefully planned: lectures, speeches, official letters | *"The Senate has approved the following regulations."* |
| **Consultative** | Two-way exchange with someone you don't know well, or a superior | *"Excuse me, sir, could you explain the second question?"* |
| **Casual** | Friends and peers: contractions, slang, ellipsis | *"You coming to the match later?"* |
| **Intimate** | Family and close partners: private words, pet names | *"Mumsy, I'm home!"* |

<SortingGame title="Which of Joos's five styles?">

```sort
@buckets Frozen | Formal | Consultative | Casual | Intimate
"Our Father, who art in heaven, hallowed be thy name…" => Frozen :: A fixed text recited word for word.
"Good morning, ma. Please, I'd like to confirm my course registration." => Consultative :: A polite exchange with an official.
"Ladies and gentlemen, it is my honour to welcome you to this convocation." => Formal :: A planned, one-way public address.
"Guy, where you dey? Lecture don start o!" => Casual :: Peer talk in Nigerian Pidgin.
"Goodnight, my darling. Sleep well." => Intimate :: Close personal relationship.
"I pledge to Nigeria my country, to be faithful, loyal and honest…" => Frozen :: The National Pledge never changes.
"In this paper, I shall examine three causes of examination malpractice." => Formal :: Academic writing.
```

</SortingGame>

## Register as Field Vocabulary

In Nigerian examinations, **register** usually means the **vocabulary of a particular field or profession**. The question gives you a passage or a gap and asks which field it belongs to, or which word completes it.

| Field | Typical register words |
|---|---|
| **Law** | plaintiff, defendant, counsel, adjourn, affidavit, bail, verdict, acquit, plea, prosecution, *sub judice* |
| **Medicine** | diagnosis, prognosis, symptom, prescription, ward, theatre, surgeon, anaesthetist, out-patient, dosage, chronic |
| **Journalism** | editor, headline, scoop, byline, column, correspondent, editorial, circulation, press release |
| **Sports** | referee, umpire, penalty, offside, tackle, striker, half-time, extra time, trophy, fixture |
| **Banking / finance** | overdraft, collateral, interest rate, deposit, teller, loan, cheque, account statement, dividend |
| **Agriculture** | harvest, fallow, mulching, irrigation, fertiliser, tuber, seedling, livestock, pest, yield |
| **Politics** | constituency, manifesto, ballot, electorate, primaries, caucus, incumbent, impeachment |
| **Religion** | congregation, sermon, pulpit, altar, pilgrimage, fasting, deacon, imam, tithe |
| **Computing** | download, cursor, bandwidth, server, virus, browser, password, upgrade, interface |
| **Aviation** | cockpit, runway, take-off, turbulence, cabin crew, boarding pass, hangar, control tower |

### One word, many registers

Many everyday words change meaning with the field. **Context decides.**

| Word | Field 1 | Field 2 | Field 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **theatre** | Medicine: the operating room | Drama: a building for plays | Military: a *theatre of war* |
| **cell** | Biology: the smallest unit of life | Prisons: a room for a prisoner | Electricity: a battery unit |
| **court** | Law: where cases are tried | Sports: a tennis or basketball court | Royalty: a king's court |
| **charge** | Law: an accusation | Electricity: positive or negative charge | Banking: a fee on your account |
| **operation** | Medicine: surgery | Military: a planned campaign | Mathematics: addition, subtraction… |
| **pitch** | Football: the field | Music: how high a note is | Business: a sales proposal |
| **interest** | Banking: money paid on a loan or deposit | General: curiosity or concern | Law: a legal share or right |
| **virus** | Medicine: a disease-causing agent | Computing: harmful software | — |

(d) 'Your account was debited with ₦5,000 for the transfer, and a stamp duty of ₦50 applies.'">
- **(a) Law** — counsel, the accused, court, bail.
- **(b) Medicine** — patient, presented with, fever, blood film.

<ExamTip>
**Register errors in your own writing.** Using the wrong register is a usage error even when the grammar is perfect. Casual words in a formal essay sound wrong: *"The lecturer was like, 'Submit your assignment'"*, *"The government should go and sort it out."* So do frozen or legal formulas in a friendly letter (*"I hereby inform you that I miss you"*). Match the **field, tenor and mode** of the task.
</ExamTip>

<Takeaway>
- **Comprehension** = understanding, interpreting and evaluating. **Levels:** literal (stated), inferential (implied), critical (judged), creative (applied).
- **Techniques:** **skim** for gist, **scan** for a specific item, read **intensively** to study and **extensively** for fluency. **SQ3R:** Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review.
- **Bad habits:** vocalising, sub-vocalising, regression, finger-pointing, word-by-word reading. Read in **phrases**; speed is useless without comprehension.
- **Skills:** topic sentence and main idea; fact vs opinion; **context clues** (definition, synonym, antonym, example, general sense); replacements must match part of speech and tense; answer in your own words.
- **Register** = language variety by **use** (vs dialect, by **user**). **Field, tenor, mode** (Halliday). **Joos:** frozen, formal, consultative, casual, intimate.
- **Field vocabulary:** law, medicine, journalism, sports, banking, agriculture… The same word (*theatre, cell, court, charge*) changes meaning with the field.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- For "**which word can replace…**", test each option **in the sentence**. The right one keeps the meaning, the part of speech and the tense.
- For register questions, find **two or three field words** in the extract before choosing. One word can mislead (*theatre*, *court*).
- Don't answer inferential questions with copied sentences. Show that you understood **what lies behind** them.
