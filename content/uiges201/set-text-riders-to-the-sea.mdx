## The Playwright and the Play

**John Millington Synge** (1871–1909) was an **Irish dramatist** of the **Irish Literary Revival** and a co-director of Dublin's **Abbey Theatre**. Between **1898 and 1902** he stayed several times on the **Aran Islands**, off the west coast of Ireland, and recorded island life in his book ***The Aran Islands***. His best-known plays are ***Riders to the Sea*** (1904) and ***The Playboy of the Western World*** (1907).

***Riders to the Sea*** is **a play in one act**, **first performed at the Molesworth Hall, Dublin, on 25 February 1904**. The introduction to our edition (by **Edward J. O'Brien**, 1911) explains that Synge built it from two true stories he heard on **Inishmaan**, the middle Aran island:

1. A man's body was **washed up on the far coast of Donegal** and identified as an islander from his **clothing**, exactly as in the play.
2. A tale of **"second sight"** — seeing the dead — which gave the play its **title**.

A *Manchester Guardian* critic called it **"the tragic masterpiece of our language in our time"**.

## Setting and Characters

**Setting:** an **island off the west of Ireland** (Inishmaan, in the Aran group). It takes place in a **cottage kitchen** with **nets, oil-skins, a spinning wheel and some new boards standing by the wall**. The action takes **less than an hour**: **one place, one day, one action**.

| Character | Who they are |
|---|---|
| **Maurya** | The **old mother**. She has lost her **husband, her husband's father and five sons** to the sea, and fears for her last son, Bartley. The **tragic heroine**. |
| **Bartley** | Her **last living son**, determined to take **horses to the Galway fair** by boat despite the rough sea |
| **Cathleen** | Her **elder daughter** (about twenty), **practical** and **managing**. She bakes, spins and directs the household. |
| **Nora** | Her **younger daughter**, more anxious; she brings the bundle of clothes. |
| **Michael** | A son who **drowned nine days earlier**; he never appears, but his **clothes** come from Donegal. |
| **The young priest** | Never seen; he **reassures** that "the Almighty God won't leave her destitute with no son living". |
| **Men and women** | Neighbours who **keen** (wail) and carry in Bartley's body. **Colum** and **Eamon** are asked to make the coffin. |

### The men Maurya has lost

| Who | How they died |
|---|---|
| Her **husband**, his **father**, and her son **Sheamus** | **Lost together on a dark night**; "not a stick or sign was seen of them" |
| **Stephen** and **Shawn** | **Lost in the great wind**; found in the **Bay of Gregory of the Golden Mouth** and carried home **on one plank** |
| **Patch** | **Drowned** when a **curagh** (small boat) **turned over**; carried in on **half of a red sail** |
| **Michael** | Drowned; body found off **Donegal** ("the far north") — identified by his **shirt and stocking** |
| **Bartley** | **Knocked into the sea by the grey pony**, washed out "where there is a great surf on the white rocks" |

"**I've had a husband, and a husband's father, and six sons in this house — six fine men… but they're gone now the lot of them.**"

## Scene-by-Scene Summary

<FlowDiagram title="Riders to the Sea — the action">
<FlowStep title="1. The bundle">
**Nora** brings a **bundle** from the young priest: **a shirt and a plain stocking taken off a drowned man in Donegal**. The daughters must find out whether they are **Michael's**. Afraid their mother will wake, Cathleen **hides the bundle in the turf-loft**. The priest **won't stop Bartley** from sailing, but says God "won't leave her destitute with no son living".
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="2. Bartley insists on going">
**Bartley** asks for the **new rope** bought for **Michael's coffin**, to use as a **halter** for the horses. **Maurya** begs him to stay: "**What is the price of a thousand horses against a son where there is one son only?**" He gives instructions for the farm (the sheep, the pig with the black feet, the kelp), says he will be back in two, three or four days, and leaves: "**The blessing of God on you.**" **Maurya will not bless him.**
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="3. The forgotten bread">
Cathleen scolds her mother for sending Bartley out "**with an unlucky word behind him**". The girls realise they **forgot to give Bartley his bread (cake)**. Cathleen sends **Maurya to the spring well** with the bread, so she can say "God speed you" and **break "the dark word"**. Maurya leaves leaning on **the stick Michael brought from Connemara**: "**In the big world the old people do be leaving things after them for their sons and children, but in this place it is the young men do be leaving things behind for them that do be old.**"
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="4. The clothes are Michael's">
The girls open the bundle, **cutting** the string "perished with the salt water" with the **knife**. The flannel matches Michael's shirt, but plenty of men wear it. **Nora counts the stitches** on the stocking: "**It's the second one of the third pair I knitted, and I put up three score stitches, and I dropped four of them.**" It is **Michael's**. They hide the clothes as Maurya returns.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="5. Maurya's vision">
Maurya returns **still holding the bread** and begins to **keen**: "**I seen the fearfulest thing.**" At the well, Bartley rode by on the **red mare**. Her blessing **choked in her throat**. Then on the **grey pony** behind him she saw **Michael** — "**with fine clothes on him, and new shoes on his feet**". The girls tell her Michael's body has been found in the north. Maurya knows Bartley is doomed: "**It's little the like of him knows of the sea**" (the priest). She recounts all her lost men.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="6. Bartley's body">
Old women come in, **crossing themselves and keening**; men carry in **Bartley's body on a plank, covered with a bit of a sail**, water dripping. "**The grey pony knocked him into the sea, and he was washed out where there is a great surf on the white rocks.**"
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="7. Resignation">
Maurya sprinkles the **last of the Holy Water**: "**They're all gone now, and there isn't anything more the sea can do to me.**" The men ask for **nails** for the coffin — Maurya **forgot the nails**. She prays for all her dead and for "every one is left living in the world", and ends: "**No man at all can be living for ever, and we must be satisfied.**" The curtain falls slowly.
</FlowStep>
</FlowDiagram>

## Themes

| Theme | How the play shows it |
|---|---|
| **Man against nature (the sea)** | The **sea** gives the islanders their living (fishing, kelp, transport) and takes their lives. Every man of Maurya's family dies by it. Humans are **powerless** against it. |
| **Fate and inevitability** | Bartley's death is **foreshadowed** at every step — the rising wind, "a star up against the moon", the withheld blessing, the vision. Like **Greek tragedy**, the outcome feels **fated**. |
| **Grief, loss and resignation** | Maurya moves from **anxious pleading** to a **calm, tragic acceptance**: "There isn't anything more the sea can do to me… it's a great rest I'll have now." Her final peace (O'Brien: "the peace that passeth understanding") is the play's **catharsis**. |
| **Faith and superstition** | Christian faith (the priest, **Holy Water**, prayers, "the grace of God") sits beside older **pagan** beliefs — **second sight**, the "unlucky word", omens, **Samhain** (the old Celtic feast). The priest's assurance proves **empty**: "It's little the like of him knows of the sea." |
| **Poverty and hardship** | Bartley **must** sell horses to survive. The family relies on **kelp, sheep, a pig**. Maurya imagines eating "**a bit of wet flour… and maybe a fish that would be stinking**". |
| **Women's endurance** | The **women stay home** to **wait, keen, knit and bury**. The men "ride to the sea". Cathleen's practicality and Maurya's strength show the **endurance of island women**. |
| **Generations reversed** | "**In this place it is the young men do be leaving things behind for them that do be old**" — the natural order of inheritance is **reversed** by the sea. |

## Symbols

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **The sea** | **Death and fate**, but also **livelihood** — a power that gives and takes |
| **The new white boards** | Bought for **Michael's coffin**; they become **Bartley's**. **Death is expected** in this house |
| **The rope** | Bought to lower Michael's coffin, taken by Bartley as a **halter** — the instrument of **death and life** at once |
| **The bread (cake)** | **Life and nourishment**, and Maurya's chance to **bless** her son. It is **never delivered** |
| **The grey pony** | **Death** itself, carrying the ghost of Michael and knocking Bartley into the sea. It echoes the **pale horse of death** in Revelation 6:8 |
| **The red mare** | **Life and the living** (Bartley rides it) |
| **The stick** | Michael's stick now supports the old woman — **the young leaving things to the old** |
| **Holy Water** | **Christian faith**; Maurya uses **the last of it** — her prayers are spent |
| **The spinning wheel and knitting** | **Women's work** and the **thread of fate**; the **dropped stitches** identify Michael |
| **The forgotten nails** | Maurya is **old and broken**, but also beyond the practical — her mind is on the eternal |

## Language and Dramatic Technique

### Synge's Hiberno-English *(features named beyond the textbook)*

Synge wrote in the **English of the Irish-speaking west**, translating Irish grammar into English. It gives the play its **poetic, rhythmic** speech:

| Feature | Example | Standard English |
|---|---|---|
| **"after + -ing"** = has just done | "The young priest **is after bringing** them." | has just brought |
| **"do be"** = habitual action | "the old people **do be** leaving things" | usually leave |
| **Fronting** for emphasis | "**It's destroyed** we are from this day." | We are destroyed |
| **"itself"** for emphasis | "Where is he **itself**?" | Where is he? |
| **"herself"** = the woman of the house | "**herself** will be down looking by the sea" | Mother |
| **"the way"** = so that | "**the way** she won't know of them" | so that |

### Dramatic techniques

- **Dramatic irony:** the audience and the daughters know about the bundle before Maurya does. Maurya keeps the boards for **Michael**, but they will hold **Bartley**.
- **Foreshadowing:** the wind "rising from the south and west", "a star up against the moon", the **rope**, the **boards**, the withheld blessing, the vision of the **grey pony**.
- **The unities of time, place and action** (a Greek ideal): one kitchen, a single short span of time, one tragic action.
- **Keening:** the traditional Irish lament, performed on stage, heightens the **ritual** atmosphere.
- **Offstage action:** the sea and the deaths are **never shown**, only reported. The kitchen becomes a place of **waiting**.
- **Tragedy:** Maurya is a **tragic heroine** whose fall is caused by **fate (the sea)** rather than a moral flaw. The ending brings **catharsis**, a cleansing peace.

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. Discuss Riders to the Sea as a tragedy.">
Maurya is a tragic heroine overwhelmed by a force she cannot resist — the sea, the agent of fate. Her fall follows the classical pattern: anxious struggle (begging Bartley to stay), a crucial failure (withholding her blessing, the undelivered bread), recognition through the vision of Michael on the grey pony, and catastrophe (Bartley's body brought in). The play keeps the unities of place, time and action, uses foreshadowing and dramatic irony, and ends in catharsis — Maurya's calm resignation, "No man at all can be living for ever, and we must be satisfied", which O'Brien calls "the peace that passeth understanding". Unlike Aristotle's heroes, Maurya falls through fate and environment rather than a moral flaw.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Examine the theme of man against nature in the play.">
The sea is both provider and destroyer. It gives the islanders fish, kelp and their only route to markets (Bartley must take the boat to sell horses at Galway), yet it has taken Maurya's husband, his father and all six sons. The weather is a constant presence ("a great roaring in the west", the wind rising from the south and west). The characters are powerless: the priest's assurance fails, prayers and Holy Water cannot protect anyone, and even bodies are often never found ("not a stick or sign"). Human life is fragile and brief beside the sea's endless power.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Discuss the symbols in Riders to the Sea.">
The sea stands for death, fate and livelihood. The white boards, bought for Michael's coffin, become Bartley's — death is ever expected. The rope meant for the coffin becomes Bartley's halter. The bread symbolises life and Maurya's undelivered blessing. The grey pony carrying the dead Michael is death itself (an echo of the pale horse of Revelation), while the red mare carries the living. Michael's stick, now supporting the old woman, shows the young leaving things to the old. The last of the Holy Water marks the end of Maurya's prayers; the forgotten nails show her age and her mind turned beyond practical things.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Compare Cathleen and Nora, and explain Maurya's role.">
Cathleen, the elder, is practical and in command: she kneads the cake, spins, hides the bundle, sends Maurya after Bartley with the bread, and organises the coffin. She can be impatient with her mother ("God forgive you…"). Nora, the younger, is more emotional and anxious; she carries the priest's message and identifies Michael's stocking through her own knitting. Maurya, the tragic mother, embodies the suffering of island women: possessive and fearful at first, she grows into a figure of dignified resignation, praying for all her dead and "every one is left living in the world".
</Reveal>

## Exam Tips

- Extract questions ask **who is speaking, to whom, about what**, and **what device** is used. Memorise the **key lines** in the "Who says it?" game.
- Know the **sequence of events** exactly: the bundle → rope → no blessing → bread → stocking → vision → body → nails.
- Be ready to discuss **two themes** and **three symbols** with quotations.
- Know the **names and fates** of Maurya's men — a favourite objective question.
