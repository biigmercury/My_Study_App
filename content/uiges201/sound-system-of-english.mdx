## Speaking and Listening

**Speaking** is a **productive skill**: the ability to produce a string of noises perceived as a **continuous flow of meaningful sound** by anyone who understands the language.

- **Language** is the principal means humans use to communicate — a system of communication **in speech and writing** used by a speech community. Language is **primarily spoken**; it can be transferred to **writing**, or to **sign language** when speech is unavailable (as among the deaf).
- The four basic skills are **listening and speaking (primary)** and **reading and writing (secondary)**. A user is **competent** if she or he can use all four effectively.
- This chapter (by **Ayo Osisanwo**) is about **speaking**, and partly **listening**. Its aim is to make users of **English as a Second Language (ESL)** more competent at understanding native speakers and at speaking clearly.

Learning the sounds of English shows ESL learners how to **pronounce words as correctly as possible**, or at least to the level of **international intelligibility**. To do that, you must know the available **speech sounds** and how each is **articulated** by the **organs of speech** (Osisanwo 2009).

## The Human Articulators

The **organs of speech**, or **human articulators**, include the **oral cavity, nasal cavity, lips, teeth, alveolar (teeth) ridge, hard palate, soft palate, uvula, tongue, pharynx, epiglottis, trachea (windpipe), lungs, vocal cords and glottis**.

![Diagram of the human speech organs: the lungs and diaphragm in the chest, the trachea rising to the larynx with the vocal folds, and in the head the nasal cavity, oral cavity, palate, velum, uvula, alveolar ridge, teeth, lips and tongue](/images/uiges201/speech-organs.png)

*The organs of speech. Diagram by Theresa Knott, CC BY-SA 2.5, via Wikimedia Commons.*

| Articulator | Role in speech |
|---|---|
| **Lungs** | A pair of spongy, air-filled organs in the chest. They **initiate vocal activity** by providing the **driving force** that compresses the air used for speech. English uses the **pulmonic egressive airstream** (air pushed **out** from the lungs). |
| **Trachea (windpipe)** | The passage that carries air to and from the lungs. |
| **Vocal cords** | Two small muscular folds in the **larynx**. **Apart:** air passes easily without vibration, giving **voiceless** sounds. **Together:** air forces its way through and they **vibrate**, giving **voiced** sounds. |
| **Glottis** | The **space between the vocal cords**. It produces the **glottal** sound /h/. |
| **Pharynx** | A muscular tube in the neck between the uvula and the larynx, connecting nose and mouth to the windpipe and oesophagus — the cavity **behind the mouth**. |
| **Epiglottis** | A tongue-like flap just above the larynx, responsible for **epiglottal** sounds. |
| **Oral cavity** (mouth) | Produces **oral** sounds. **Upper region:** upper lip, upper teeth, alveolar ridge, hard palate, soft palate, uvula. **Lower region:** lower lip, lower teeth, tongue. |
| **Nasal cavity** (nose) | Produces **nasal** sounds, made when the nose is the **only escape route** for air. |
| **Lips** | Take different shapes (**lip posture**) for vowels, and are fully or partly involved in consonants. |
| **Teeth** | Work with the lips and tongue to control airflow, helping you **speak clearly**. |
| **Alveolar ridge** | The rim just behind the upper teeth that the tongue touches for **alveolar** sounds (/t d s z n l r/). |
| **Hard palate** | The bony front half of the roof of the mouth. It produces **palatal** and **palato-alveolar** sounds. |
| **Soft palate (velum)** | The movable back part of the roof of the mouth. It produces **velar** sounds and **decides whether a sound is oral or nasal**. |
| **Uvula** | The small dangling end of the velum. When the velum is raised, it **closes the passage into the nose**. |
| **Tongue** | An extremely flexible muscle attached to the floor of the mouth, divided for description into the **tip, blade, front and back** (also centre, root, rims, edges and apex). |

<Analogy>
Put two fingers on your throat (on your "Adam's apple") and say a long **"zzzz"**, then a long **"ssss"**. You feel a **buzz** for /z/ but not for /s/. That buzz is your **vocal cords vibrating**: /z/ is **voiced**, /s/ is **voiceless**. Try /v/–/f/ and /ð/ (*this*)–/θ/ (*think*) too.
</Analogy>

## Vowels and Consonants

| | **Vowels** | **Consonants** |
|---|---|---|
| **Airflow** | **No obstruction**, or the **least possible** obstruction, in the oral cavity | **Significant obstruction** that impedes the airflow |
| **How many in English** | **20** (12 pure vowels + 8 diphthongs) | **24** |
| **Described by** | Tongue height, tongue position, lip shape, length, number of nuclei | State of the glottis, place of articulation, manner of articulation |

### Describing vowels — five parameters

1. **Height of the tongue** (vertical axis): **close, half-close, half-open, open**.
2. **Part of the tongue raised** (horizontal axis): **front, central, back**.
3. **Shape of the lips**: **spread** (unrounded), **neutral** or **rounded**.
4. **Length**: **long** (marked ː) or **short**.
5. **Nucleus**: **monophthong** (one), **diphthong** (two) or **triphthong** (three).

### The 12 pure vowels (monophthongs)

**Pure vowels** have a **single vowel nucleus**: the tongue stays in one position.

### The 8 diphthongs

**Diphthongs** have a **double vowel nucleus**: the tongue **glides from one vowel position to another**. *(The terms "closing" and "centring" are beyond the textbook.)* **Closing** diphthongs glide towards /ɪ/ or /ʊ/; **centring** diphthongs glide towards /ə/.

| # | Diphthong | Type | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 13 | /eɪ/ | closing | fate, sachet, chalet, faith, pay, grey, eight, reign |
| 14 | /əʊ/ | closing | load, coal, go, cold, show, toe |
| 15 | /aɪ/ | closing | hide, rise, fly, high, night, tie |
| 16 | /aʊ/ | closing | found, mouth, pound, now, dowry |
| 17 | /ɔɪ/ | closing | boy, joy, enjoy, voice, oil, noise |
| 18 | /ɪə/ | centring | cheer, steer, dear, here, weird, pier, real |
| 19 | /eə/ | centring | air, chair, rare, share, swear, where, there |
| 20 | /ʊə/ | centring | pure, amour, tour, moor |

**Two slips in the textbook's lists:** it puts **"shear"** under /eə/, but *shear* sounds like *sheer*, /ʃɪə/. It also puts **"area"** under /ɪə/, but *area* begins with /eə/: /ˈeəriə/.

### The 5 triphthongs

**Triphthongs** glide through **three** vowel qualities. They form when one of the **five closing diphthongs** combines with **/ə/**. They are not numbered like vowels 1–20.

| Triphthong | Examples |
|---|---|
| /eɪə/ | payer, slayer |
| /əʊə/ | lower, mower |
| /aɪə/ | plier, fire, choir |
| /aʊə/ | flour, tower, bowel |
| /ɔɪə/ | employer, buoyant, royal |

## The 24 Consonants

Consonants are described by **three parameters**: the **state of the glottis**, the **place of articulation** and the **manner of articulation**.

### 1. State of the glottis: voiced or voiceless

- **Voiced** consonants — the vocal cords **vibrate**: /b d g v ð z ʒ dʒ m n ŋ l r j w/ (**15**).
- **Voiceless** consonants — **no vibration**: /p t k f θ s ʃ tʃ h/ (**9**).

### 2. Place of articulation — *where* the greatest constriction is

| Place | How it is made | Sounds |
|---|---|---|
| **Bilabial** | Upper and lower **lips** meet | /p b m w/ |
| **Labio-dental** | **Upper teeth** on the **lower lip** | /f v/ |
| **Inter-dental** | **Tongue tip between** the upper and lower teeth | /θ ð/ |
| **Alveolar** | Tongue tip against the **alveolar ridge** | /t d s z n l r/ |
| **Palato-alveolar** | Tongue between the alveolar ridge and the **hard palate** | /ʃ ʒ tʃ dʒ/ |
| **Palatal** | Front of the tongue raised towards the **hard palate** | /j/ |
| **Velar** | **Back of the tongue** against the **velum** | /k g ŋ/ |
| **Glottal** | Constriction at the **glottis** | /h/ |

### 3. Manner of articulation — *how* the air is obstructed

| Manner | What happens | Sounds |
|---|---|---|
| **Stops / plosives** | **Complete stoppage** of the airstream, then **sudden release with plosion** | /p b t d k g/ |
| **Fricatives** | **Extreme narrowing**, so the air forces its way through with **friction** | /f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ h/ |
| **Affricates** | Complete stoppage followed by a **gradual release** (a stop plus a fricative) | /tʃ dʒ/ |
| **Nasals** | **Velum lowered**, mouth closed off, so air passes **only through the nose** | /m n ŋ/ |
| **Liquids** | Tongue raised and air escapes **around** it: the **lateral** liquid /l/ (air over the sides of the tongue) and the liquid /r/ | /l r/ |
| **Semi-vowels** (approximants, glides) | Organs **come close but not completely together** | /j w/ |

*(Beyond the textbook: the textbook calls /r/ a "roll" liquid. In standard British English, /r/ is an approximant — the tongue does not trill. A rolled or tapped r is common in Nigerian English but is not the RP model.)*

So a full consonant label reads **voicing + place + manner**: /b/ is a **voiced bilabial plosive**, /ʃ/ a **voiceless palato-alveolar fricative**, /ŋ/ a **voiced velar nasal**.

**Patterns to remember:**

- **Silent letters** — t (castle, ballet), b (debt, plumber), d (Wednesday), h (hour, honest), l (yolk), n (column), g (phlegm, gnash).
- **th is not t or d** — think /θ/, three /θ/, them /ð/. But **Thames and Thomas** are pronounced with a plain /t/.
- **ch has three sounds** — /tʃ/ (archbishop, nature), /k/ (architect, archangel) and /ʃ/ (Chicago, champagne, brochure, chassis).
- **Long and short vowels** distinguish words — sheep/ship, sport/spot, aunt/ant.

## Summary

The chapter examined speaking skills through the **segmental** sounds of English — the **consonants and vowels**. It described the **articulators** that produce them. **Vowels** are made **without impediment** to the airflow and described by several parameters. **Consonants** are made with **significant obstruction** and described by **voicing, place and manner**. It ended with **100 words often mispronounced** by Nigerian speakers.

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. What are human articulators? List ten and state the functions of four.">
The organs of the body used to produce speech sounds: lungs, trachea, vocal cords, glottis, pharynx, epiglottis, oral cavity, nasal cavity, lips, teeth, alveolar ridge, hard palate, soft palate (velum), uvula and tongue. Lungs provide the driving air (pulmonic egressive airstream). Vocal cords vibrate for voiced sounds and stay apart for voiceless ones. The velum produces velar sounds and decides whether a sound is oral or nasal. The alveolar ridge is where the tongue tip makes /t d s z n l r/.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Differentiate between vowel and consonant sounds.">
Vowels are produced with no obstruction (or the least possible) to the airflow in the mouth; there are 20 (12 pure vowels, 8 diphthongs), described by tongue height, tongue part, lip shape, length and number of nuclei. Consonants are produced with significant obstruction; there are 24, described by the state of the glottis (voiced/voiceless), place of articulation and manner of articulation.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Describe /p/, /v/, /ð/, /ŋ/, /tʃ/ and /j/.">
/p/ voiceless bilabial plosive; /v/ voiced labio-dental fricative; /ð/ voiced inter-dental fricative; /ŋ/ voiced velar nasal; /tʃ/ voiceless palato-alveolar affricate; /j/ voiced palatal semi-vowel (approximant).
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Explain pure vowels, diphthongs and triphthongs with examples.">
Pure vowels (monophthongs) have one vowel nucleus — the tongue holds one position: /iː/ see, /æ/ man, /ʌ/ love. Diphthongs have two nuclei — the tongue glides from one vowel to another: /eɪ/ pay, /aɪ/ high, /ɪə/ dear. Triphthongs glide through three qualities, formed when a closing diphthong combines with /ə/: /aɪə/ fire, /aʊə/ tower, /eɪə/ payer.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Speaking** is a **productive** skill; listening and speaking are **primary**, reading and writing **secondary**. The goal is **international intelligibility**.
- **Articulators:** lungs (**pulmonic egressive** air), vocal cords (**voiced** if vibrating), glottis, pharynx, epiglottis, oral and nasal cavities, lips, teeth, alveolar ridge, hard palate, **velum** (oral vs nasal), uvula, tongue (tip, blade, front, back).
- **20 vowels:** **12 pure** vowels and **8 diphthongs** (5 closing, 3 centring), plus **5 triphthongs**. Vowels are described by height, front/central/back, lips, length and nuclei.
- **24 consonants** = **voicing + place + manner**. 9 voiceless: /p t k f θ s ʃ tʃ h/. Places: bilabial, labio-dental, inter-dental, alveolar, palato-alveolar, palatal, velar, glottal. Manners: plosive, fricative, affricate, nasal, liquid, semi-vowel.
- **Common errors:** silent letters (debt, Wednesday, hour, column), th → t/d, ch confusions, and long/short vowels (sheep/ship, sport/spot).
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Past papers ask you to **identify the sound underlined** and to **write the phonemic symbol**. Practise with the chart above until each symbol is automatic.
- **Describe consonants in three terms** (voicing, place, manner) **in that order**.
- Learn the **100 words**, especially silent letters and the **ch / th** patterns — they turn up as "which word contains the sound…" items.
- Don't confuse **letters** with **sounds**: *phone* starts with /f/, *knee* with /n/, and *new* contains /j/.
