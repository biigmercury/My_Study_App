## Why ICT Belongs in a Use of English Course

The way things were done **fifty years ago** differs from how they are done today, and teaching is no exception. The modern classroom is **no longer limited to the physical, chalk-and-talk setting**. **Babatunde Raphael Ojebuyi**, the chapter's author, makes the case:

- University students must show **minimum language and communication competence** to excel academically. They also need it to be good communicators among peers and, later, as professionals.
- Unless you can **use modern communication tools** for learning, you may struggle in a world that is **progressively becoming ICT-driven**.
- **Prudent use of ICT** to teach English **enhances communicative competence** and boosts learning in terms of **attitudes, autonomy and authenticity** (Rhalmi 2017).

The chapter also covers ICT's **negative effects**, "because unless one is familiar with **how not to use a device**, one may abuse it".

## What Is ICT?

> **Information and Communications Technology (ICT)** refers to the **communication applications and electronic devices** used for **interactive discussions, exchange of different kinds of information, and sharing of global knowledge**. These are essential for **skill acquisition** and for **social, political and economic development**, and they can also serve as **learning materials**.

| **Old ICT** | **New (modern) ICT** |
|---|---|
| Radio, television, analogue telephones | Modern telecommunication systems: **wireless signals, mobile phones, computers, storage and audio-visual devices, software, middleware**, and all **internet-enabled features** for storing, manipulating, retrieving and exchanging information electronically |

**Modern ICT** is described as the **integration of different technologies**, using **common transmission lines** to carry very diverse information in many formats among different users (Ojebode and Yeku 2012; Technopedia 2017). The chapter focuses on **modern ICT used in learning and collaboration**.

## How ICT Evolved

**The term.** Researchers have used the phrase *information and communications technology* **since the 1980s** (Melody et al. 1986; Mansell 1994). The abbreviation **ICT** became popular after it appeared in a **1997 report to the UK government by Dennis Stevenson**, and in the **revised National Curriculum for England, Wales and Northern Ireland in 2000**.

**The technology.** In teaching and learning, Kaino (2008) describes **four phases**:

<FlowDiagram title="Four phases of ICT in teaching and learning (Kaino 2008)">
<FlowStep title="Phase 1 — Broadcast media">
**Films, radio, television and satellite broadcasting.** Teaching was dominated by the **paper-and-pencil method**, and the learner depended **almost solely on the instructor**.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Phase 2 — Telecommunications and microcomputers">
**Telecommunications** and **microcomputers** came into instruction.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Phase 3 — Integration">
**Telecommunications integrated with microelectronic technology.** It promised **productive, problem-solving, life-long learners** — better informed, rational and participative, living in the **web and network-wide electronic community**.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Phase 4 — Globalisation (today)">
An era of **rapid interconnectedness** with **no barriers of distance** in the global exchange of knowledge. Knowledge is delivered through **computer software and applications**. **You belong to this phase.**
</FlowStep>
</FlowDiagram>

As a fourth-phase learner, you can interact with the **computer screen**, not only with your lecturers. You can receive lectures online, share materials, discuss assignments and collaborate within and beyond your locality, using:

> **email, SMS, MMS, Facebook, Twitter, Online Learning Management Systems (OLMS) and Moodle, video conferencing, messengers, blogs, WeTransfer, WhatsApp, Skype and webinars.**

## The Internet, the Web, Browsers and Search Engines

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **The web** (World Wide Web, WWW) | The **segment of the Internet** enabled for media such as **text, animation, video, audio and graphics** (Commonwealth of Learning 2015). It is a **network of servers that store pages**. |
| **The Internet** | The wider network. **You can be on the Internet without being on the web**: when you use it to send or receive mail, you are not on the web. You are **on the web only when you browse** (search for information). |
| **Browser** | **Software you download and install** that lets you read web pages and embeds search engines: **Internet Explorer, Google Chrome, Safari, Firefox**. |
| **Search engine** | A **software system that helps you find information on the web**: Google, Bing, Yahoo… You **cannot, and need not, download** a search engine. Your browser gives you access to it. |
| **URL** (Uniform Resource Locator) | The **unique web address**, the public link to a text page, video or audio. **Without a URL you cannot locate an item or share your document publicly.** |
| **SERPs** (search engine results pages) | The list of results — texts, videos, images — that a search returns. |

<Analogy>
Think of the **Internet** as Nigeria's whole road network, the **web** as the markets you can visit on it, a **browser** as your vehicle, a **search engine** as the guide who tells you which market sells what, and a **URL** as a shop's exact address. Sending an email is like sending a parcel along the roads: you use the network without visiting any market.
</Analogy>

### Some top search engines (as described in 2017)

| Search engine | What the textbook says |
|---|---|
| **Google** | **The most popular by far.** In 2016 it held **89%** of the mobile/tablet search market (comScore); **64.0%** of all searches were powered by Google and **21.4%** by Bing. **Google Scholar** (scholar.google.com) makes it popular with researchers — use it for **research publications**. |
| **Bing** | **Second most popular**, Microsoft's initiative to challenge Google — though some users are yet to be convinced it gives better results. |
| **Yahoo** | Still **the most popular email provider**, and **third** in search. **Since October 2011, Yahoo search has been powered by Bing.** |
| **Ask.com** | Founded in **1996 by Garrett Gruener and David Warthen in Berkeley, California**; formerly **Ask Jeeves**. About **3%** of searches. Its distinctive feature: a **question-and-answer** platform where other users answer questions or polls. |
| **AOL** | About **0.6%** of the market, but still in the top 10. Its network includes **engadget.com, huffingtonpost.com and techcrunch.com**. It is a destination for the **latest stories** around the world. |
| **Wolfram Alpha** | Different from all the others: a **computational knowledge engine** that gives **facts and data** and performs **calculations** across culture, geography, science, linguistics, health and more. |
| **Internet Archive** | Shows **how a website looked as far back as 1996**. It is a San Francisco-based **non-profit** offering free access to books, films, music and **294 billion archived web pages**. |
| **Booksee.org, B-OK.org** | Listed by the textbook as free e-book libraries. *(Beyond the textbook: B-OK was a Z-Library domain distributing copyrighted books without permission, and its domains were **seized by US authorities in 2022**. Prefer legal sources — your university library, Google Books, Project Gutenberg, the Internet Archive.)* |

The textbook adds a rule: when you use material found through a search engine, **cite the material itself, not the search engine** that led you to it.

## Five Categories of ICT Tools for Language Learning

Following **Lim and Tay (2003)**, as updated by UniAssignment.com (2017):

| Category | What the tools do | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Informative** | Provide **large amounts of information** in many formats (graphics, audio, text, video). A **repository** to retrieve what you need | **The Internet** (the best example), **e-books, audio books**, homepages, network virtual drives, **intranet** systems |
| **Situating** | **Hypermedia (multimedia)** that lets learner and teacher **experience** the learning environment through text, audio, graphics, animation and video | A multimedia presentation on **manner and place of articulation** in phonology that lets you **hear and see** how speech sounds are made, shared on a **flash drive or CD-ROM** |
| **Constructive** | General-purpose tools to **manipulate information, construct your own knowledge** and **monitor your language competence** | **Microsoft Word** (with **spell-checker, dictionary, grammar checker**), **PowerPoint**, Lego Mindstorms, Adobe Photoshop, FrontPage; installed **pronouncing dictionaries** |
| **Communicative** | **Easy communication** with lecturers and classmates, inside and outside the classroom. **Synchronous** tools (chat, video conference) allow **real-time** exchange | **Electronic whiteboard, email, chat, SMS, electronic bulletin boards, teleconference** |
| **Collaborative** | Support **collaborative activities**: meetings, discussions, information sharing, **group work** | **Email messaging, Skype, discussion boards, OLMS and Moodle, group chats, video conferencing and webinars** |

<SortingGame title="Which category of ICT tool?">

```sort
@buckets Informative | Situating | Constructive | Communicative | Collaborative
Downloading an e-book on English grammar from the Internet => Informative :: A repository you retrieve information from.
A multimedia presentation showing how the lips and tongue form speech sounds => Situating :: Hypermedia that lets you hear and see the sounds.
Using Microsoft Word's spell-checker and grammar checker on your essay => Constructive :: A general-purpose tool for building and checking your own text.
A real-time video call with your lecturer about your assignment => Communicative :: Synchronous communication with your lecturer.
Your study group editing a shared document and discussing it on a Moodle forum => Collaborative :: Group work across distance.
Listening to an audio book of a set text => Informative :: Audio books are informative tools.
Designing slides in PowerPoint for a class presentation => Constructive :: You construct and present your own knowledge.
Sending an SMS to a classmate about tomorrow's tutorial => Communicative :: Simple text-based communication.
A department-wide webinar where students present group projects => Collaborative :: Shared, interactive online activity.
```

</SortingGame>

### Free language tools on the Internet

| Tool | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Grammarly** (grammarly.com) | Checks that what you type is **easy to read, effective and mistake-free**. Install it in your browser (it works with Chrome), or paste text into its editor. |
| **Grammar Check for Sentence** | A free online tool giving **meanings of words** and checking the **correctness of sentences**. |
| **Ginger Grammar Checker** | Uses the **context of complete sentences** to correct **grammar, spelling and misused words** — "just like a human reviewer would". |
| **Grammar Check (Editor)** | Paste your text into the **"editor's box"**, press **Free Check**, then accept or reject each underlined suggestion. **Read through again** afterwards. |
| **LanguageTool** (languagetool.org) | An **open-source** proofreader for English, French, German, Polish and **more than 20 other languages**. |
| **Cambridge Online Dictionary**, **Dictionary.com**, **Macmillan Dictionary** | Meanings, usage and **pronunciation** — click the sound icon to **listen**. Dictionary.com includes a **thesaurus**. |

## Using ICT to Build the Four Language Skills

| Receptive skills (we **receive** information) | Expressive skills (we **express** thoughts and share ideas) |
|---|---|
| **Listening**, **reading** | **Speaking**, **writing** |

- **Smartphones** offer **portability, social interactivity, individuality and immediacy** (Ammanni and Aparanjani 2016). They come with **dictionary look-up** and language apps, so a good phone may replace a printed dictionary. **Predictive text** helps with spelling. You can download language **quizzes and games** from the App Store or Play Store, and play **podcasts** (downloadable digital audio) and **vodcasts** (downloadable digital video).
- **Computer-Assisted Language Learning (CALL)** — using a computer to aid language learning — now includes the **Internet** and software such as CD-ROMs and Office applications (spell-checker, dictionary, film maker).
- **Online grammar lessons and drills:** English Club, English Grammar Secrets, EnglishGrammar.org, Using English, **British Council LearnEnglish**, Perfect English Grammar, the **Grammarly Handbook**. The Internet's **instant response** can motivate you, and many resources can be **downloaded for offline use**.

## The Other Side of ICT

ICT is like **a coin with two sides** — or, as the chapter's summary puts it, **a double-edged sword**. **Indiscriminate and careless use** can harm your language skills, especially **writing and reading**.

1. **Unproductive activities** — addictive games, non-educational films, **indecent sites** and unwholesome social activity — distract you from study. Even when you think you are reading, the content may be **harmful**.
2. **Don't replace real-life engagement with online interaction.** Hiding in cyberspace can make you an **unsociable individual**, too shy to approach a lecturer when you need help.
3. **The computer is not human.** It acts only on **pre-programmed instructions**, and spell-checkers and grammar checkers **miss errors where the wrong word is spelt correctly**:

| Sentence | What was meant |
|---|---|
| I am hungry and **stick**. | sick |
| I need your **hemp** on this matter. | help |
| The library was **quite** today. | quiet |
| I have been **leaving** in the city for the past 15 years. | living |
| The secretary could not prepare the memo because our **stationary** had been exhausted. | stationery |

**Always proofread.** Avoid over-relying on **predictive text** — accepting its suggestions blindly can cause **unimaginable embarrassment**. The golden rule: **don't always rely on the intelligence of your device; read and edit before you send or post.**

<SortingGame title="Would a spell-checker catch it?">

```sort
@buckets Spell-checker catches it | Spell-checker misses it
I will recieve the parcel tomorrow => Spell-checker catches it :: "Recieve" is not an English word, so it gets underlined.
The library was quite today => Spell-checker misses it :: "Quite" is a real word — only the meaning (quiet) is wrong.
Their going to the lecture together => Spell-checker misses it :: "Their" is a real word; you meant "they're". Some grammar checkers may flag it, many won't.
We bought new stationary for the office => Spell-checker misses it :: "Stationary" (not moving) is a real word; you meant "stationery".
The goverment announced a new policy => Spell-checker catches it :: "Goverment" is misspelt (government).
I need your hemp on this matter => Spell-checker misses it :: "Hemp" is a real word, just the wrong one.
Definately, the exam is on Monday => Spell-checker catches it :: "Definately" is not a word (definitely).
```

</SortingGame>

4. **Don't become addicted to cyber language.** **Cyber language** — also called **Internet shorthand, cyber-slang, netspeak, Internet slang or chatspeak** — is the slang, expressions and codes Internet users employ. Its meanings are **restricted to frequent users**.

| Code | Meaning | Code | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| ILU | I love you | MYOB | mind your own business |
| LOL | laugh out loud | FYI | for your information |
| AFAIK | as far as I know | OMG | oh my God |
| ASAP | as soon as possible | NIMBY | not in my back yard |
| BM&Y | between you and me | CWYL | chat with you later |
| CTN | cannot talk now | AMA | ask me anything |
| FYNE | fine | BAE | babe / before anyone else |
| CUS | see you soon | ICYMI | in case you missed it |

Cyber language **has its value**: it is useful in **email, chat rooms and SMS**, where it **saves time and space** in real-time conversation. But it **deviates from the conventional language system**, so it is **not acceptable in formal conversation or academic writing**.

<SortingGame title="Appropriate or not?">

```sort
@buckets Fine to use | Not appropriate
"CWYL, gotta run!" in a WhatsApp chat with a friend => Fine to use :: Informal, real-time chat between frequent users.
"FYI the tutorial has moved to 4pm" in a class group chat => Fine to use :: Cyber language saves time in quick group messages.
"I want 2 apply 4 d post ASAP" in a job application letter => Not appropriate :: Formal writing needs conventional spelling and expressions.
"LOL, the theory is sha confusing" in an exam answer => Not appropriate :: Academic writing must avoid chatspeak.
"Pls sir, I wnt 2 c u abt my result" in an email to your lecturer => Not appropriate :: An email to a lecturer is formal correspondence.
"OMG, ICYMI — the results are out!" in a text to your roommate => Fine to use :: Casual SMS between friends.
```

</SortingGame>

### A note on AI writing tools *(beyond the textbook)*

Since this chapter was written (2019), **generative AI tools** such as chatbots and AI writing assistants have joined the ICT toolkit. The chapter's advice applies to them even more strongly:

- they can **explain grammar, suggest wording and quiz you**, which is a useful **constructive and informative** tool;
- but they can be **confidently wrong**, and submitting AI-written work as your own breaches **academic integrity** rules;
- treat them like any device: **don't rely on their intelligence; read, check and edit everything**.

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. How would you define ICT? Give examples of ICT components.">
ICT refers to the communication applications and electronic devices used for interactive discussion, the exchange of information and the sharing of global knowledge — essential for skill acquisition and social, political and economic development. Old ICT: radio, television, analogue telephones. New ICT: wireless signals, mobile phones, computers, storage and audio-visual devices, software, middleware and internet-enabled features for storing, retrieving and exchanging information.

<Reveal question="2. Identify and explain the four stages of ICT evolution.">
(Kaino 2008) 1. Films, radio, television and satellite broadcasting — paper-and-pencil teaching, with the learner depending almost wholly on the teacher. 2. Telecommunications and microcomputers in instruction. 3. Integration of telecommunications and microelectronics, promising productive, problem-solving, life-long learners in a networked community. 4. <Reveal question="3. What is the difference between the web and search engines?">
The web is the part of the Internet enabled for media (text, audio, video, graphics) — a network of servers storing pages that you read through a browser. A search engine (Google, Bing, Yahoo) is a software system that helps you find information on the web; you reach it through your browser and need not download it. You can be on the Internet without being on the web (for example, sending email); you are on the web when you browse.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Explain, with examples, constructive and collaborative ICT tools.">

Collaborative tools support group activity — meetings, discussions, information sharing and distributed group work: email messaging, Skype, discussion boards, OLMS/Moodle, group chats, video conferences and webinars.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="5. Mention any five sites that provide lessons and practice on English grammar.">
English Club (englishclub.com/grammar), English Grammar Secrets, EnglishGrammar.org, Using English (usingenglish.com), British Council LearnEnglish, Perfect English Grammar, and the Grammarly Handbook.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="6. Discuss the negative side of ICT for language learning.">
Careless use harms reading and writing: addictive games, non-educational films and indecent sites distract and may harm; replacing real-life interaction with online life can make a student unsociable and shy of lecturers. Computers are not human — spell-checkers miss correctly spelt wrong words (stick/sick, hemp/help, quite/quiet, leaving/living, stationary/stationery), and blind acceptance of predictive text causes embarrassment, so you must proofread. Cyber language (LOL, ASAP, FYI…) saves time in chats and SMS but deviates from conventional language and is unacceptable in formal and academic writing.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **ICT** = communication applications and devices for interaction, information exchange and knowledge sharing. **Old:** radio, TV, analogue phones. **New:** internet-enabled devices, software, middleware.
- The term was used **since the 1980s** and popularised by **Dennis Stevenson's 1997 report**. **Four phases** (Kaino): broadcast media → telecoms and microcomputers → integration → **globalisation (today)**.
- **Web** ≠ Internet; **browser** (downloaded) ≠ **search engine** (not downloaded); **URL** = unique web address; **SERPs** = results pages. Google, Bing, Yahoo (powered by Bing since 2011), Ask.com (Ask Jeeves, 1996), AOL, Wolfram Alpha, Internet Archive. **Cite the material, not the search engine.**
- **Lim and Tay's five tool types:** informative, situating, constructive, communicative, collaborative.
- **Receptive** skills: listening, reading. **Expressive** skills: speaking, writing.
- **The other side:** distraction, unsociability, **real-word errors spell-checkers miss**, predictive-text slips, **cyber language** (fine in chats, never in formal writing). **Always proofread.**
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Know the **five categories of ICT tools** with one example each.
- Be ready to explain the **web vs Internet** and **browser vs search engine** distinctions — favourite short-answer questions.
- Learn the **four phases of ICT evolution** in order.
- For "negative effects", give the **spell-checker examples** (stationary/stationery and others) and the point about **cyber language in formal writing**.
