## What Is Poetry?

**Poetry** is the genre written in **lines, verses, stanzas and cantos**, marked by **rhythm, imagery and special language**. The coursebook says poetry is meant to **entertain, express, inform and teach morals**, and lists what makes it unique:

1. **varied length** — from a two-line couplet to a book-length epic;
2. **lines and stanzas** rather than sentences and paragraphs;
3. **sound devices** — rhyme, alliteration, onomatopoeia and others;
4. **poetic licence** with space, punctuation and grammar (deliberately breaking the rules for effect);
5. **word pictures** that build sensory impressions and create **images**;
6. the **sounds of words** and the **rhymes of phrases**.

## The Elements of Poetry

| Element | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Imagery** | Words that appeal to the **senses** (visual, auditory, gustatory, olfactory, tactile, kinaesthetic). Poets choose their images to suit their world: **T. S. Eliot** used images of **urban life**, **Wordsworth** used **nature**. |
| **Diction** | The poet's **choice of words, phrases and sentences** |
| **Style** | The **way** the poem is written — the arrangement of words that expresses the poet's individuality: **free verse, ballad, sonnet**, each with its own metre and stanzas |
| **Symbol** | An object, person, situation or action that **stands for an idea** — a flag for a nation, a dove for peace |
| **Rhyme** | The recurrence of **similar sounds**, especially at line endings. The coursebook names **internal, cross, random and mixed** rhyme. It gives the poem **flow** |
| **Rhythm** | The **beat** of the poem — how the words resonate and flow when linked together. **Every poem has a rhythm** |
| **Metre** | A **regular rhythmic structure**: a sequence of **feet**, each a fixed pattern of **stressed and unstressed** syllables. It makes poetry **melodious** |
| **Tone** | The poet's **attitude** towards the subject — angry, ironic, tender, mocking |
| **Mood** | The **feeling or atmosphere** the poem creates in the reader — gloomy, joyful, tense |

**Tone vs mood.** **Tone** belongs to the **speaker**; **mood** is what the **reader** feels. The coursebook shows how punctuation and emphasis change tone: *"Yeah, that sausage was great!"* (genuine praise) versus *"Yeah, that sausage was great."* (flat — probably sarcastic).

### Rhyme and rhyme scheme

We label line endings with letters: the **same letter = the same rhyme sound**. The pattern is the **rhyme scheme**.

> If we must die — let it not be like **hogs** *(a)*<br />
> Hunted and penned in an inglorious **spot** *(b)*<br />
> While round us bark the mad and hungry **dogs** *(a)*<br />
> Making their mock at our accursed **lot** *(b)*

*(Beyond the textbook:)* **end rhyme** comes at the ends of lines, **internal rhyme** within a line ("Once upon a midnight **dreary**, while I pondered, weak and **weary**"). **Full (perfect) rhyme:** *night/light*. **Half (slant) rhyme:** sounds that are close but not identical — *love/move*, *bear/near*. In "If We Must Die", *hogs/dogs* and *spot/lot* are both full rhymes.

<Reveal question="Exercise: work out the rhyme scheme of Wordsworth's 'My Heart Leaps Up' (a set poem).">
My heart leaps up when I **behold** (a) / A rainbow in the **sky** (b) / So was it when my life **began** (c) / So is it now I am a **man** (c) / So be it when I shall grow **old** (a) / Or let me **die** (b)! / The Child is father of the **Man** (c) / And I could wish my days to **be** (d) / Bound each to each by natural **piety** (d).

Rhyme scheme: **a b c c a b c d d**.
</Reveal>

### Metre and feet *(beyond the textbook in detail)*

A **foot** is a unit of stressed (´) and unstressed (˘) syllables. The name of a metre combines the **foot** and the **number of feet per line**.

| Foot | Pattern | Example word |
|---|---|---|
| **Iamb** (iambic) | ˘ ´ | be**HOLD**, to**DAY** |
| **Trochee** (trochaic) | ´ ˘ | **TWIN**kle, **HAP**py |
| **Anapaest** (anapaestic) | ˘ ˘ ´ | under**STAND** |
| **Dactyl** (dactylic) | ´ ˘ ˘ | **BEAU**tiful |
| **Spondee** (spondaic) | ´ ´ | **HEART**-**BREAK** |

| Feet per line | Name |
|---|---|
| 1 | monometer |
| 2 | dimeter |
| 3 | trimeter |
| 4 | **tetrameter** |
| 5 | **pentameter** |
| 6 | hexameter |

So **iambic pentameter** = five iambs: **˘ ´ ˘ ´ ˘ ´ ˘ ´ ˘ ´** — the commonest metre in English, used in sonnets and blank verse.

**Scansion** means marking the stressed and unstressed syllables in a line. Try it — read each line aloud and tap the syllables you hear stressed:

**Two line-level devices** *(beyond the textbook)*: **enjambment** — a sentence **runs on** from one line into the next without a pause. **Caesura** — a **pause within a line**, often marked by punctuation: "If we must die**—**let it not be like hogs".

### Stanzas *(names beyond the textbook)*

A **stanza** is a regular group of lines — the "paragraph" of a poem.

| Lines | Name |
|---|---|
| 2 | **couplet** |
| 3 | **tercet** (triplet) |
| 4 | **quatrain** |
| 5 | cinquain |
| 6 | **sestet** |
| 8 | **octave** |

<SortingGame title="Stanzas and sound devices">

```sort
@buckets Couplet | Quatrain | Sestet | Octave | Refrain | Enjambment | Caesura
A stanza of four lines => Quatrain :: Four lines; the ballad's usual stanza.
The last two rhyming lines of a Shakespearean sonnet => Couplet :: Two lines.
The first eight lines of a Petrarchan sonnet => Octave :: Eight lines (rhymed abbaabba).
The last six lines of a Petrarchan sonnet => Sestet :: Six lines (often cdecde).
A line or phrase repeated at intervals, like a chorus => Refrain :: E.g. Osundare's "Ara ponmbe ponmbe ponmbe".
A sentence that runs on from one line into the next without a pause => Enjambment :: The sense flows over the line break.
A strong pause in the middle of a line, marked by a dash => Caesura :: "If we must die — let it not be like hogs".
```

</SortingGame>

## Forms of Poetry

| Form | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ballad** | A narrative poem, mostly in **quatrains** alternating **iambic tetrameter** and **iambic trimeter**. Usually **only lines 2 and 4 rhyme (abcb)** | Yeats's "The Ballad of Moll Magee"; Charles Causley's "Ballad of the Bread Man" |
| **Blank verse** | A **regular metre** (usually **iambic pentameter**) with **no rhyme** | Much of Shakespeare's drama; Milton's *Paradise Lost* |
| **Free verse** (*vers libre*) | **No regular metre or rhyme** — its form is free | Most modern African poetry, e.g. Diop's "Africa" |
| **Heroic couplet** | **Rhymed pairs of iambic pentameter lines**, common in narrative poetry | Pope's *The Rape of the Lock* |
| **Sonnet** | A **fourteen-line** poem in metre | See the comparison below |
| **Terza rima** | **Three-line stanzas** with an **interlocking** rhyme: **aba bcb cdc ded…** | Robert Frost's "Acquainted with the Night" |
| **Villanelle** | A **nineteen-line** poem with **two alternating refrains** and a complex rhyme scheme | Dylan Thomas's "Do Not Go Gentle into That Good Night" |

| **Shakespearean (English) sonnet** | **Petrarchan (Italian) sonnet** |
|---|---|
| **Three quatrains + a couplet** | **An octave (8) + a sestet (6)** |
| **abab cdcd efef gg** | Octave **abbaabba**; sestet usually **cdecde** |
| The closing **couplet** often **sums up** or twists the argument | A **turn (*volta*)** between octave and sestet: the octave puts forward a **proposition**, the sestet a **solution** |
| Shakespeare's Sonnet 18 ("Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?"); **McKay's "If We Must Die"** | Milton's "On His Blindness"; Yeats's "Leda and the Swan" |

**Other forms you should know** *(beyond the textbook)*:

- **lyric** — a short poem expressing personal feeling;
- **ode** — a formal poem **in praise** of someone or something;
- **elegy** — a **lament for the dead**; a **dirge** or **threnody** is a mournful song;
- **epic** — a **long narrative** in an elevated style about a hero (*Sunjata*);
- **limerick** — a five-line **comic** poem, rhymed aabba;
- **haiku** — a three-line Japanese form of 5–7–5 syllables;
- **satire** — blends criticism with humour.

**African oral forms** include **praise poetry** (Yoruba *oríkì*), **ìjálá** (hunters' chants), **incantations** (*ọfọ̀*) and **dirges** — the set poem "When a Strainer Takes in Water" is a translated Yoruba incantation.

## How to Analyse a Poem

The coursebook warns against **taking a poem apart like a car engine and then not knowing how to put it back together**. Each time you find a technique, ask: ***Why has the poet used this word, this image, this rhythm?*** Techniques mean something only in their **context**.

<FlowDiagram title="Analysing an unseen poem (the coursebook's steps)">
<FlowStep title="1. Read — two or three times">
Many students start writing after reading only the title. Hear the poem in your head; notice the sounds, rhythms, rhymes and striking words. Note your **first response**.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="2. Who is the speaker?">
The voice is the **persona**, not necessarily the poet. Is it first, second or third person? Whom is it speaking to?
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="3. What is the setting?">
Where and when does the poem take place, and how does that shape the **atmosphere**?
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="4. What is the form?">
**Count**: stanzas, lines, syllables. Mark the **rhymes and stresses** to find the pattern — and where the poem **breaks** it. Form interacts with content: a sonnet about a broken relationship carries special poignancy.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="5. Subject matter">
Describe exactly **what is happening** — the experience, object or feeling the poet writes about. It is the springboard for the themes.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="6. Purpose and theme">
What does the poet want you to **know or feel**? Some poems simply amuse or entertain. Keep an open mind.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="7. Imagery and devices">
Pick the most interesting images and devices and explain **what they do** — the atmosphere they create, and how they deepen the meaning.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="8. Mood and tone">
What mood dominates, and does it shift? What is the speaker's attitude? The same topic (a child's red bicycle) in a bright mood suggests **the joy of childhood**; in a sad mood, **the pain of growing up**.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="9. Overall meaning">
End with a **summary**: what the poem means and whether the poet succeeds.
</FlowStep>
</FlowDiagram>

## A Model Analysis: Lenrie Peters' "We Have Come Home"

**The poet.** **Lenrie Peters** (1932–2009) was born in **Gambia** and educated in **Sierra Leone** and at **Trinity College, Cambridge**, where he qualified in **medicine** and became president of the **African Students' Union**. He worked on **BBC Africa programmes**, wrote one novel (*The Second Round*, 1965) and three volumes of poetry, and chaired the **West African Examinations Council** (1985–1991). He is a **Pan-Africanist** and one of the most original voices of modern African poetry.

**The poem** opens:

> We have come home<br />
> From the bloodless wars<br />
> With sunken hearts<br />
> Our boots full of pride —<br />
> From the true massacre of the soul<br />
> When we have asked<br />
> "What does it cost<br />
> To be loved and left alone?"

*(The coursebook prints line 4 as "Our booths full of pride" and reads "booths" as the inner enclosures of their hearts. Published versions of the poem read "boots".)*

**Theme.** A warning to **foreign invaders, possibly the colonial masters**, that **Africa wants her freedom granted and her dignity respected** (lines 7–8).

**Subject matter.** The persona announces that "we" have returned home from a **"bloodless war"**, which can be read as **colonialism and the slave trade**: lives were traded for gunpowder, wine and gin without a shot fired. It was a **"massacre of the soul"**. The returnees come with **sunken hearts**, carrying **"the pledge / Which is written in rainbow colours"** (the rainbow suggests **equality**) for burial. The persona decides **"It is not the time / To lay wreaths / For yesterday's crimes"**: the future promises little, the **"dark sun"** appears, songs are alien, the march is a **death march**, and lives are as random as **"the spinning coin"**. Home offers natural beauty — green foothills, birdsong, hot beaches, fishing boats — and a **spirit** that survives famine and drought and asks only **"to have dignity"**.

**The poet's methods.**

| Device | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Repetition (anaphora)** | Every stanza but one opens with **"We have come home"** | Stresses **self-realisation** and the hope of freedom, not merely a physical return |
| **Simple language and local images** | "the gurgling drums", "the forest", "the green foothills", "birdsong", "the thundering rain" | Makes the poem accessible and **African** in colour |
| **Metaphor** | "**bloodless wars**" | The struggle abroad was violence without blood |
| **First-person pronouns** | "We have come home…", "We have asked…" | Shows the poet's **involvement** and wins the reader's **sympathy** |
| **Personification** | "The forest **howls**" | Nature shares the sorrow |
| **Alliteration** | "**g**liding **g**ulls", "**s**inging **s**ongs", "**s**udden **s**pirit" | Rhythm and music |
| **Tone** | — | **Pathetic and not very optimistic**: Africans are still pleading "to be loved and left alone" |

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. What are the features that make poetry distinct from other forms of writing?">
Poetry varies in length; it is written in lines and stanzas; it uses sound devices (rhyme, alliteration, onomatopoeia); it exercises poetic licence with space, punctuation and grammar; it builds word pictures (imagery) for sensory impressions; and it exploits the sounds of words and the rhymes of phrases. It aims to entertain, express, inform and teach morals, with rhythm, metre, diction, tone and mood as key elements.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Distinguish the Shakespearean from the Petrarchan sonnet.">
Both have fourteen lines in metre (usually iambic pentameter). The Shakespearean (English) sonnet has three quatrains and a final couplet, rhymed abab cdcd efef gg; the couplet often sums up or twists the argument (Sonnet 18; McKay's "If We Must Die"). The Petrarchan (Italian) sonnet has an octave rhymed abbaabba and a sestet often rhymed cdecde, with a turn (volta) between them: the octave poses a proposition and the sestet offers a resolution (Milton's "On His Blindness").
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Explain these terms with examples: iambic pentameter, free verse, terza rima, villanelle, ballad.">
Iambic pentameter: five iambs (˘´) per line — "If WE must DIE—let IT not BE like HOGS". Free verse: no regular metre or rhyme. Terza rima: three-line stanzas with interlocking rhymes aba bcb cdc (Frost, "Acquainted with the Night"). Villanelle: nineteen lines with two alternating refrains (Dylan Thomas, "Do Not Go Gentle into That Good Night"). Ballad: a narrative poem in quatrains alternating iambic tetrameter and trimeter, usually rhymed abcb.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Outline the steps for analysing an unseen poem.">
Read it several times; identify the speaker (persona) and audience; the setting; the form (count stanzas, lines and syllables; mark rhymes and stresses; note departures from the pattern); the subject matter; the poet's purpose and themes; the key images and devices and what they achieve; the mood and tone and any shifts; and finally the overall meaning and how well the poet achieves it. Always ask why each technique is used.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Poetry:** lines and stanzas, sound devices, poetic licence, imagery. It aims to entertain, express, inform and teach morals.
- **Elements:** imagery, diction, style, symbol, rhyme (**scheme** in letters), rhythm, **metre** (feet × number), **tone** (the speaker's attitude) vs **mood** (the reader's feeling).
- **Feet:** iamb ˘´, trochee ´˘, anapaest ˘˘´, dactyl ´˘˘, spondee ´´. **Iambic pentameter** = five iambs. **Enjambment** (run-on) vs **caesura** (mid-line pause).
- **Stanzas:** couplet 2, tercet 3, **quatrain 4**, sestet 6, octave 8.
- **Forms:** ballad (abcb), blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter), free verse, heroic couplet, **sonnet** (Shakespearean abab cdcd efef gg; Petrarchan octave + sestet with a volta), terza rima (aba bcb), villanelle (19 lines); also ode, elegy, dirge, epic, limerick and African praise poetry.
- **Method:** read → speaker → setting → form → subject matter → theme → imagery and devices → mood and tone → overall meaning. **Always ask why.**
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Objective questions love **definitions**: sonnet, quatrain, free verse, blank verse, caesura, rhyme scheme, tone. Learn the **numbers** (14, 19, 4, 6, 8).
- For poem extracts, past papers ask for the **dominant figure of speech, the mood of the persona, the kind of imagery and the subject matter** — practise on the set poems.
- Distinguish the **persona** from the **poet**. Say "the persona laments…", not "the poet laments…", unless the poem is clearly autobiographical.
