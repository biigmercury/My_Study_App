## What Makes Drama Different

**Drama** is the genre of literature realised through **performance**. In the previous lessons, it was defined by **impersonation, action, dialogue, stage, audience, costume, mime and movement**. Drama is meant to **express and entertain**, and many plays also **educate** and **criticise**.

The coursebook lists the **features that distinguish drama** from the other genres:

| Feature | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Stage directions** | Instructions in the script (usually in italics or brackets) telling actors **how to move, speak and react**, and describing the scene |
| **Dramatis personae** | The **list of characters** at the start of the play (Latin, "persons of the drama") |
| **Performance** | A play is written **to be acted on stage** before an audience |
| **Dialogue** | The story is told mainly through **characters talking to one another** — there is usually no narrator |
| **Directions for costuming** | Notes on what characters **wear**, which reveals status, period and personality |

### Text and context: reading a play versus watching it

Drama **"cannot be considered a purely textual phenomenon"**. Plays are written to be transformed into another medium — **the theatre** — through **direction, production and performance**. So a play script has two layers:

| **The text** (main text) | **The context** (secondary text) |
|---|---|
| The words **spoken by the characters** | Everything **around** the speeches: the **dramatis personae, scene descriptions, stage directions** for acting and speaking |

| **Reading** a play | **Watching** a play |
|---|---|
| You get **first-hand written information** about how characters look, act and speak, and the setting — but you must **imagine** it all yourself | You see a version **already interpreted** by the director, actors, costume designers and make-up artists |
| Draws on **one medium**: the written text | **Multi-sensory**: music, sound effects, lighting, props and acting |

When analysing a play, remember that it was written **first and foremost for the stage**.

## Forms of Drama

| Form | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Tragedy** | One of the **oldest** forms. In ancient times, a historical drama showing the **downfall of a great man** through his **tragic flaw**. Today the definition is looser, but tragedy usually involves **serious subject matter** and the **death of one or more main characters**. |
| **Comedy** | **Light in tone**, usually with a **happy ending**, meant to make the audience **laugh**. It ranges from realistic humour drawn from everyday life to outrageous **slapstick**. |
| **Tragicomedy** | A blend: serious events with comic elements, often with a happy or ambiguous ending *(named in the textbook's genre overview)*. |
| **Farce** | A **sub-category of comedy** with **greatly exaggerated characters and situations**. Characters are **one-dimensional** and stereotyped; there are **mistaken identities, physical comedy and outrageous plot twists**. |
| **Melodrama** | Another **exaggerated** form, with **simplified, one-dimensional** characters and a formulaic story: a **villain**, a **heroine**, and a **hero** who must rescue her. |
| **Musical / opera** | The story is told through **music and dance** as well as dialogue — often comic, usually with a large cast and lavish sets and costumes. |

The textbook also mentions **ritual drama, liturgical drama and historical drama**. In **modern theatre the lines between these types are blurred**: comedy, drama and tragedy often appear **in the same play**.

<SortingGame title="Which form of drama?">

```sort
@buckets Tragedy | Comedy | Farce | Melodrama | Musical
A great king's pride leads to his downfall and death => Tragedy :: The fall of a great man through a tragic flaw.
Two friends' misunderstandings are cleared up and the play ends in a double wedding => Comedy :: Light tone and a happy ending.

A wicked landlord seizes a poor girl's house until a brave young man rescues her => Melodrama :: Villain, heroine and rescuing hero — a formula.
The story of a village festival told through songs and choreographed dances => Musical :: Music and dance carry the story.
A fisherman's mother loses every son to the sea and ends in grief => Tragedy :: Serious subject matter and death — like the set play Riders to the Sea.
```

## Tragedy and Aristotle *(beyond the textbook)*

The Greek philosopher **Aristotle**, in his ***Poetics*** (about 335 BC), gave the classic account of tragedy. Its key terms still appear in exams:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Tragic hero** | A person of **high standing** whose **flaw**, combined with external forces, leads to **suffering and downfall** — Sophocles' **Oedipus**, Shakespeare's **Macbeth**, Ola Rotimi's **Odewale** in *The Gods Are Not to Blame* |
| **Hamartia** | The **tragic flaw** or error of judgement — pride, ambition, rashness |
| **Hubris** | **Excessive pride** that defies the gods or the moral order — a common hamartia |
| **Peripeteia** | The **reversal of fortune** |
| **Anagnorisis** | **Recognition**: the moment the hero discovers the truth |
| **Catharsis** | The **purging of pity and fear** that the audience experiences |

Aristotle names **six elements of tragedy**: **plot, character, thought, diction, song and spectacle**. He ranks **plot** first, as "the soul of tragedy".

## Dramatic Structure

Plays are divided into **acts and scenes**. Many follow the five-part structure seen in earlier lessons:

<FlowDiagram title="The structure of a play">
<FlowStep title="Exposition (introduction)">
Characters and setting are introduced; we learn the background to the conflict.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Rising action (complication)">
The conflict develops and tension builds.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Climax">
The **most exciting and tense** point — the turning point of the conflict.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Falling action">
The consequences of the climax unfold.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Denouement (resolution)">
The conflict is **resolved** — happily in comedy, catastrophically in tragedy.
</FlowStep>
</FlowDiagram>

**Playwrights develop the plot through dialogue**, which also reveals characters' backgrounds and personalities. A **prologue** is a speech or section at the **beginning** of a play; an **epilogue** comes at the **end**.

### Dramatic devices *(beyond the textbook, except dialogue and mime)*

| Device | What it is | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Dialogue** | Characters **talking to each other** | Advances the plot and reveals character |
| **Monologue** | A long speech by **one character to others** | Shows a character's views at length |
| **Soliloquy** | A character **alone on stage, speaking their thoughts aloud** | Lets the audience into the character's mind — Macbeth's "Is this a dagger…" |
| **Aside** | A brief remark **to the audience**, which other characters "cannot hear" | Shares secrets and creates **dramatic irony** |
| **Dramatic irony** | The **audience knows something a character does not** | Builds suspense and tension |
| **Foreshadowing** | **Hints** about what will happen later | Creates suspense and makes events feel inevitable |
| **Mime** | A performance with **bodily movement but no words** | Expression through gesture alone |
| **Chorus** | A group that **comments on the action** (from Greek drama) | Guides the audience's response |
| **Flashback** | A scene showing **earlier events** | Fills in the background |
| **Play within a play** | A performance staged inside the main play | Comments on the main action — the players in *Hamlet* |

<SortingGame title="Name the dramatic device">

```sort
@buckets Soliloquy | Aside | Dialogue | Dramatic irony | Foreshadowing | Mime
Alone on stage, Hamlet speaks his doubts aloud: "To be, or not to be…" => Soliloquy :: A character alone, thinking aloud.
Turning to the audience while others are present, a character whispers, "Little does he know I took the money." => Aside :: A remark the other characters "do not hear".
Two characters argue about whether to sell the family land => Dialogue :: A conversation between characters.
The audience knows the cup is poisoned, but the king raises it happily to drink => Dramatic irony :: The audience knows more than the character.
In Act One, a character dreams of a coffin; in Act Three, he dies => Foreshadowing :: An early hint of later events.
An actor shows a whole fishing trip using only gestures and movement, without a word => Mime :: Performance through bodily movement alone.
```

</SortingGame>

## Space, Time and the Stage

The coursebook lists the **main features to examine when analysing a play**: **overall structure, space, time, characters (dramatis personae), dialogues, types of stage, dramatic sub-genres and dramatic techniques**.

**Types of stage** *(beyond the textbook)*:

| Stage | Audience position | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Proscenium (picture-frame)** | **In front**, looking through a framed arch | Realistic scenery; the audience watches a "picture" |
| **Thrust** | On **three sides** | Intimacy between actors and audience |
| **Arena (theatre-in-the-round)** | **All around** | Very intimate; minimal scenery — close to the **village-square performances** of African tradition |
| **Open-air / traditional space** | Around a **market square, shrine or compound** | Festivals, masquerades and the **Alarinjo** travelling theatre of the Yoruba |

**Space and time** concern where the action happens (one room? many places?) and how much time passes (one day? years?). These choices shape the play's tension. Synge's *Riders to the Sea*, for example, takes place in **one cottage kitchen** in **a short span of time**, which intensifies its tragedy.

## Drama in Africa and Nigeria *(beyond the textbook)*

- **Traditional African performance** is drama in its own right: **festivals, rituals, masquerades, storytelling with song and dance**, and the Yoruba **Alarinjo** travelling theatre. It blends **dialogue, music, dance, mime and audience participation**.
- **Modern Nigerian playwrights:** **Wole Soyinka** (*The Lion and the Jewel*, *Death and the King's Horseman*), **J. P. Clark-Bekederemo**, **Ola Rotimi** (*The Gods Are Not to Blame*), **Femi Osofisan**, **Zulu Sofola**, **Tess Onwueme** — and, from earlier GES 201 reading lists, **Adenike Akinjobi** (*Mirage*).

## How to Analyse a Play

1. **Read the dramatis personae and opening stage directions.** Who are the characters? Where and when is the play set?
2. **Follow the structure:** acts and scenes; exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, denouement.
3. **Identify the conflict(s):** person against person, against society, against nature or fate, or against themselves.
4. **Study the characters** through what they **say and do**, what **others say** about them, and the **stage directions**. Identify the protagonist, antagonist and foils.
5. **Examine dialogue and language** — diction, imagery, proverbs, figures of speech and dialect.
6. **Note dramatic techniques** — soliloquy, aside, irony, foreshadowing, symbolism, song and dance.
7. **Consider space, time and staging** — how would this look and sound on stage?
8. **State the subject matter and themes**, and support each point with **evidence** (brief quotations or references to scenes).
9. **Write in the present tense:** "Maurya **mourns**…", not "Maurya mourned…".

<Analogy>
A play script is like a **recipe**, and the performance is the **meal**. Reading the recipe tells you the ingredients (dialogue) and the method (stage directions). Only the cooking (performance) produces the taste, smell and look of the dish. When you analyse a play, read the recipe while imagining the meal.
</Analogy>

## Practice Questions

<Reveal question="1. What features distinguish drama from prose and poetry?">
Drama is realised through performance before an audience. It has stage directions (how actors move and speak, and the setting); dramatis personae (the list of characters); dialogue as the main means of telling the story, usually with no narrator; and directions for costume, alongside impersonation, action, mime and movement. A play combines the text (the spoken words) with a context (dramatis personae, scene descriptions, stage directions), and a performance adds music, lighting, sound and props.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="2. Distinguish tragedy, comedy, farce and melodrama.">
Tragedy is serious, and traditionally shows the downfall and often the death of a great person through a tragic flaw. Comedy is light in tone, aims to make the audience laugh and ends happily. Farce, a type of comedy, exaggerates characters and situations, with one-dimensional stereotypes, mistaken identities, physical comedy and wild plot twists. Melodrama is also exaggerated, with simplified characters in a formula of villain, heroine and rescuing hero. Modern plays often mix these forms.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="3. Explain these dramatic terms: soliloquy, aside, dramatic irony, catharsis, hamartia.">
A soliloquy is a character alone on stage speaking their thoughts aloud. An aside is a short remark to the audience that other characters on stage do not hear. Dramatic irony is when the audience knows something a character does not. Catharsis (Aristotle) is the purging of pity and fear that the audience experiences in tragedy. Hamartia is the tragic hero's flaw or error of judgement that leads to their downfall.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="4. Outline the steps you would follow to analyse a play.">
Read the dramatis personae and stage directions; follow the structure (acts and scenes; exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, denouement); identify the conflicts; analyse characters through their words, actions, others' comments and stage directions; examine the dialogue, language and figures of speech; note the dramatic techniques (soliloquy, aside, irony, foreshadowing, song, symbolism); consider space, time and staging; state the subject matter and themes with evidence; and write in the present tense.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
- **Drama** = literature for **performance**. Distinctive features: **stage directions, dramatis personae, performance, dialogue, costume directions**.
- **Text** (spoken words) vs **context** (dramatis personae, scene descriptions, stage directions). Reading uses **one medium**; watching is **multi-sensory** and already **interpreted**.
- **Forms:** tragedy, comedy, tragicomedy, **farce** (exaggerated comedy), **melodrama** (villain–heroine–hero), musical or opera; also ritual, liturgical and historical drama.
- **Aristotle:** tragic hero, **hamartia**, hubris, peripeteia, anagnorisis, **catharsis**; six elements, with plot first.
- **Structure:** acts and scenes; exposition → rising action → climax → falling action → **denouement**; prologue and epilogue.
- **Devices:** dialogue, monologue, **soliloquy**, **aside**, **dramatic irony**, foreshadowing, **mime**, chorus, flashback.
- **Stages:** proscenium, thrust, arena (in-the-round), open-air or traditional.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

- Past questions on drama extracts ask: **Who is the speaker? Who is spoken to? What is the subject of discussion? What does a phrase mean? What device is used?** Know your set play's characters and key scenes well enough to place any extract.
- Learn the **definitions of dialogue, soliloquy, aside, mime, denouement and dramatis personae** — frequent objective questions.
- For essays, state the **themes** and support each with **specific scenes** from the play.
