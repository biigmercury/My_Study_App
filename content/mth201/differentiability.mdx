# Differentiability

Differentiation is the process of finding the instantaneous rate of change of a function. The derivative measures exactly how fast a function changes at a point. Understanding when a derivative exists — and what it looks like when it does not — is fundamental to everything that follows in calculus.

## The Derivative from First Principles

The **derivative** of $f$ at a point $x = a$ is defined as the limit of the difference quotient:

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}$$

provided this limit exists and is finite. When this limit exists, $f$ is said to be **differentiable at $x = a$**.

The difference quotient $\dfrac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$ is the slope of the secant line through the points $(a, f(a))$ and $(a+h, f(a+h))$. As $h \to 0$, the secant line becomes the **tangent line** to the curve at $x = a$.

**Real-world analogy:** Average speed over a time interval is $\dfrac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}}$. As the time interval shrinks to zero, average speed becomes instantaneous speed — the derivative of position.

---

## Alternative Notations

The derivative of $y = f(x)$ is written in several equivalent ways:

$$f'(x) \quad = \quad y' \quad = \quad \frac{dy}{dx} \quad = \quad \frac{d}{dx}[f(x)] \quad = \quad Df(x)$$

---

## Left-Hand and Right-Hand Derivatives

Just as limits have one-sided versions, so does the derivative:

**Left-hand derivative (LHD):**
$$f'(a^-) = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}$$

**Right-hand derivative (RHD):**
$$f'(a^+) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}$$

**$f$ is differentiable at $a$** if and only if:
$$f'(a^-) = f'(a^+)$$

(both exist and are equal)

This is directly analogous to how a two-sided limit requires matching one-sided limits.

---

## Differentiability on an Interval

- $f$ is **differentiable on an open interval $(a, b)$** if it is differentiable at every point in $(a, b)$.
- $f$ is **differentiable on a closed interval $[a, b]$** if it is differentiable on $(a, b)$ and additionally the right-hand derivative exists at $a$ and the left-hand derivative exists at $b$.

---

## Continuity vs Differentiability

These two properties are closely related but not equivalent:

**Theorem:** If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$.

$$\text{Differentiable at } a \implies \text{Continuous at } a$$

The **converse is false**: a function can be continuous at a point without being differentiable there.

**Classic counterexample:** $f(x) = |x|$ at $x = 0$.

- $f(0) = 0$. ✓
- $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} |x| = 0 = f(0)$, so $f$ is continuous at $0$. ✓
- LHD: $\displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0^-} \dfrac{|h|}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \dfrac{-h}{h} = -1$
- RHD: $\displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0^+} \dfrac{|h|}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \dfrac{h}{h} = +1$

LHD $\neq$ RHD, so $f$ is **not differentiable at $x = 0$**, even though it is continuous there.

## When is a Function NOT Differentiable?

A function fails to be differentiable at $a$ when the graph has:

| Situation | Description |
|---|---|
| Sharp corner or cusp | LHD and RHD exist but are unequal (e.g., $|x|$ at $x=0$) |
| Vertical tangent | Derivative is $\pm\infty$ (limit does not exist as a finite number) |
| Jump discontinuity | Function is not even continuous — derivative cannot exist |
| Oscillation | Limit of difference quotient does not exist |

---

## Standard Differentiation Rules

Once differentiability is established, use these rules to find derivatives:

| Rule | Formula |
|---|---|
| Constant | $\dfrac{d}{dx}[c] = 0$ |
| Power | $\dfrac{d}{dx}[x^n] = nx^{n-1}$ |
| Constant multiple | $\dfrac{d}{dx}[cf(x)] = cf'(x)$ |
| Sum/Difference | $\dfrac{d}{dx}[f \pm g] = f' \pm g'$ |
| Product | $\dfrac{d}{dx}[fg] = f'g + fg'$ |
| Quotient | $\dfrac{d}{dx}\!\left[\dfrac{f}{g}\right] = \dfrac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}$ |
| Chain | $\dfrac{d}{dx}[f(g(x))] = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$ |

---

## Common Derivatives

$$\frac{d}{dx}[\sin x] = \cos x \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[\cos x] = -\sin x \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[\tan x] = \sec^2 x$$

$$\frac{d}{dx}[e^x] = e^x \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[a^x] = a^x \ln a \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[\ln x] = \frac{1}{x}$$

$$\frac{d}{dx}[\sqrt{x}] = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[x^{1/n}] = \frac{1}{n}x^{(1/n)-1}$$

## Example 1 — Derivative from First Principles

**Problem:** Find $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = x^2 + 3x$ using the definition.

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$$

**Expand $f(x + h)$:**
$$f(x + h) = (x+h)^2 + 3(x+h) = x^2 + 2xh + h^2 + 3x + 3h$$

**Difference:**
$$f(x+h) - f(x) = 2xh + h^2 + 3h = h(2x + h + 3)$$

**Divide by $h$ and take limit:**
$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h(2x + h + 3)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}(2x + h + 3) = 2x + 3$$

---

## Example 2 — Checking Differentiability of a Piecewise Function

**Problem:** Is $f$ differentiable at $x = 1$?

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 & x \leq 1 \\ 2x - 1 & x > 1 \end{cases}$$

**Step 1: Check continuity first.**

$f(1) = 1^2 = 1$

$\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 1^-} x^2 = 1$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 1^+} (2x - 1) = 1$. Both sides agree and equal $f(1)$. ✓ Continuous at $x = 1$.

**Step 2: Check LHD and RHD.**

$$\text{LHD} = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{f(1+h) - f(1)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{(1+h)^2 - 1}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{2h + h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^-}(2 + h) = 2$$

$$\text{RHD} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{f(1+h) - f(1)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{[2(1+h) - 1] - 1}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{2h}{h} = 2$$

LHD $=$ RHD $= 2$. **$f$ is differentiable at $x = 1$**, with $f'(1) = 2$.

---

## Example 3 — Non-Differentiable Piecewise Function

**Problem:** Is $g$ differentiable at $x = 0$?

$$g(x) = \begin{cases} 3x + 1 & x \geq 0 \\ x^2 + 1 & x < 0 \end{cases}$$

**Step 1: Check continuity at $x = 0$.**

$g(0) = 3(0) + 1 = 1$

$\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0^+} (3x + 1) = 1$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0^-} (x^2 + 1) = 1$. Continuous. ✓

**Step 2: Compute one-sided derivatives.**

$$\text{LHD} = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{g(h) - g(0)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{(h^2 + 1) - 1}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^-} h = 0$$

$$\text{RHD} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{g(h) - g(0)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{(3h + 1) - 1}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} 3 = 3$$

LHD $= 0 \neq 3 =$ RHD. **$g$ is not differentiable at $x = 0$** — there is a sharp corner at $(0, 1)$.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The derivative $f'(a)$ is the limit of the difference quotient as $h \to 0$.
- $f$ is differentiable at $a$ when LHD $=$ RHD $=$ finite number.
- Differentiability implies continuity, but continuity does not imply differentiability.
- Piecewise functions: check continuity first (necessary), then check LHD vs RHD.
- Non-differentiable points arise at corners, cusps, vertical tangents, or discontinuities.

---

## Exam Tips

- For "show $f$ is differentiable at $x = a$": you must check continuity AND show LHD $=$ RHD.
- For "find $f'(x)$ from first principles": expand $f(x+h)$, subtract $f(x)$, factor out $h$, cancel, then take $h \to 0$.
- At UI, piecewise differentiability is a major exam topic — always compute both LHD and RHD; never just use the derivative formula directly without checking.
- If LHD $\neq$ RHD, the function is not differentiable there — state this clearly and identify it as a corner.
- Continuity is a necessary condition for differentiability. If continuity fails, differentiability fails automatically — no need to compute derivatives.
