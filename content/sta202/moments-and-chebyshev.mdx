# Moments and Chebyshev's Theorem

The mean and variance describe where a distribution is centred and how spread out it is. But they only tell part of the story. **Moments** are a family of descriptive measures that capture additional features of a distribution — its asymmetry (skewness) and the heaviness of its tails (kurtosis). **Chebyshev's theorem** then gives a universal guarantee about how much data must lie close to the mean — for any distribution whatsoever.

---

## Moments of a Random Variable

A **moment** is an expected value of a power of $X$ (or a centred version of $X$). There are two types: moments about the origin, and moments about the mean.

### Moments About the Origin (Raw Moments)

The **$r$-th moment about the origin** (also called the $r$-th raw moment) is:

$$\mu'_r = E(X^r) = \begin{cases}
\displaystyle\sum_{\text{all } x} x^r f(x) & \text{discrete} \\[10pt]
\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^r f(x)\, dx & \text{continuous}
\end{cases}$$

The **first raw moment** is just the mean: $\mu'_1 = E(X) = \mu$.

The **second raw moment** $\mu'_2 = E(X^2)$ is used to compute variance: $Var(X) = \mu'_2 - (\mu'_1)^2$.

### Moments About the Mean (Central Moments)

The **$r$-th moment about the mean** (central moment) is:

$$\mu_r = E\left[(X - \mu)^r\right] = \begin{cases}
\displaystyle\sum_{\text{all } x} (x - \mu)^r f(x) & \text{discrete} \\[10pt]
\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (x - \mu)^r f(x)\, dx & \text{continuous}
\end{cases}$$

Key central moments:
- $\mu_1 = E(X - \mu) = 0$ — always zero (deviations from the mean cancel)
- $\mu_2 = E[(X - \mu)^2] = Var(X) = \sigma^2$ — the variance
- $\mu_3$ — related to **skewness** (asymmetry of the distribution)
- $\mu_4$ — related to **kurtosis** (how heavy-tailed the distribution is)

### Relationship Between Raw and Central Moments

The central moments can be expressed in terms of raw moments. These are the formulas you may need to derive or apply in exams:

$$\mu_1 = 0$$

$$\mu_2 = \mu'_2 - (\mu'_1)^2$$

$$\mu_3 = \mu'_3 - 3\mu'_2 \mu'_1 + 2(\mu'_1)^3$$

$$\mu_4 = \mu'_4 - 4\mu'_3 \mu'_1 + 6\mu'_2 (\mu'_1)^2 - 3(\mu'_1)^4$$

The general pattern uses the **binomial theorem** to expand $(X - \mu)^r = (X - \mu'_1)^r$.

## Worked Example 1: Computing Raw and Central Moments

A continuous random variable $X$ has PDF:

$$f(x) = \frac{3}{4}x(2-x), \quad 0 < x < 2 \quad (\text{zero elsewhere})$$

Verify: $\frac{3}{4}\int_0^2 x(2-x)\, dx = \frac{3}{4}\left[x^2 - \frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^2 = \frac{3}{4}\left(4 - \frac{8}{3}\right) = \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{4}{3} = 1$ ✓

**First raw moment $\mu'_1 = E(X)$:**
$$\mu'_1 = \frac{3}{4}\int_0^2 x^2(2-x)\, dx = \frac{3}{4}\int_0^2 (2x^2 - x^3)\, dx = \frac{3}{4}\left[\frac{2x^3}{3} - \frac{x^4}{4}\right]_0^2 = \frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{16}{3} - 4\right) = \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{4}{3} = 1$$

**Second raw moment $\mu'_2 = E(X^2)$:**
$$\mu'_2 = \frac{3}{4}\int_0^2 x^3(2-x)\, dx = \frac{3}{4}\int_0^2 (2x^3 - x^4)\, dx = \frac{3}{4}\left[\frac{x^4}{2} - \frac{x^5}{5}\right]_0^2 = \frac{3}{4}\left(8 - \frac{32}{5}\right) = \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{8}{5} = \frac{6}{5}$$

**Third raw moment $\mu'_3 = E(X^3)$:**
$$\mu'_3 = \frac{3}{4}\int_0^2 x^4(2-x)\, dx = \frac{3}{4}\int_0^2 (2x^4 - x^5)\, dx = \frac{3}{4}\left[\frac{2x^5}{5} - \frac{x^6}{6}\right]_0^2 = \frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{64}{5} - \frac{64}{6}\right) = \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{64}{30} = \frac{8}{5}$$

**Fourth raw moment $\mu'_4 = E(X^4)$:**
$$\mu'_4 = \frac{3}{4}\int_0^2 x^5(2-x)\, dx = \frac{3}{4}\int_0^2 (2x^5 - x^6)\, dx = \frac{3}{4}\left[\frac{x^6}{3} - \frac{x^7}{7}\right]_0^2 = \frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{64}{3} - \frac{128}{7}\right) = \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{64}{21} = \frac{16}{7}$$

**Deriving central moments:**

$\mu_1 = 0$ (always)

$\mu_2 = \mu'_2 - (\mu'_1)^2 = \dfrac{6}{5} - 1 = \dfrac{1}{5}$

$\mu_3 = \mu'_3 - 3\mu'_2\mu'_1 + 2(\mu'_1)^3 = \dfrac{8}{5} - 3\left(\dfrac{6}{5}\right)(1) + 2(1) = \dfrac{8}{5} - \dfrac{18}{5} + \dfrac{10}{5} = 0$

The third central moment is zero because this distribution is symmetric about $\mu = 1$.

## Worked Example 2: First Four Moments for a Discrete Distribution

A discrete random variable $X$ has PMF:

| $x$ | -1 | 0 | 1 |
|-----|-----|---|---|
| $f(x)$ | 1/4 | 1/2 | 1/4 |

**First four raw moments:**

$\mu'_1 = E(X) = (-1)(1/4) + (0)(1/2) + (1)(1/4) = -1/4 + 0 + 1/4 = 0$

$\mu'_2 = E(X^2) = (-1)^2(1/4) + (0)^2(1/2) + (1)^2(1/4) = 1/4 + 0 + 1/4 = 1/2$

$\mu'_3 = E(X^3) = (-1)^3(1/4) + 0 + (1)^3(1/4) = -1/4 + 1/4 = 0$

$\mu'_4 = E(X^4) = (-1)^4(1/4) + 0 + (1)^4(1/4) = 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2$

**Central moments** (since $\mu = \mu'_1 = 0$, raw and central moments are the same here):

$\mu_1 = 0, \quad \mu_2 = 1/2, \quad \mu_3 = 0, \quad \mu_4 = 1/2$

## Worked Example 3: Deriving Central from Raw Moments

A random variable has: $\mu'_1 = 2$, $\mu'_2 = 5$, $\mu'_3 = 14$, $\mu'_4 = 46$.

Find the first four central moments.

$\mu_1 = 0$ (always)

$\mu_2 = \mu'_2 - (\mu'_1)^2 = 5 - 4 = 1$

$\mu_3 = \mu'_3 - 3\mu'_2\mu'_1 + 2(\mu'_1)^3 = 14 - 3(5)(2) + 2(8) = 14 - 30 + 16 = 0$

$\mu_4 = \mu'_4 - 4\mu'_3\mu'_1 + 6\mu'_2(\mu'_1)^2 - 3(\mu'_1)^4$
$= 46 - 4(14)(2) + 6(5)(4) - 3(16)$
$= 46 - 112 + 120 - 48 = 6$

## Skewness and Kurtosis

Moments give us descriptors beyond the mean and variance:

### Skewness (Third Standardised Moment)
$$\beta_1 = \frac{\mu_3}{\sigma^3} = \frac{\mu_3}{(\mu_2)^{3/2}}$$

- $\beta_1 = 0$: distribution is symmetric about the mean
- $\beta_1 > 0$: positively skewed (right tail is longer)
- $\beta_1 < 0$: negatively skewed (left tail is longer)

### Kurtosis (Fourth Standardised Moment)
$$\beta_2 = \frac{\mu_4}{\sigma^4} = \frac{\mu_4}{(\mu_2)^2}$$

- $\beta_2 = 3$: normal distribution (mesokurtic)
- $\beta_2 > 3$: leptokurtic (heavy tails, sharper peak)
- $\beta_2 < 3$: platykurtic (lighter tails, flatter peak)

## Chebyshev's Theorem

Chebyshev's theorem is remarkable: it makes a probabilistic guarantee about **any distribution** without knowing its shape. It says that for any random variable with finite mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$:

$$\boxed{P(\left|X - \mu\right| \geq k\sigma) \leq \frac{1}{k^2}}$$

Equivalently:

$$\boxed{P(\left|X - \mu\right| < k\sigma) \geq 1 - \frac{1}{k^2}}$$

**Reading:** The probability that $X$ falls **within $k$ standard deviations of the mean** is at least $1 - \frac{1}{k^2}$, for any $k > 1$.

> **Analogy:** Imagine you're told someone's height but you don't know whether they're from a tall or short population. Chebyshev's theorem says: regardless of the distribution of heights, **at least 75%** of the population falls within 2 standard deviations of the mean (since $1 - 1/4 = 3/4 = 75\%$). It's a conservative but universally valid guarantee.

| $k$ | Guaranteed % within $k\sigma$ of mean |
|-----|---------------------------------------|
| $k = 2$ | at least $1 - 1/4 = 75\%$ |
| $k = 3$ | at least $1 - 1/9 \approx 88.9\%$ |
| $k = 4$ | at least $1 - 1/16 = 93.75\%$ |
| $k = 5$ | at least $1 - 1/25 = 96\%$ |

### Worked Example 4: Applying Chebyshev's Theorem

A random variable $X$ has mean $\mu = 12$ and variance $\sigma^2 = 9$ (so $\sigma = 3$). Find the probability that $X$ ranges between 6 and 18 using Chebyshev's theorem.

**Step 1:** Identify the interval $[6, 18]$ in terms of $k\sigma$ from the mean:
$$6 = \mu - k\sigma = 12 - 3k \implies k = 2$$
$$18 = \mu + k\sigma = 12 + 3(2) = 18 \checkmark$$

So we want $P(6 \leq X \leq 18) = P(|X - 12| \leq 6) = P(|X - 12| < 2 \times 3)$

**Step 2:** Apply Chebyshev's theorem with $k = 2$:
$$P(|X - \mu| < k\sigma) \geq 1 - \frac{1}{k^2} = 1 - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{4} = 0.75$$

Therefore, $P(6 \leq X \leq 18) \geq 0.75$. At least 75% of the values lie in this interval.

### Worked Example 5: Finding $k$ from an Interval

A random variable has $\mu = 50$ and $\sigma^2 = 25$. Use Chebyshev's theorem to find the minimum probability that $X$ falls between 35 and 65.

$\sigma = 5$.

$|65 - 50| = 15 = k \times 5 \implies k = 3$

$P(35 \leq X \leq 65) \geq 1 - \frac{1}{9} = \frac{8}{9} \approx 0.889$

---

> **Key Takeaways**
>
> - **Raw moments:** $\mu'_r = E(X^r)$. The first raw moment is the mean; the second raw moment is used to compute variance.
> - **Central moments:** $\mu_r = E[(X-\mu)^r]$. The second central moment is the variance. $\mu_1 = 0$ always.
> - The shortcut formula $\mu_2 = \mu'_2 - (\mu'_1)^2$ converts between raw and central moments
> - Higher moments ($\mu_3$, $\mu_4$) describe skewness and kurtosis — the shape of the distribution
> - **Chebyshev's theorem:** $P(|X - \mu| < k\sigma) \geq 1 - 1/k^2$ — works for **any** distribution with finite mean and variance; gives a lower bound on the probability of being within $k$ standard deviations

---

> **Exam Tips**
>
> - **Exam questions on moments** typically ask: "Find the first four moments about the origin, and the first four moments about the mean." Do them in this order — compute all four $\mu'_r$ first, then convert to $\mu_r$ using the formulas.
> - **The conversion formulas are testable in "show that" format.** The key one is $\mu_3 = \mu'_3 - 3\mu'_2\mu'_1 + 2(\mu'_1)^3$ — derive it from the binomial expansion of $(X - \mu'_1)^3$.
> - For **Chebyshev's theorem**, the question will give you $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ (or $\sigma$) and an interval. Your job is to find $k$ by computing how many standard deviations from the mean the endpoints are, then apply $1 - 1/k^2$.
> - Chebyshev gives a **lower bound** — the actual probability may be higher. If the distribution is known to be normal, you can use the empirical rule (68-95-99.7) for a tighter bound.
> - A common trap: if asked for $P(|X - \mu| \geq k\sigma)$, the answer is $\leq 1/k^2$ (the probability of being **far** from the mean is small). Don't confuse the direction.
