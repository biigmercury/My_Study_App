# Descriptive Statistics

Once you have collected data, the very first thing you need to do is **describe it**. Descriptive statistics are the tools that let you summarise and communicate a dataset concisely — what is the centre? How spread out are the values? What does a typical observation look like?

Think of descriptive statistics as making a "portrait" of your data. Before you draw any conclusions or run any tests, you need to understand what you are actually working with.

---

## Measures of Central Tendency

A measure of central tendency tells you where the **middle** of your data is — the "typical" or "representative" value. There are three: mean, median, and mode.

### 1. The Mean (Average)

The mean is the sum of all values divided by the number of values. It is the most widely used measure of centre.

**For a population (all $N$ values):**
$$\mu = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} X_i}{N}$$

**For a sample (a subset of $n$ values):**
$$\bar{x} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i}{n}$$

Where:
- $\mu$ (mu) = population mean
- $\bar{x}$ (x-bar) = sample mean
- $\Sigma X$ = sum of all values
- $N$ = number of values in the population
- $n$ = number of values in the sample

> **Analogy:** Imagine 5 people sharing a prize pool. The mean is what each person would get if the money were split equally. It balances the total.

**Strengths:** Uses every data point; best for symmetric data.  
**Weakness:** Heavily affected by extreme values (outliers).

### 2. The Median

The median is the **middle value** when data is arranged in order from smallest to largest. It divides the distribution exactly in half: 50% of values are below it, 50% above it.

**To find the median:**
1. Sort the data in ascending order
2. If $n$ is **odd**: the median is the middle value — position $\frac{n+1}{2}$
3. If $n$ is **even**: the median is the average of the two middle values — positions $\frac{n}{2}$ and $\frac{n}{2}+1$

> **Analogy:** If 5 people stand in a line ordered by height, the median is the height of the person standing in the middle (3rd position). Two people are shorter, two are taller.

**Strength:** Not affected by outliers. It is the preferred measure for skewed data (e.g., income distributions, house prices).

### 3. The Mode

The mode is the value that **appears most frequently** in a dataset. A dataset can have:
- **No mode** (all values appear equally)
- **One mode** (unimodal)
- **Two modes** (bimodal)
- **More than two modes** (multimodal)

> **Analogy:** If you sell shoes, the mode is the size you sell most often. It is the most "popular" value.

**Strength:** Can be used with nominal data (e.g., "most common blood type").

## Order of Operations in Statistical Formulas

Statistical formulas often involve multiple operations on the same data. Getting the **order of operations** wrong is one of the most common sources of calculation errors. Dr Oyamakin's course follows this specific order:

1. **Parentheses first**: everything inside $( \; )$ is done first
2. **Exponents second**: squaring or raising to other powers
3. **Multiplication and Division third**: left to right
4. **Summation ($\Sigma$) fourth**: add up the values
5. **Addition and Subtraction last**: left to right

The key conflict that trips students up is the difference between:

$$\Sigma X^2 \quad \text{vs} \quad (\Sigma X)^2$$

- $\Sigma X^2$: **Square first, then sum.** Apply the exponent to each $X$ individually, then add them up.
- $(\Sigma X)^2$: **Sum first, then square.** Add up all the $X$ values first, then square the total.

These almost always give different answers. A variance formula will typically use both.

---

## Worked Example 1: Mean, Median, Mode

**Data:** Test scores of 7 students: $\{45, 62, 62, 70, 74, 81, 90\}$

**Step 1: Sort the data.** Already sorted: $45, 62, 62, 70, 74, 81, 90$

**Mean:**
$$\bar{x} = \frac{45 + 62 + 62 + 70 + 74 + 81 + 90}{7} = \frac{484}{7} \approx 69.14$$

**Median:**
$n = 7$ (odd), so median is at position $\frac{7+1}{2} = 4\text{th}$ position.
The 4th value is $\mathbf{70}$.
$$\text{Median} = 70$$

**Mode:**
The value $62$ appears twice; all others appear once.
$$\text{Mode} = 62$$

---

## Worked Example 2: Computing $\Sigma X$, $\Sigma X^2$, $(\Sigma X)^2$

**Data:** $X = \{2, 4, 6, 8\}$

**$\Sigma X$ (sum of all values):**
$$\Sigma X = 2 + 4 + 6 + 8 = 20$$

**$\Sigma X^2$ (square each value first, then sum):**
$$\Sigma X^2 = 2^2 + 4^2 + 6^2 + 8^2 = 4 + 16 + 36 + 64 = 120$$

**$(\Sigma X)^2$ (sum first, then square):**
$$(\Sigma X)^2 = (20)^2 = 400$$

Notice: $120 \neq 400$.

---

## Worked Example 3: Even-Numbered Dataset

**Data:** Daily temperature readings (°C): $\{18, 21, 23, 25, 27, 30\}$

$n = 6$ (even).

**Mean:**
$$\bar{x} = \frac{18 + 21 + 23 + 25 + 27 + 30}{6} = \frac{144}{6} = 24 \text{°C}$$

**Median:**
For $n=6$ (even), median is the average of the $\frac{6}{2} = 3\text{rd}$ and $\frac{6}{2}+1 = 4\text{th}$ values.

3rd value: 23, 4th value: 25.
$$\text{Median} = \frac{23 + 25}{2} = \frac{48}{2} = 24 \text{°C}$$

In this case, mean and median happen to agree — this suggests the data is fairly symmetric.

**Mode:** Each value appears once, so there is **no mode**.

## Measures of Dispersion (Spread)

Central tendency tells you where the data is centred. But two datasets can have the same mean while being completely different in how spread out they are. We need **measures of dispersion**.

### Range
$$\text{Range} = \text{Maximum} - \text{Minimum}$$

Simple but sensitive to outliers — one extreme value can make the range misleadingly large.

### Variance

Variance measures how far each value is from the mean, on average. We square the differences to avoid positive and negative deviations cancelling each other out.

**Population variance:**
$$\sigma^2 = \frac{\Sigma(X - \mu)^2}{N}$$

**Sample variance:**
$$s^2 = \frac{\Sigma(x - \bar{x})^2}{n - 1}$$

We divide by $n-1$ (not $n$) for sample variance because using $n$ would systematically underestimate the true population variance. The $n-1$ is called the **degrees of freedom**.

**Computational shortcut (equivalent formula for sample variance):**
$$s^2 = \frac{\Sigma x^2 - \frac{(\Sigma x)^2}{n}}{n-1}$$

This shortcut is much faster when you have the raw data.

### Standard Deviation

The standard deviation is simply the **square root of the variance**. It brings the units back to the original scale of measurement.

$$\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2} \quad \text{(population)}$$
$$s = \sqrt{s^2} \quad \text{(sample)}$$

> **Analogy:** If you measure incomes in dollars, variance is in "dollars squared" — not very interpretable. Standard deviation converts it back to dollars. A standard deviation of ₦5,000 is immediately understandable; a variance of 25,000,000 ₦² is not.

---

## Worked Example 4: Variance and Standard Deviation

**Data:** Voltage readings from 5 circuits (Volts): $\{10, 12, 14, 16, 18\}$

**Step 1: Find the sample mean:**
$$\bar{x} = \frac{10+12+14+16+18}{5} = \frac{70}{5} = 14 \text{ V}$$

**Step 2: Find each deviation from the mean $(x - \bar{x})$ and square it:**

| $x$ | $x - \bar{x}$ | $(x - \bar{x})^2$ |
|-----|--------------|------------------|
| 10  | $10 - 14 = -4$ | 16 |
| 12  | $12 - 14 = -2$ | 4 |
| 14  | $14 - 14 = 0$ | 0 |
| 16  | $16 - 14 = 2$ | 4 |
| 18  | $18 - 14 = 4$ | 16 |
| **Sum** | **0** | **40** |

The sum of deviations is always 0 — a useful check that your mean is correct.

**Step 3: Sample variance:**
$$s^2 = \frac{\Sigma(x - \bar{x})^2}{n-1} = \frac{40}{5-1} = \frac{40}{4} = 10 \text{ V}^2$$

**Step 4: Standard deviation:**
$$s = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16 \text{ V}$$

**Verify with computational shortcut:**
$\Sigma x^2 = 100 + 144 + 196 + 256 + 324 = 1020$
$(\Sigma x)^2 = (70)^2 = 4900$

$$s^2 = \frac{1020 - \frac{4900}{5}}{4} = \frac{1020 - 980}{4} = \frac{40}{4} = 10 \checkmark$$

---

> **Key Takeaways**
>
> - **Mean**: Sum ÷ Count. Uses all values. Best for symmetric data. Pulled by outliers.
> - **Median**: Middle value when sorted. Not affected by outliers. Best for skewed data.
> - **Mode**: Most frequent value. The only measure usable for nominal data.
> - **Order of operations**: Always square before summing for $\Sigma X^2$; sum before squaring for $(\Sigma X)^2$. These are different.
> - **Standard deviation** tells you how far a "typical" value sits from the mean — the bigger it is, the more spread out your data.
> - **Sample variance uses $n-1$** in the denominator (degrees of freedom). Using $n$ instead is a common exam error.

---

> **Exam Tips**
>
> - When computing the mean from a **frequency table**, multiply each value by its frequency, sum those products, then divide by the total frequency: $\bar{x} = \frac{\Sigma f \cdot x}{\Sigma f}$.
> - For **grouped data**, use the midpoint of each class interval as your $x$ value.
> - The exam will often give you a list of values and ask for mean, median, **and** mode in the same question. Sort the data first — it makes finding the median and mode immediate.
> - **Which measure to use?** If the data is skewed or has outliers (e.g., income, house prices), use the median. If it is roughly symmetric and no extreme outliers, use the mean.
> - Lecturers at UI frequently test **$\Sigma X^2$** and **$(\Sigma X)^2$** in the same calculation. Always label which you are computing. A common trap question: "Compute $\Sigma X^2 - \frac{(\Sigma X)^2}{n}$" — the subtraction happens at the end, not inside the summation.
> - The **computational shortcut for variance** is faster in exams than the definition formula. Memorise: $s^2 = \frac{\Sigma x^2 - \frac{(\Sigma x)^2}{n}}{n-1}$
