## Two Workers, One Job

| Worker A | Worker B |
|---|---|
| Adjustable chair, **monitor at eye level**, wrists straight on a proper desk | Laptop **balanced on knees** at a kitchen table, **hunched** toward a small, glare-covered screen |

Both are "using computers". Yet their **comfort, productivity and long-term health** will differ enormously. That difference is **physical ergonomics** — when **the body, not the code**, decides whether an interaction succeeds.

**Did you know?** "Ergonomics" comes from the Greek **ergon (work)** and **nomos (natural laws)** — literally *"the laws of work"* — first proposed by Polish scientist **Wojciech Jastrzębowski in 1857**, long before computers existed.

## 4.1 Workspace Design

> **Workspace design** is the deliberate arrangement of **desk, chair, monitor, keyboard and mouse** for comfortable, efficient and safe use — **a small system in which every part affects every other**.

**Why it matters:** workers may spend **6–10 hours daily** at a workstation; **small misalignments compound into chronic pain** over months.

### ISO 9241-5: Five Core Principles

<FlowDiagram title="ISO 9241-5 workstation principles">
<FlowStep title="Neutral posture access">
**No excessive reaching, twisting or bending** to use the equipment.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Adjustability">
**Chairs, desks and monitor arms fit a range of body sizes** — one fixed setup can't fit a 1.5 m and a 1.9 m user.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Sufficient clearance">
**Adequate leg room and desk depth** — knees must fit under the desk.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Appropriate sightlines">
**Top of the monitor at or below eye level**, **50–70 cm** away.
</FlowStep>
<FlowStep title="Organised reach zones">
**Frequently used items within easy reach**; rarely used items farther away.
</FlowStep>
</FlowDiagram>

![Side view of an ergonomic workstation: seated person with elbows, hips and knees at about 90 degrees, feet flat, lower back supported, forearms level with the keyboard, eyes level with the top of the screen at 50 to 70 centimetres, and desk height 68 to 75 centimetres with leg clearance](/images/ins202/ergonomic-workstation.svg)

*Figure: An ISO 9241-5-style workstation.*

- **The "90-90-90 rule":** **elbows ≈ 90°**, **knees ≈ 90°** with feet flat, **hips ≈ 90°** relative to the torso — a **starting heuristic, not a rigid law**.
- **Desk height:** elbows rest at about 90° with **forearms parallel to the floor** — typically **68–75 cm** from the floor.
- **Remember:** comfort over rigid angles — adjust to what feels **sustainable for your own body**.

### When Setup Goes Wrong — and Right

- **"Text neck" (real-life):** a student studying with a **laptop flat on a bed** tilts their head forward **30–45°**. This can **more than triple the effective load on the neck** compared with neutral posture (Hansraj, 2014).
- **Industry:** banks running large **call centres** employ certified ergonomic assessors and provide **headsets, height-adjustable chairs and monitor arms** — because **one repetitive strain injury (RSI) claim can cost more than equipping an entire team**.
- **Common mistake:** using a **laptop alone as a primary display**. Because the screen and keyboard are joined, users **can't get good hand posture and good neck posture at the same time**. Fix: raise the laptop on a stand and add an **external keyboard and mouse**.

<Reveal question="Quick quiz: a laptop used flat on a bed for hours mainly risks which body region? A) Ankles B) Neck and cervical spine C) Ears D) Fingertips only">
**B — Neck and cervical spine.** Looking down at a flat laptop tilts the head forward 30–45°, multiplying the load on the neck ("text neck").
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="Think–pair–share: audit the last place you studied on a laptop for over an hour. Which ISO 9241-5 principle did it violate most, and what's one low-cost fix?">
A typical hostel setup — laptop on a low table, sitting on the bed — violates **appropriate sightlines** (screen far below eye level) and **neutral posture access** (hunching forward). **Low-cost fix:** raise the laptop on a stack of textbooks so the top of the screen is near eye level, and use a cheap external keyboard and mouse at elbow height. A folded towel behind the lower back adds support.
</Reveal>

## 4.2 Keyboard Design

### QWERTY and Path Dependence

The **QWERTY** layout was designed in the **1870s** to **reduce mechanical jamming** on typewriters by separating commonly paired letters (Yamada, 1980) — **not to slow typists down**, as the popular myth says. It persists today through sheer adoption — a pattern economists call **"path dependence"**: once everyone has learned it, switching costs outweigh any benefit.

**Historical note:** the **Dvorak Simplified Keyboard (1930s)** put frequent letters on the home row to reduce finger travel — but evidence for its ergonomic superiority over QWERTY **remains mixed**, showing that **habituation matters as much as theoretical efficiency**.

### Ergonomic Keyboard Features

| Feature | What it means | Why it helps |
|---|---|---|
| **Key travel** | How far a key moves when pressed | **Short** (laptops): less finger movement but less tactile feedback. **Long** (mechanical): more feedback but more force |
| **Split and angled layouts** | Keys divided into two angled halves | Each hand approaches at a **natural angle** |
| **Negative tilt** | Keyboard slopes **down at the back** | Reduces **wrist extension** |
| **Wrist rests** | Padded support in front of the keys | Support the **palm during pauses only — never while actively typing** |

![A split and rotated keyboard layout: the left-hand and right-hand halves of the QWERTY keys are separated and angled outward](/images/ins202/split-keyboard-layout.png)

*Figure: A split, rotated keyboard layout — each half angles toward the hand that uses it. Image: Mliu92, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.*

**Why it matters:** poor keyboard positioning forces **ulnar deviation** (bending the wrist toward the little finger) or **wrist extension** — both **raise pressure in the carpal tunnel** and are strongly linked to **RSI** (Topic 10).

- **Real-life example:** a journalist typing **six hours daily** on a compact laptop keyboard develops finger and forearm fatigue. Switching to an **external mechanical keyboard at elbow height**, with a wrist rest used only during breaks, **measurably reduces discomfort within weeks**.
- **Industry example:** Microsoft (**Sculpt** series) and Logitech (**Ergo** series) sell split, curved, negatively tilted keyboards for developers and transcriptionists.

## 4.3 Mouse Design

### Grip Styles and Ergonomic Mouse Types

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Grip styles">

| Grip | Description |
|---|---|
| **Palm grip** | The **whole hand rests** on the mouse |
| **Claw grip** | The **palm rests at the rear**; fingers **arch** to click |
| **Fingertip grip** | **Only the fingertips** touch; the palm is raised |

A mouse should match the user's **hand size and grip style** — a mismatch is a major contributor to wrist and forearm strain.

</Tab>
<Tab label="Vertical mouse">

![A vertical mouse held in an upright handshake position](/images/ins202/vertical-mouse.jpg)

*Image: TaurusEmerald, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Holds the hand in a **"handshake" position**, reducing **forearm pronation** (twisting the palm flat).

</Tab>
<Tab label="Trackball">

![A trackball with a large ball surrounded by buttons, which stays stationary on the desk](/images/ins202/trackball.jpg)

*Image: Bobulous, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.*

The **hand stays stationary**; the thumb or fingers **roll a ball**. Minimal arm movement.

</Tab>
<Tab label="Trackpad">

**Finger gestures on a flat surface** — no extra hardware, built into laptops. Convenient, but heavy use causes **repetitive finger strain**.

</Tab>
</Tabs>

- **Real-life example:** a **graphic designer** using a standard mouse for detailed editing **10 hours daily** develops lateral forearm pain from sustained **pronation**. Switching to a **vertical mouse**, with the forearm in a neutral thumb-up orientation, relieves the pressure.
- **Industry example:** esports manufacturers (Logitech, Razer) make mice with **adjustable weights, customisable buttons and grip-specific shapes** for multi-hour sessions.

<Reveal question="Quick quiz: tingling in the thumb, index and middle fingers after mouse use is an early warning sign of… A) Digital eye strain B) Nerve compression C) Path dependence D) Divided attention">
**B — Nerve compression** — specifically the **median nerve** in the carpal tunnel, the early sign of **carpal tunnel syndrome** (Topic 10).
</Reveal>

### Table 4.1 — Ergonomic Comparison of Input Devices

| Device type | Primary benefit | Primary risk | Best suited for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard flat keyboard | Familiar, widely compatible | Wrist extension/ulnar deviation over time | Occasional to moderate typing |
| Split/ergonomic keyboard | Reduces wrist deviation | Learning curve, higher cost | Heavy typists (developers, writers) |
| Standard mouse | Precise, widely available | Sustained forearm pronation | General office tasks |
| Vertical mouse | Neutral (handshake) forearm | Adjustment period; less precise at first | Users with forearm/wrist pain |
| Trackball | Minimal arm movement | Needs fine thumb/finger control | Confined desks, limited arm mobility |
| Touchpad/trackpad | Compact, no extra hardware | Repetitive finger strain with heavy use | Laptop users, mobile professionals |

<Reveal question="Think–pair–share: a product says 'ergonomic' on the box with no evidence. How would you evaluate that claim?">
Ask: **What problem does it claim to solve** (wrist extension, pronation)? Is there **independent evidence** — peer-reviewed studies, occupational-health or ergonomics-association recommendations, or published test results — rather than marketing words? Does it **fit your body and task** (hand size, grip style, how many hours you type)? Can you **try it** for a few weeks and track discomfort? "Ergonomic" is not a regulated label; evidence and fit matter more than the word on the box.
</Reveal>

## 4.4 Mobile Device Ergonomics: Thumb Zones

Mobile is now the **primary computing device** for a large share of the world — especially where desktop ownership is low but smartphone use is high, as in Nigeria. Most large phones are **operated one-handed** while the other hand is busy.

![Two phones, left-hand and right-hand, each shaded into a green natural zone near the thumb at the bottom, an amber stretch zone in the middle, and a red hard-to-reach zone at the top, with a bottom navigation bar in the natural zone](/images/ins202/thumb-zones.svg)

*Figure: Thumb reach zones for one-handed use (after Hoober, 2013).*

| Zone | Where | Reach |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural zone** | Bottom of the screen, near the thumb | **Easy** one-handed reach |
| **Stretch zone** | Middle of the screen | Needs **thumb extension** |
| **Hard-to-reach zone** | Top of the screen (and far corners) | Needs a **grip shift or second hand** |

**Placing primary navigation in the natural zone significantly improves one-handed usability.**

- **Real-life example:** if a banking app's **"Confirm Transfer" button sits at the top-right**, a one-handed user must stretch or shift grip — increasing the chance of a **mis-tap that sends money to the wrong account**.
- **Industry example:** **Instagram** and other major apps place their **primary navigation bar** (home, search, post, notifications, profile) **at the bottom**.
- **Applications:** retail POS, food-delivery rider apps, mobile banking, ride-hailing, and field tools used by **agricultural extension workers** recording crop data on tablets.

<Reveal question="Quick quiz: why do apps like Instagram put navigation at the bottom of the screen? A) Aesthetics only B) Thumb-zone reach C) Battery saving D) Screen resolution">
**B — Thumb-zone reach.** The bottom of the screen is in the natural zone for one-handed use.
</Reveal>

## 4.5 Display Technology

Four properties affect **visual comfort**:

| Property | Meaning | Guidance |
|---|---|---|
| **Resolution** | Number of pixels — higher allows finer detail and **sharper text** | Higher resolution reduces the effort of reading small text |
| **Refresh rate** | Redraws per second (**Hz**) — higher reduces perceived **flicker** | Office work typically **60–75 Hz**; motion-sensitive work **120 Hz+** |
| **Brightness** | Measured in **nits** — should **match, not clash with**, ambient light | Too dim in a bright room → squinting and leaning forward; too bright in a dark room → glare and disrupted sleep (blue light) |
| **Colour accuracy** | Fidelity to a reference standard | **Critical** for design and **medical imaging** |

**Best practice:** match brightness to ambient light; position the screen **perpendicular to windows** to avoid glare; use **matte anti-glare** coatings.

### When Display Quality Is a Safety Issue

- **Radiology:** a radiologist reviewing scans 8 hours daily needs a **diagnostic-grade monitor** with high brightness uniformity and **calibrated greyscale accuracy** — a consumer monitor tuned for vibrant colour **could cause a missed fracture or tumour**.
- **Air traffic control:** tower displays use **high-brightness, anti-glare, wide-viewing-angle** screens because controllers must read critical flight data accurately in variable daylight — misreading has **life-safety consequences**.

<Reveal question="Quick quiz: a radiologist missing a subtle fracture because of poor monitor calibration is primarily a failure of which display property? A) Refresh rate B) Colour/greyscale accuracy C) Speaker volume D) Battery life">
**B — Colour/greyscale accuracy.** Medical imaging depends on precise, calibrated greyscale rendering.
</Reveal>

## Class Activity: Workstation Audit

<Reveal question="Score a setup against three ISO 9241-5 principles — sightlines, adjustability and reach zones — find three deviations and propose low-cost fixes. (Model answer for a typical student setup.)">
**Setup:** laptop on a plastic table, fixed plastic chair, phone and charger scattered at the far end of the table.
1. **Sightlines — deviation:** top of screen ~25 cm below eye level. **Fix:** raise the laptop on books; add a cheap USB keyboard.
2. **Adjustability — deviation:** fixed chair too low, so elbows sit below the table. **Fix:** a firm cushion on the seat and a box or stool as a footrest.
3. **Reach zones — deviation:** frequently used phone and notebook placed at the far edge, forcing repeated reaching. **Fix:** keep them within forearm reach beside the keyboard; move rarely used items farther away.
</Reveal>

<Takeaway>
**Physical ergonomics** — "the laws of work" — treats the workstation as **one system**. **ISO 9241-5** asks for **neutral posture access, adjustability, sufficient clearance, appropriate sightlines** (screen top at or below eye level, 50–70 cm away) and **organised reach zones**; the **90-90-90 rule** is a starting point, not a law. **Keyboards** (QWERTY persists through path dependence) should avoid wrist extension and ulnar deviation — split layouts, negative tilt, and wrist rests only during pauses. **Mice** should fit hand size and grip (palm, claw, fingertip); vertical mice and trackballs reduce strain. On **mobile**, put primary actions in the **thumb's natural zone** at the bottom. **Displays** need suitable resolution, refresh rate, brightness and colour accuracy — in radiology and air traffic control, display quality is a **safety** issue.
</Takeaway>

## Exam Tips

**What lecturers test:**
- The **five ISO 9241-5 principles** and the 90-90-90 rule
- Ergonomic keyboard features; why QWERTY persists (path dependence)
- Mouse grip styles and ergonomic mouse types; Table 4.1
- **Thumb zones** and why navigation goes at the bottom
- The four display properties and safety-critical examples

**Common mistakes:**
- Saying QWERTY was designed to slow typists (it reduced typewriter jamming)
- Recommending wrist rests **during** typing
- Treating the 90-90-90 rule as an exact requirement

**Likely question:**
> "Explain the ISO 9241-5 principles of workstation design and discuss how keyboard, mouse and mobile-device design affect comfort and long-term health." (20 marks)
